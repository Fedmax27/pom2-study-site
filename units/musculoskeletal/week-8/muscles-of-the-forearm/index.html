    Clinical Approach to the Muscles of the Distal Upper Extremity

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Anterior forearm = flexor-pronators from the medial epicondyle, all median nerve except 1½: flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of FDP (to digits 4 and 5) are ulnar.
    • Posterior forearm = extensor-supinators from the lateral epicondyle, all radial nerve, no exceptions. Brachioradialis is the odd one out: radial-supplied but an elbow flexor in mid-pronation (“the drinking muscle”).
    • Layers. Anterior: 4 superficial (pronator teres, FCR, palmaris longus, FCU), 1 intermediate (FDS → middle phalanges), 3 deep (FDP → distal phalanges, FPL, pronator quadratus). Posterior: 6 superficial, 5 deep (supinator, extensor indicis and the three thumb “outcropping” muscles). Anconeus sits on its own.
    • Wrist moves. Radial deviation = FCR + ECRL + ECRB; ulnar deviation = FCU + ECU. The anatomical snuffbox (EPL on one side, EPB + APL on the other) has the scaphoid in its floor and the radial artery running through it.
    • Most “tendinitis” is tendinosis: degenerate collagen, no inflammation, slow recovery (100+ days to make new collagen). Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylosis) is mostly ECRB; golfer's elbow (medial) is the common flexor origin. Treat with activity modification, a counterforce strap and eccentric strengthening twice a day for 12 weeks.
    • Distal biceps rupture is rare, in the young and acute, with weak supination and flexion. Refer to ortho fast, before the tendon retracts. Mallet finger (can't extend the DIP) gets a splint in extension; an avulsion fracture needs pinning.

    Learning Objectives

    • Identify the important functional anatomy of the upper extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of arm pathology.
    • Identify the major muscles of the upper extremity, including the forearm muscles from the medial and lateral epicondyles.
    • Create a differential diagnosis and management plan for upper extremity complaints: numbness, weakness, pain and loss of range of motion.
    • Describe the anatomic basis of common elbow and arm complaints: medial and lateral epicondylitis, distal biceps rupture, olecranon bursitis, ulnar neuropathy, arthritis, subluxation/dislocation and UCL injury, and outline their diagnosis and management.

    Part 1

    The Anterior Forearm

    Dr. Martin's first recording.

    Forearm Organization

    The forearm runs from the elbow to the wrist. Its deep fascia, the antebrachial fascia (antebrachium = forearm), continues from the brachial fascia and attaches to the ulna, which you can feel all the way down the medial forearm. The radius, the ulna, the interosseous membrane and one anterior intermuscular septum (radius to fascia) make two compartments. In cross-section they look more medial and lateral than anterior and posterior. They're named for where their tendons end up: the front or the back of the wrist and hand.

    CompartmentCommon originFunctionNerve
    Anterior (3 layers: 4 + 1 + 3)Medial epicondyle (except the deep layer)Flex the wrist and fingers; pronate the forearmMedian, except 1½ muscles (ulnar)
    Posterior (2 layers: 6 + 5)Lateral epicondyle (except the deep layer)Extend the wrist and fingers; supinate the forearmRadial (and its branches), no exceptions
    Why the Muscle Is in the Forearm

    Most forearm muscles work the hand. If all that bulk sat in the hand, the hand would be too fat to be dexterous. So the bellies stay in the forearm and send long tendons across the wrist.

    Those tendons need holding down or they'd bowstring when they contract. The palmar carpal ligament (a thickening of the antebrachial fascia) does it at the front and continues round as the extensor retinaculum at the back. The flexor retinaculum is a different structure, deeper and more distal, and is often confused with the palmar carpal ligament.

    Three Things Worth Understanding

    How a deep tendon reaches further. Each FDS tendon splits in two and wraps round to the sides of the middle phalanx. FDP passes through the split to the distal phalanx. So FDS flexes the MCP and PIP joints, and only FDP can flex the DIP. People who can bend only the fingertip are hyperextending the MCP and PIP joints, which takes FDS out of play, and then firing FDP.

    The names tell you the route. Flexor carpi (to the carpals) radialis or ulnaris (which side). Pollicis = thumb. A “longus” in the limb usually has a “brevis” further down.

    Palmaris longus is missing in about 14% of people, and some have it on one side only. To check, flex the wrist against resistance with the fingers in a fist: the tendon pops up in the middle of the wrist. Its aponeurosis is why palm skin won't pinch up the way the back of the hand does: it keeps the hand's muscles and tendons tightly packed.

    Knowledge checks

    Which anterior forearm muscles are not supplied by the median nerve? → flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus: the 1½, both ulnar.

    Extensors Grip Too

    A strong fist needs the wrist held in extension, so ECRL and ECU fire during fist clenching. That's why rock climbing can leave the back of the forearm sorer than the front, and why gripping hurts in tennis elbow.

    The Thumb, the Snuffbox and Wrist Movements

    The three “outcropping” muscles (APL, EPL, EPB) emerge from under the superficial extensors to reach the thumb. Their tendons frame the anatomical snuffbox: EPL on one side, EPB and APL on the other. Extend your thumb to see it.

    Thumb movementWhat it is
    Flexion / extensionAcross the palm, staying in the plane of the hand / back out again
    Abduction / adductionAway from the plane of the palm (forward) / back into it
    Opposition / repositionThumb to the little finger, which sets us apart from other animals / back to anatomical position
    Why the Snuffbox Matters

    Its floor is the scaphoid (with the trapezium), so snuffbox tenderness is where you palpate for a scaphoid fracture. The radial artery crosses it: press it against the scaphoid and you feel a pulse. It's named for people who put snuff (tobacco) there to sniff.

    FlexionAnterior compartment
    ExtensionPosterior compartment
    Abduction (radial deviation)Both compartments: FCR + ECRL + ECRB
    Adduction (ulnar deviation)Both compartments: FCU + ECU
    Extensor featurePoint
    Synovial sheathsEvery extensor tendon crossing the wrist has one, to cut friction under the tight retinaculum
    6 osseofibrous tunnelsUnder the extensor retinaculum, formed by its attachments to the radius and ulna. You don't need which tendon goes where.
    Intertendinous connectionsLink adjacent extensor digitorum tendons near the knuckles, so the middle and ring fingers can't extend independently. The index and little fingers can: they have their own extensors.
    Extensor expansionA triangular aponeurosis over the back and sides of each finger. Extensor digitorum, EDM, extensor indicis and the lumbricals and interossei all insert through it.

    Synovial (ganglion) cystMallet (baseball) finger
    WhatA cyst of clear, mucinous fluid, most often on the dorsum of the wrist. It is connected to the synovial sheaths of the extensor tendons. Grape-sized, sometimes plum-sized; cause unknown.     Muscle and Tendon Problems at the Elbow

    Dr. Cassidy's recording. The approach and the elbow differential are the same as in 8.5. Muscle and tendon problems show up as pain and loss of strength, where joint problems affect range and stability.

    Tendinitis vs Tendinosis

    TendinitisTendinosis
    How commonLess common than we thinkMore common than we think
    WhatTrue inflammation: rheumatologic disease, acute overload (too much, too suddenly), infection (e.g. infectious tenosynovitis)Collagen degeneration from chronic overuse with too little recovery, not the inflammatory cascade. Under the microscope: immature, disorganized collagen and abnormal vascularity.
    TreatmentReduce inflammation: RICE for acute injury, anti-inflammatories, treat the cause (antibiotics, rheumatology)Little inflammation to reduce: therapy to encourage collagen recovery; analgesics (anti-inflammatories only for their pain relief)
    RecoveryFaster, once the cause is fixedSlow and stuttering: “100+ days” to make new collagen

    Lateral epicondylosis (“tennis elbow”)Medial epicondylosis (“golfer's elbow”)

    Knowledge check

    A left-handed woman's elbow hurts after tightening a lot of screws on a weekend project, and you diagnose golfer's elbow. What's injured? → the common flexor origin at the medial epicondyle (medial epicondylitis/osis).

    Biceps Tendinopathy and Rupture

    Tendinitis / tendinosisRupture
    Clinical pointsInsidious onset; pain on resisted movement and on stretch; usual treatmentsTrauma or “end-overuse”; a pop heard or felt; pain (or, once it has let go, little); bruising; weakness; a change in muscle contour
    Proximal (long or short head)Anterior shoulder pain (8.3)Not uncommon, especially in the elderly; often degenerative. Usually still functional (brachialis, brachioradialis and the other head still flex), so typically non-operative. The belly bunches low: the Popeye bulge.
    DistalAnterior elbow painRare but consequential: both heads let go, so poor supination and weak flexion; usually younger and acute; the belly retracts up the arm. Call ortho early: once the tendon has retracted it's hard to pull back down and reattach.

    Managing Tendinitis and Tendinosis

    1
    Educate

    Name it, and set expectations: many improve quickly, some become chronic. Find and address the offending movements.

    2
    Modify the activity

    Ice/RICE. Relative rest by time or by task: less time at it, more breaks, or change how it's done. Adjust ergonomics and biomechanics to cut excess force.

    3
    Orthoses and taping

    A counterforce strap worn during the offending activity, pressing just distal to the point of maximal tenderness, spreads the force away from the tendon origin.

    4
    Physiotherapy: the mainstay

    Eccentric strengthening twice a day for 12 weeks to stimulate collagen. For tennis elbow: lift the hand into extension with the other hand, then lower the weight (a water bottle) slowly into flexion. Massage may stimulate fibroblasts too.

    5
    Medication

    Anti-inflammatories can be tried but help less in chronic tendinosis. Prefer topical: upper-limb tendons are shallow enough for it to work, with fewer systemic effects.

    6
    Intervention

    Regenerative options such as platelet-rich plasma (evidence unclear, sometimes useful). Surgical referral if refractory; surgery is avoided where possible.

    Practice Pointer

    Don't ask “have you tried physio?” (the answer is always yes). Ask “show me what you've been doing in physio”. If they can't, they're probably only doing the exercises in the gym every two weeks, and that explains why they aren't better.
