    Lower Limb Radiology

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Same read every time: skeletal maturity (growth plates fused?), bone (cortex, medulla, density), alignment, joints, then soft tissues. Sort what you find with TIMS-C.
    • Hip series: AP hip, AP pelvis and cross-table lateral; add a frog-leg in children to see the femoral head epiphyses. On the AP pelvis, trace three lines: the pelvic ring, each obturator ring and Shenton's line. A step in Shenton's line means a femoral neck fracture.
    • Knee series: AP (with or without weight bearing), lateral and skyline (for the patella). On the lateral, an effusion distends the suprapatellar recess. A fat-fluid level (lipohemarthrosis) after trauma means an intra-articular fracture, even when you can't see the fracture line.
    • Blumensaat's line is the roof of the intercondylar notch; the ACL runs parallel to it. A separate fragment at the superolateral patella is usually a bipartite patella, a normal variant, not a fracture.
    • Ankle series: AP, mortise and lateral. The mortise is the medial malleolus, tibial plafond and lateral malleolus around the talar dome. On the lateral, find the Achilles tendon and Kager's fat pad in front of it.
    • Osteoarthritis at any joint: joint-space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, marginal osteophytes. An enthesophyte is new bone where a tendon or ligament attaches; an osteophyte sits at a joint margin.

    Learning Objectives

    • Identify the important functional anatomy of the lower extremity as it pertains to clinical presentations of leg pathology.
    • Identify the bony landmarks of the lower extremity and relate them to their appearances on plain radiographs.

    Dr. V. Kalia's interactive module, scheduled for 20 minutes (the title says about 45): general concepts, then the hip, knee and ankle. There are no recordings. It's the lower-limb twin of 9.1 Upper Limb Radiology, which has the full general lesson; the spine version of the same read is 7.5.

    The Systematic Read

    The general lesson is the same one as in 9.1: parts of a long bone (epiphysis, physis, metaphysis, diaphysis; cortex and medullary cavity), how the image is made and the order of the read. In a child the physis is a lucent line; once it fuses it disappears.

    1
    Skeletal maturity

    Fused growth plate = mature; visible, unfused growth plate = immature.

    2
    Bone integrity

    Cortex and medullary cavity: any break or lesion? Density changes or generalized bone disease?

    3
    Alignment

    Malalignment means a fracture, dislocation or other injury.

    4
    Joints

    Inflammation, joint-space narrowing, degenerative change.

    5
    Soft tissues

    The problem can sit outside the bone: effusions, fat pads, masses.

    TIMS-C: categories of bone pathologyExamples
    Trauma and TumoursFractures, dislocations; bone tumours
    Infection, Inflammatory, IdiopathicRheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis; idiopathic = cause unknown
    Metabolic / Malignancy
    Systemic / SenileSystemic: leukemia, lymphoma. Senile: osteoarthritis
    CongenitalLooked for mostly in the immature skeleton

    Hip Radiology

    ViewWhat it's for
    AP hipA focused image of one hip. The beam goes anterior to posterior, centred on the hip joint.
    AP pelvisBoth hips and the whole pelvis, centred on the pubic symphysis. Routinely part of a hip series, so you can compare sides.
    Cross-table lateralThe key lateral projection: opposite leg raised, beam horizontal across the hip.
    Frog-leg (when indicated)Mostly in children: both femurs abducted and externally rotated, to check that the femoral head epiphyses are symmetrically ossified and in position.
    Normal anatomyWhat to find
    Proximal femur (AP)Femoral head (with the fovea, where the ligamentum teres attaches, often not visible), neck, greater trochanter, lesser trochanter (a curved sclerotic bump) and the intertrochanteric region between them. These landmarks are how you name the fracture for the orthopaedic team.
    Acetabulum (AP)The acetabular roof (dense sclerotic line), the quadrilateral plate medial to it, the iliopectineal and ilioischial lines, the teardrop and Shenton's line. These describe acetabular fractures. Also the pubic rami, body of the pubis, symphysis and ischial tuberosity.
    Cross-table lateralAcetabulum (anterior column, quadrilateral plate, posterior column), femoral head, neck, both trochanters, ischial tuberosity. The intertrochanteric region is hard to see here (overlap).
    AP pelvisIliac wings and crests, ASIS and AIIS, pubic rami and symphysis, ischium; both proximal femurs (the lesser trochanter is often clearer than on the AP hip); the symphysis, both sacroiliac joints and both hips.
    AP pelvis lineNormalA break suggests
    Pelvic ringThe inner margins of the pelvic bones make one continuous ringA pelvic fracture
    Obturator ringsEach obturator foramen has a complete, unbroken outlineAn injury to the pubic rami or ischium
    Shenton's lineA smooth curve from the inferior femoral neck along the inferior superior pubic ramusA subtle hip fracture (or other injury) you might otherwise miss
    Hip pathologyWhat you see
    Femoral neck fractureOn the normal side, Shenton's line runs smoothly with no step-off. On the fractured side it's clearly disrupted with a step-off at the femoral neck. For who gets them and how they're fixed, see 9.3.
    Total hip arthroplastyAn acetabular cup plus a ball-shaped femoral head, neck and a stem down the proximal femur, recreating the ball and socket.
    OsteoarthritisJoint-space narrowing, marginal osteophytes (bony spurs at the joint edges) and subchondral sclerosis (denser bone on both sides of the joint). Look for all three; one may stand out more than the others.

    Knee Radiology

    A routine series is three views: AP (supine, or standing / weight-bearing, which often takes both knees in one exposure for comparison), lateral and skyline (for the patella).

    ViewWhat to find
    APMedial and lateral femoral condyles and epicondyles, the intercondylar fossa, the adductor tubercle (medial) and the popliteal groove (lateral, where popliteus arises). The patella overlaps the femur (base and apex). The medial and lateral tibial plateaus (radiologists' term, rather than tibial condyles), the tibial spines with the intercondylar eminence between them, and the fibular head, neck and shaft. The joint spaces are the cartilage, which is invisible because it isn't calcified. Alignment check: a line down the lateral distal femur should pass within 5 mm of the lateral proximal tibia.
    LateralFemur, tibia, fibula, patella; the femoral cortices. The condyles overlap. Blumensaat's line is the roof of the intercondylar notch, and the ACL runs parallel to it. The plateaus overlap too: the medial is flatter, the lateral slightly convex. The tibial tuberosity takes the patellar tendon.
    SkylineOrient first: the soft tissue is thicker on the medial side. Then the medial and lateral patellar facets with the median ridge between them, sitting in the trochlear groove between the medial and lateral trochlea.
    Read the Soft Tissues on the Lateral

    The quadriceps tendon attaches to the top of the patella and the patellar tendon runs from its lower pole to the tibial tuberosity. Above the patella, two radiolucent fat pads (the anterior suprapatellar, triangular, and the posterior suprapatellar / prefemoral) sandwich the suprapatellar recess, which is normally collapsed. An effusion distends the recess into a bulbous shape and pushes the fat pads apart. Bleeding into the joint may also show a fat-fluid level. Hoffa's fat pad is the radiolucent pad in front, below the patella.

    Knee pathologyWhat you see
    Proximal tibia fracture with lipohemarthrosisA thin lucent line through the proximal tibia (oblique views help when it's hard to see). If the fracture reaches the joint, fatty marrow escapes into the blood in the joint and floats on top: a fat-fluid level. After trauma, that finding points to a subtle or occult intra-articular fracture.
    OsteoarthritisThe same three features. Here: tibial spine "spiking", osteophytes on both sides of the tibia and the medial femoral condyle, a much narrower medial compartment (thin medial cartilage) and mild subchondral sclerosis.
    Bipartite patella (variant)The patella develops in two pieces; the accessory fragment is usually superolateral and well defined. Even after trauma, that's more likely a bipartite patella than a fracture. Real patellar fractures more often hit the superior or inferior pole or the mid-body and have a sharper line. A multipartite patella (3+ pieces) also sits superolaterally or laterally.
    Total knee arthroplastyComponents on both sides of the joint; check their alignment (loosening is beyond this module). A little air in the joint and skin staples mean it's recent. A prosthetic patellar component = TKA with patellar resurfacing.
    ACL reconstructionA graft (part of the patellar or hamstring tendon) runs through bone tunnels in the tibia and femur, fixed with sutures or interference screws (often not radiopaque, so you see the tunnels, not the screws). Tunnels shouldn't be widened, and the tibial tunnel shouldn't sit in the anterior third of the tibia.

    Ankle Radiology

    The standard series is the AP (with or without weight bearing), the mortise (ankle rotated slightly inward, which opens up the joint space and the distal tibiofibular joint) and the lateral. The AP and mortise show similar anatomy, so the module teaches the mortise.

    ViewWhat to find
    MortiseThe mortise is three structures: the medial malleolus, the tibial plafond (the flat articular surface of the distal tibia) and the lateral malleolus. The talar dome sits in it. Also the malleolar fossa (a curved sclerotic line by the lateral malleolus) and the fibular notch of the tibia, which forms the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis (its ligaments show on MRI, not x-ray).
    LateralDistal tibia and fibula with both malleoli; the talus (head, neck, body) and calcaneus (tuberosity, anterior process), which meet at the subtalar joint; talus to navicular, calcaneus to cuboid. Soft tissues: the Achilles tendon behind, inserting on the calcaneal tuberosity, and Kager's fat pad, a radiolucent triangle in front of it. A soft-tissue bulge by the joint may be an effusion.
    Immature ankleAt 11 years the physes of the distal tibia and fibula are dark lucent lines, and the calcaneal apophysis is visible at the back of the heel. By 17 they've fused; faint sclerotic lines can linger into adulthood.
    Ankle pathologyWhat you see
    Distal tibia and fibula fracturesDisplaced, oblique fractures of both bones that are extra-articular (they don't reach the joint surface)
    Medial and lateral malleolus fractures (bimalleolar)A transverse medial malleolus fracture and an oblique lateral malleolus fracture, with a lot of soft-tissue swelling. The mortise looks preserved on this non-weight-bearing film; weight-bearing views may be asked for to stress it. Fracture patterns and management are in 10.3.
    Calcaneal spur and Achilles enthesophyteA common reason for heel-pain films. A calcaneal (plantar) spur projects from the plantar calcaneus. New bone at the Achilles insertion is an enthesophyte (chronic stress where a tendon attaches), unlike an osteophyte at a joint margin. A thickened Achilles with a little calcification and encroachment on Kager's fat pad fits Achilles tendinopathy.
    OsteoarthritisAsymmetric joint-space narrowing, lateral talar tilt, subchondral sclerosis and osteophytes
