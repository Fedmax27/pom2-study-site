    Case 2 · Neck and Shoulder Pain (Parts I–III)

    Likely answers: Part I = chronic mechanical neck pain, facet-mediated (worse on extension, tender facets, normal neuro). Part II = right C6 radiculopathy (absent biceps and brachioradialis reflexes, weak elbow flexion and wrist extension, Spurling's positive). Part III = rotator cuff tendinopathy with impingement (painful arc, active < passive); global active and passive loss, worst in external rotation, would be adhesive capsulitis.

    Case 4 · Thigh Pain

    Likely answer: greater trochanteric pain syndrome (gluteus medius and minimus tendinopathy: tender trochanter, pain on resisted abduction, can't lie on that side, Trendelenburg). The lost internal rotation means coexisting hip OA, and the painful lumbar extension means the lumbar spine is also in play.

    Case 6 · Foot Drop

    Likely answer: after a hip replacement, the sciatic nerve (its fibular division) is the classic injury; the common fibular nerve at the fibular head (positioning, stockings) and an L5 root are the other two to sort out. The new burning, shooting pain in the numb area is neuropathic pain.

    Case 8 · Flaccid Limb (Optional)

    Likely answer: a traction brachial plexus injury of C5–C6 (upper trunk, Erb pattern) with partial C7. Rhomboids at 0/5 is the key: the dorsal scapular nerve leaves the C5 root, so the lesion is at root level, possibly a root avulsion, which won't recover on its own.

    Not on the Site: Quick Facts

    TopicThe short version
    Sensory terms (case 5)Allodynia: pain from something that shouldn't hurt (light touch, a sling strap). Hyperalgesia: too much pain from something that should hurt a little. Paraesthesia: abnormal sensation, like tingling or pins and needles, that isn't unpleasant (dysaesthesia is the unpleasant kind). Hypoaesthesia: reduced sensation.
    Time off work (case 3)Bed rest slows recovery. Stay active and return to modified duties early (days, not weeks); full heavy work as the pain allows.
    Chiropractor (case 3)Spinal manipulation gives a small short-term benefit, about the same as other conservative care, for acute mechanical back pain without red flags. Serious harm in the lumbar spine is rare. Avoid it with a neurologic deficit, osteoporosis, cancer or infection. It works best alongside staying active and exercise.
    Radial nerve care (case 5)Wrist cock-up or dynamic extension splint, range of motion so joints don't stiffen, physio and OT. EMG at about 3–4 weeks (after Wallerian degeneration) to grade it, then again at 3–4 months for reinnervation (brachioradialis and extensor carpi radialis longus recover first). No recovery by about 3–6 months: surgical exploration, nerve grafting, or tendon transfers later.
    Foot drop after hip replacement (case 6)The sciatic nerve's fibular division is the most often injured nerve in a hip replacement (retraction, stretch from lengthening the leg, haematoma). The fibular head (positioning, tight stockings) and L5 are the other two. Help: an ankle-foot orthosis, calf stretching to prevent an equinus contracture, falls prevention, neuropathic pain medication (gabapentinoid or tricyclic), EMG at 3–4 weeks, and urgent imaging if a haematoma is suspected.
    Root avulsion clues (case 8)Weak rhomboids (dorsal scapular nerve) or serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve), a raised hemidiaphragm (phrenic), or Horner's (T1) point to a root-level injury. Another clue on nerve conduction is that sensory responses are preserved in a numb area, because the sensory cell bodies sit outside the cord. MRI or CT myelogram shows pseudomeningoceles. Avulsions don't recover, so refer early: nerve transfers work best within about 3–6 months. Meanwhile treat the neuropathic pain, keep the shoulder moving, and swap the figure-8 sling for something tolerable.
    Other differentials (case 2 Part II)Thoracic outlet syndrome (symptoms with the arm overhead; ulnar side of the hand), brachial plexopathy (e.g. neuralgic amyotrophy: severe shoulder pain, then weakness), and a Pancoast tumour are worth adding to the differential.
    Ulnar nerve self-exam (case 1)Abduct and adduct the fingers (interossei), cross the fingers, Froment's sign (gripping paper flexes the thumb's interphalangeal joint because adductor pollicis is weak), and check sensation on the back of the little finger. The dorsal cutaneous branch leaves above the wrist, so dorsal loss puts the lesion at the elbow, not Guyon's canal.
