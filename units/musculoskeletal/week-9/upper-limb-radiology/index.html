    Upper Limb Radiology

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Read every film in order: is the skeleton mature (growth plates fused)? Is the bone intact (cortex, medulla, density)? Is the alignment normal? Are the joints normal? Then the soft tissues, because normal bones don't rule out disease.
    • Shoulder. Cartilage and the labrum are invisible on x-ray. The Y-view is the one for dislocation: the humeral head should sit centred on the glenoid at the middle of the "Y" (coracoid in front, scapular spine behind, body below). Anterior dislocations sit under the coracoid. On the axillary view, the coracoid and lesser tuberosity point anteriorly.
    • Osteoarthritis anywhere: joint-space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, marginal osteophytes.
    • Elbow fat pads. A small anterior fat pad is normal. Any visible posterior fat pad is abnormal (effusion, usually from a fracture): in a child look for a supracondylar fracture; in an adult, a radial head or neck fracture.
    • Elbow alignment. The anterior humeral line (lateral view) should pass through the middle of the capitellum; anterior to that suggests a supracondylar fracture. The radiocapitellar line (radial shaft axis) should hit the centre of the capitellum on every view; if not, think radial head dislocation.
    • Wrist. Carpal bones radial to ulnar: proximal row scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform; distal row trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate ("She Looks Too Pretty, Try To Catch Her"). Then trace the three Gilula arcs: any break, step or widened gap means a carpal injury.

    Learning Objectives

    • Identify the distinguishing features of the bones and joints of the upper extremity and relate them to their appearance on plain radiographs.

    Dr. V. Kalia's interactive module, about 20 minutes: general concepts, then the shoulder, elbow and wrist. The general lesson is repeated in all three P2 radiology modules. For the spine version of the same systematic read (ABCS), see 7.5.

    Basic Concepts for Image Interpretation

    Everything is described with the body in anatomic position (medial = toward the midline, lateral = away; proximal = closer to the body, distal = farther).

    Part of a long boneWhat it isOn x-ray
    EpiphysisThe end of the bone; contains a growth centre
    Physis (growth plate)Where new bone is made during growthA lucent line between epiphysis and metaphysis in children; invisible once fused in the mature skeleton
    MetaphysisThe transition zone between epiphysis and diaphysis
    DiaphysisThe shaft, most of the length
    Cortex / medullary cavityThe dense outer layer / the inner cavityCheck both for abnormalities, density changes or generalized bone disease

    How an x-ray is made: the patient is positioned between the x-ray tube and the image receptor (detector); x-rays are attenuated by the tissues (bone most) on the way through; the computer stores the image in PACS; a radiologist reads it and sends a report to the ordering physician.

    1
    Skeletal maturity

    Fused growth plate = mature; visible, unfused growth plate = immature.

    2
    Bone integrity

    Cortex and medullary cavity: any break or lesion? Density changes or generalized bone disease that weakens bone?

    3
    Alignment

    Essential for joint function; misalignment means a fracture, dislocation or other injury.

    4
    Joints

    Inflammation, joint-space narrowing, degenerative change.

    5
    Soft tissues

    Muscle and soft tissue can be abnormal when the bones look normal.

    Shoulder Radiology

    Standard views are for general indications such as trauma or osteoarthritis; special views (instability, confirming a dislocation) aren't covered. The module shows the AP, axillary and Y views.

    ViewWhat to find
    APThe glenoid, humeral head and tuberosities, clavicle, acromion and coracoid. The glenoid's labrum is fibrocartilage, so it isn't visible. Two reference lines (not visible structures) used to describe fractures: the anatomical neck (where head meets neck) and the surgical neck (a transverse line just below the articular surface).
    Axillary (arm out from the body, beam through the axilla)Harder to read. Find the distal clavicle, acromion (together the AC joint), glenoid and humerus; some overlap is normal but the joint space shouldn't look widened. The coracoid and the lesser tuberosity both point anteriorly, which orients the film. Watch for an os acromiale (an unfused ossification centre: a lucent line or separate bit of acromion), not a fracture. The anatomical and surgical necks aren't distinguishable here.
    Y-viewThe "Y": coracoid = anterior limb, scapular spine = posterior limb, scapular body = vertical limb, with the glenoid at the centre. The humeral head should be centred over (overlapping) the glenoid. Head in front = anterior subluxation or dislocation; behind = posterior.
    Shoulder pathologyWhat you see
    Osteoarthritis (age-related degenerative joint disease)(1) Joint-space narrowing from cartilage loss (cartilage is radiolucent, so the space is the cartilage), (2) subchondral sclerosis (denser bone under the surface from stress, not necessarily stronger), (3) marginal osteophytes (new bone at the joint edges as the body tries to stabilize it). Graded mild, moderate or severe on the combination.
    Surgical neck fractureA fracture line along the surgical neck with mild displacement
    DislocationAnterior: the head lies in front of the glenoid, usually subcoracoid (under the coracoid). Posterior: it lies behind, farther from the coracoid. The Y-view tells them apart. A subluxation keeps partial contact with the glenoid; a dislocation is complete loss of contact.

    Elbow Radiology

    The standard views are the AP (elbow extended on the plate) and the lateral. Radiographs are two-dimensional, so you need the three-dimensional anatomy in your head.

    ViewWhat to find
    APFind the medial side first: the medial epicondyle is larger than the lateral, and the ulna is medial. Medial to lateral across the distal humerus: medial epicondyle, trochlea, capitellum, lateral epicondyle; the coronoid and olecranon fossae. On the ulna, the sublime tubercle: the UCL attaches there, so check it after trauma. The AP is the best view for telling capitellum from trochlea.
    Lateral(1) The humeral shaft; the capitellum and trochlea overlap (the capitellum is the one next to the radius, slightly magnified). (2) The proximal ulna: olecranon, trochlear notch, coronoid process, all fracture-prone. (3) The radius: head, neck, radial tuberosity (where biceps attaches), shaft. (4) The fat pads, then the olecranon bursa and soft tissues.
    The Fat Pad Rule

    The anterior fat pad is normally seen as a small radiolucent triangle in front of the distal humerus. The posterior fat pad is normally hidden in the olecranon fossa; when a joint effusion lifts it into view, the posterior fat pad sign is always abnormal, usually trauma. Then look hard for a fracture matched to skeletal maturity: a supracondylar fracture in a child (visible epiphysis, physis and metaphysis), a radial head or neck fracture in an adult.

    Alignment lineNormalAbnormal means
    Anterior humeral line (lateral view, along the anterior humeral cortex)Passes through the centre of the capitellum (slight variation is fine)Through the anterior capitellum, with an elevated posterior fat pad, in an immature skeleton: supracondylar fracture (in an adult, think radial head or neck)
    Radiocapitellar line (along the radial shaft's axis)Passes through the centre of the capitellum on every view (AP, oblique, lateral)Misses the capitellum: radial head dislocation or another alignment problem (also seen with displaced supracondylar fractures)
    Elbow pathologyWhat you see
    OsteoarthritisThe same three features (narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, marginal osteophytes), here with anterior and posterior intra-articular osseous bodies, common in OA
    LipomaNormal bones, but a large swelling: a well-defined, low-density oval soft-tissue mass pushing the soft tissues out. Suspected lipoma, confirmed on MRI. The lesson: a normal-looking bone doesn't exclude pathology.

    Wrist Radiology

    The standard views are the AP (hand flat, palm down) and a true lateral (hand on its side).

    ViewWhat to find
    APThe distal radius and ulna meeting at the DRUJ; the beak-like ulnar styloid and the broader radial styloid; the radiocarpal joint; the two carpal rows; metacarpals 1–5. The circled TFCC region: fibrocartilage, so invisible, but calcification there may mean TFCC pathology.
    LateralTrace the dorsal radius to Lister's tubercle (a dorsal bump separating extensor tendon compartments; check it for fractures). The distal radial joint surface, the overlapping ulna and its styloid. Proximal row: scaphoid (anterior), lunate, triquetrum (posterior), pisiform (anterior); distal row: trapezium, capitate, hamate (the trapezoid hides behind the trapezium). So many bones overlap that the AP is essential for spotting fractures.
    Gilula arcFollows
    1stThe proximal surfaces of the proximal carpal row
    2ndThe distal surfaces of the proximal row
    3rdThe proximal surfaces of the distal row

    All three should be smooth and continuous. A break, step-off, widened joint space or loss of congruity suggests a carpal fracture, subluxation or instability.

    Wrist pathologyWhat you see
    Scaphoid and triquetrum fracturesA thin lucent line through the proximal pole of the scaphoid and another through the triquetrum. Inspect every cortex: any disruption or lucent line is a fracture in the right setting. The Gilula arcs are preserved, so carpal alignment is intact.
    Distal radius and ulnar styloid fracturesA transverse distal radius fracture (displacement and impaction are for the fracture module) and a fracture at the base of the ulnar styloid; carpal alignment maintained.
    Giant cell tumour (not shown)An expanded distal radius with a large lytic lesion, a markedly thinned cortex, reaching the subarticular surface
