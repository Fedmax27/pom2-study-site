    Pathology of AUB & Pelvic Pain

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Exclude structural causes before you call anything functional. Dr. Weir says this at the end of all three parts. Calling it "dysfunctional bleeding" without excluding a structural lesion is how you miss something significant.
    • Structural vs functional is the first cut. Structural = something you can see when you open the uterus or under the microscope. Functional = a chemical or hormone acting on a structurally normal uterus. DUB is a diagnosis of exclusion.
    • PALE is her mnemonic for the benign structural causes — Polyp, Adenomyosis, Leiomyoma, Endometritis. Her own aide-mémoire: it's what you look like if you've been bleeding a lot. Then add malignancy and baby.
    • A mass at the cervical os collapses the differential. Only polyp, leiomyoma, malignancy and baby present that way. Adenomyosis, endometriosis and endometritis do not present as mass lesions.
    • Learn the four lesions by where the endometrium is: polyp = endometrium overgrown above the surface · adenomyosis = endometrium deep in the myometrium · endometriosis = endometrium outside the uterus · leiomyoma = not endometrium at all, it's smooth muscle.
    • Leiomyoma is the commonest neoplasm in women, and it is NOT pre-malignant — it does not progress to leiomyosarcoma. But a single dominant, rapidly growing mass in a peri- or postmenopausal woman may be a sarcoma: do not put her on hormones and wait — refer urgently.
    • Adenomyosis is diagnosed at hysterectomy, not on biopsy — the glands are too deep for the pipelle to reach.
    • Endometriosis needs any 2 of 3 on histology: endometrial glands, endometrial stroma, hemosiderin. Late "burned-out" disease has no endometrial tissue left, so it can't be diagnosed that way.
    • The infertility question (her own quiz): endometriosis, endometrial polyp and leiomyoma — NOT adenomyosis.
    • Always send the tissue to pathology — and the entire polyp, because rarely it harbours malignancy.

    Learning Objectives

    • Build a differential diagnosis for structural causes of AUB & pelvic pain based on knowledge of anatomy and pathology.
    • Narrow the differential diagnosis based on age, common reasons & clues in history & physical examination.
    • Compose an oral narrative which explains to patients the reasons for AUB & pelvic pain.

    The Framework She Reuses Three Times

    Every case runs through the same structure, and she loops back to the same two differential slides each time. Learn the framework once and the three cases become variations.

    How One Finding Collapses a Long List

    Her reasoning, step by step: a 40-year-old has a long differential for AUB. But a mass at the os means there is a structural lesion, and coagulopathy, DUB, endocrine causes, exogenous hormones and infection do not present as a mass at the os. That leaves anatomic, baby and neoplasia — and within the benign anatomic group, adenomyosis and endometritis don't form mass lesions either. Final differential: polyp, leiomyoma, malignancy, baby. Four items from twelve, on the strength of one speculum exam.

    Part 1

    Endometrial Polyp and Leiomyoma

    The two lesions that present as a mass. Both are estrogen-responsive, both most often cause abnormal bleeding, and both can cause infertility.

    Endometrial Polyp

    Definition — general: a growth which projects above a mucosal surface (you get them anywhere there is mucosa — she references GI polyps). Specific: a benign overgrowth of endometrium, driven by hormone-dependent growth — associated with estrogen and with tamoxifen.

    Her Homework Question, Answered

    She sets this explicitly: why does tamoxifen, an anti-estrogen drug used for breast cancer, stimulate endometrial growth?

    Because tamoxifen is a SERMSelective estrogen receptor modulator — a drug that acts as an estrogen receptor antagonist in some tissues and an agonist in others., not a pure anti-estrogen. It is an ER antagonist in breast tissue but a partial estrogen agonist in the endometrium. The tissue-specific switch comes down to which co-regulators get recruited to the receptor: in breast cancer cells tamoxifen recruits co-repressors (NCoR, SMRT) to ER target promoters, whereas in the endometrium it recruits co-activators, so the same drug-receptor complex turns transcription off in one tissue and on in the other. There is also a dose-of-estrogen effect: premenopausally, when estrogen is high, tamoxifen behaves as an antagonist even in the endometrium; postmenopausally, when estrogen is low, it behaves as an agonist. Polyps are the commonest endometrial change in tamoxifen users, reported at 13–30% prevalence.

    Verified outside the deck — the deck only names the association. Source: the review of estrogen and progesterone receptor expression in endometrial polyps of tamoxifen-exposed postmenopausal women in PMC (PMC3915668) and the Oncology Letters review of molecular mechanisms of tamoxifen-associated endometrial changes.

    Objective 3 — What to Tell Your Patient

    "Not a tumour. A benign growth of the tissue lining the uterus. The tissue will be examined by Pathology to confirm it's benign and exclude malignancy, and we'll wait for that report." Note the structure she uses for all four lesions: reassure → explain in plain language → state why you're still sending it.

    Leiomyoma

    Her framing: "fibroid" is the silly term; the pathology term is leiomyoma — leio- smooth, myoma muscle. A benign smooth muscle neoplasm.

    The Point She Makes Three Ways

    Leiomyoma is NOT pre-malignant and NOT a precursor to malignancy. It does not turn into leiomyosarcoma. Her slide says it three times because students reliably get it wrong.

    Mass effect, mechanically: the fibroid and therefore the uterus gets large enough to push forward onto the bladder (urinary frequency) or backward onto the bowel (constipation) — so the patient may present with GU or GI complaints rather than gynecologic ones.

    The One Thing Not to Get Wrong

    Leiomyoma does not become sarcoma — but leiomyosarcoma exists and presents differently. The pattern she wants you to recognize: a peri- or postmenopausal woman with a single dominant uterine mass that is growing rapidly. Fibroids are usually multiple and are supposed to shrink after menopause, so this is the wrong picture in the wrong age group. Her instruction, verbatim in substance: do not put that patient on hormones and wait — refer urgently for further treatment.

    Microscopic: a well-circumscribed nodule, distinct from the background myometrium, made of bundles of smooth muscle cells — uniform in size and shape, with scarce mitoses. Her handout lists three benign variants that can look alarming but are not malignant: bizarre (atypical), cellular, and mitotically active. Other gross changes: hemorrhage, necrosis, cystic change. Diagnosis: by endometrial sampling if submucosal; otherwise in resected specimens (myomectomy or hysterectomy).

    Objective 3 — What to Tell Your Patient

    "A benign tumour of the uterine muscle. If it needs to be removed, we await pathology for the definitive diagnosis."

    Part 2

    Adenomyosis

    Same patient, two changes: no lesion at the cervix, and severe dysmenorrhea. That reopens the differential and adds a second one.

    The Dysmenorrhea Differential

    With no exam findings in a 40-year-old, the AUB list is long again. Combining the two differentials, the things causing bleeding and pain are PALE, DUB, and baby. In the case, β-hCG is negative, ultrasound shows diffuse but non-specific changes, and an endometrial biopsy excludes hyperplasia and cancer. She fails the pill, keeps bleeding and hurting, and opts for hysterectomy — which is exactly how adenomyosis is usually diagnosed.

    Adenomyosis

    Definition: endometrial tissue deep in the myometrium. Non-neoplastic. Present in 15–30% of resected uteri (she says "about a third" out loud). Cause unknown, but ?parity-related — uncommon in nulligravids.

    Why It Hurts and Why the Uterus Enlarges

    The key insight is that ectopic endometrium keeps cycling even though it is buried in muscle. Cyclical bleeding in an enclosed space releases prostaglandins and inflammatory mediators, which are highly irritating to muscle. The myometrium responds by contracting — which is the pain — and, since it is doing that work every cycle for years, it undergoes smooth muscle hypertrophy, which is why the uterus becomes diffusely enlarged. Patients may describe gushing or very heavy bleeding when cystic areas rupture.

    Microscopic: endometrial tissue (stroma and/or glands) deep in the myometrium, usually >¼ of the wall thickness, with smooth muscle hypertrophy. Clinical picture: pre- or perimenopausal, abnormal bleeding, pain (menstrual and pelvic), enlarged uterus.

    Objective 3 — What to Tell Your Patient

    "Not a tumour. Not pre-malignant. The lining of the uterus is deeper in the wall than usual."

    Part 3

    Endometriosis

    A different patient with a history so characteristic it puts one lesion at the top of the differential before you examine her.

    Endometriosis

    The History That Makes the Diagnosis

    Pelvic pain prior to the onset of the period — she calls this "very unusual in patients without this entity." That, plus new worsening dysmenorrhea and one year of infertility, is the classic triad. Then the exam confirms it: a very painful pelvic exam, palpable nodules in the rectovaginal septum, and a fixed uterus — normally the uterus is somewhat mobile on its ligaments, and here it is tethered in the pelvis by adhesions.

    Why It Happens — Three Theories

    1
    Implantation. Retrograde menses happens in every woman — shed endometrium travels out along the tubes into the peritoneum. In most people the host clears it. In some it implants but sits quiescent without cycling. In endometriosis the host permits the implant to establish a blood supply and to cycle. The surgical variant of the same theory: entering the endometrial cavity at caesarean section can implant endometrium in the C-section scar.
    2
    Metaplasia. The peritoneum "switches personality" to endometrial tissue — plausible because the Müllerian and peritoneal tissues share an embryological origin.
    3
    Vascular or lymphatic spread. Her point: this is the only way to explain endometriosis above the diaphragm, in lung or lymph nodes.

    Two Mechanisms of Pain, and Why the Colour Changes

    Early pain is inflammatory. Cyclical endometrial tissue bleeding where it does not belong is intensely irritating; macrophages clear the red cells and store the released iron as hemosiderin, the brown pigment that makes a "powder burn" brown. Late pain is mechanical. Chronic inflammation lays down fibrosis and adhesions, which tether the uterus in the pelvis (Adele's fixed uterus), anchor and stretch the peritoneum, and bind the tubes and ovaries — which is also the anatomical basis of the infertility. Extensive disease produces dense adhesions of tubes and ovaries (hence infertility) and of the pouch of Douglas (hence pain). On the ovary, cystic disease forms endometriomas — "chocolate cysts" with a fibrous wall and degenerated blood contents.

    Objective 3 — What to Tell Your Patient

    "The tissue that lines the uterus is occurring outside the uterus. It is not a tumour. And it goes through the menstrual cycle just like the uterine lining does — it's just in a place where that shouldn't be happening." That last clause is the one that makes the symptoms make sense to a patient.

    • Exclude structural reasons for AUB and pain before assigning anything functional.
    • Use age, common reasons and clues to narrow the differential.
    • Recognize the role of the pathologist in the diagnosis — and always send the tissue.
