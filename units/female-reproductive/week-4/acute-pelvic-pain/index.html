    Clinical Approach to Acute Pelvic Pain

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • β-hCG in every woman of reproductive age. Dr. Black's single most emphasized test. And when a patient insists she isn't sexually active, still ask to do it — "just to be reassured."
    • The differential crosses every organ system — GI, urologic, MSK, gynecologic, vascular, neurologic, plus referred pain from pneumonia or a herniated disc. A gynecologist gets consulted to rule out gynecologic causes, not because it is always gynecologic.
    • Your job is to identify the life- and limb-threatening causes. That framing drives everything below.
    • History and physical exam are the most important part of any approach to pelvic pain — her opening slide, and it is what narrows the investigations rather than the reverse.
    • Onset is the highest-yield history question. Woken from sleep by sudden pain vs pain that began during vigorous physical activity point to very different diagnoses.
    • Four cases, four must-know diagnoses: ruptured ectopic (shoulder-tip pain, tenesmus, β-hCG positive, dropping Hb) · PID (unwell, foul discharge, chandelier sign, β-hCG negative) · ovarian torsion (sudden pain during activity, vomiting, adnexal mass — a surgical emergency) · ruptured hemorrhagic ovarian cyst (2–3 weeks post-LMP, sudden pain, postural hypotension).
    • Ultrasound is the imaging modality of choice for the pelvis — cheap, readily available, and no radiation. A bedside FAST answers the one question that matters acutely: is there free fluid / hemoperitoneum?
    • Ovarian torsion is time-critical: IP-ligament compression drops venous then arterial flow → edema → more compression → ischemia and necrosis. Enlarged ovaries tort more readily — "a weighted pendulum" — so always look for the underlying mass.
    • Don't skip the social history. She says it twice: smoking, alcohol, drug use and sexual practices materially change the differential.

    Learning Objectives

    • Outline a clinical approach to both acute and chronic pelvic pain.
    • Recognize common causes of acute and chronic pelvic pain.
    • List a differential diagnosis for both acute and chronic pelvic pain.
    • Outline appropriate investigations for pelvic pain.
    • Identify treatment options for common causes of pelvic pain.

    Physical Exam

    • Vital signs first — her reasoning: clinical instability sends you down the life-threatening path, so this is a triage step, not a formality.
    • Overall appearance from the end of the bed — does she look genuinely sick and unwell, or fairly healthy despite the pain? A cheap and surprisingly discriminating observation.
    • Abdomen: inspection, auscultation, palpation.
    • Pelvic: inspection of the external genitalia, speculum inspection, and bimanual palpation — two fingers in the vagina, one hand on the abdomen, palpating the uterosacral ligament insertions, the uterus, and the adnexal regions.
    Why Each Investigation, and the One Rule

    CBC does two jobs: the white count if you suspect infection, and the hemoglobin if you are worried about intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Quantitative β-hCG is the critical test in any woman of reproductive age — and she addresses the awkward case head on: when a patient insists she is not, or has not recently been, sexually active, still advise doing it anyway, framed as reassurance. Urinalysis and culture exclude GU causes such as cystitis. Vaginal and cervical cultures where infection is possible. Group and screen when transfusion is conceivable.

    On imaging: ultrasound is the modality of choice for the pelvis — cheap and widely available. In London it is available until at least 11 pm and can be obtained overnight by calling a tech back in for life-threatening gynecologic causes; in some peripheral centres it is not available overnight, so CT is relied on for an initial impression. And a bedside FAST scan can be used to look for free fluid or hemoperitoneum, particularly when transfusion may be needed.

    Part 2

    Case 1 — Ectopic Pregnancy

    Answers below each case. Cover them and work it through first.

    Case 1: Katie, 25

    Reading This Case

    Five findings, each of which independently raises the diagnosis. β-hCG 2345 — she is pregnant, and condoms are a method with real typical-use failure. Sudden RLQ pain + vaginal bleeding — the classic pair. Hb 82 — she is bleeding internally; you cannot see it. Shoulder pain — blood in the peritoneum irritating the diaphragm (see below). Tenesmus — blood pooling in the pouch of Douglas irritating the rectum. Lightheaded — hypovolemia. Put together: not just an ectopic, but a ruptured one, and she is unstable.

    The IUD Point, Stated Precisely

    Her slide footnote is worth quoting almost exactly, because the sloppy version is wrong: IUDs do NOT increase the incidence of ectopic pregnancy. What they increase is the likelihood that a pregnancy is ectopic if a pregnancy occurs with an IUD in situ. The mechanism is straightforward once stated: the IUD is very effective at preventing intrauterine implantation, so it removes intrauterine pregnancies from the denominator far more than extrauterine ones — the proportion shifts without the absolute risk rising. Practically: an IUD user with a positive pregnancy test needs an ectopic excluded urgently.

    Risk factors to know: pelvic infection · previous ectopic · smoking · adhesions · multiple sexual partners · IUD in situ (as qualified above). Other sites beyond the tube: ovarian, cervical, cornual, abdominal, and caesarean-section scar. And the reason this matters: ectopic pregnancy accounts for 4–10% of first-trimester deaths in pregnancy.

    OptionDetail
    SurgicalResection via salpingectomy (remove the tube) or salpingostomy (open the tube, remove the pregnancy, preserve the tube)
    Medical — methotrexateRequires all of: clinically stable · size <3–4 cm · no fetal heartbeat · β-hCG <5000 · normal liver and renal function · reliable patient with access to emergency services
    Apply the Criteria to Katie

    Worth doing explicitly, because it is how this gets examined. Katie's β-hCG is 2345 (<5000, so that one passes) — but she has shoulder-tip pain, tenesmus, lightheadedness and an Hb of 82. She fails the very first criterion, clinically stable, and by a wide margin. Methotrexate is not an option; she goes to the operating room. The last criterion is also worth noticing as a principle: medical management commits the patient to follow-up and to recognizing deterioration at home, so reliability and access to emergency services are clinical criteria, not administrative ones.

    Part 3

    Case 2 — Pelvic Inflammatory Disease

    Case 2: Sara, 17

    The "Chandelier Sign"

    Extreme cervical motion tenderness — the patient jumps toward the chandelier when the cervix is moved on bimanual exam. It signals peritoneal irritation of the upper genital tract and is a classic PID finding. Note also that Sara presented for a routine exam, not with an emergency — the diagnosis came from asking and examining.

    The Definitional Point

    Her slide draws a line that is easy to miss: PID is specifically a community-acquired infection initiated by a sexually transmitted agent. That is what distinguishes it from pelvic infections caused by medical procedures, by pregnancy, or by other primary abdominal processes. So "pelvic infection" is not synonymous with PID, and the distinction changes both the likely organisms and the management. 4.6 covers PID in much greater depth, including the organisms, admission criteria and the 14-day rule — read the two together.

    Escalation for non-response: laparoscopy if the diagnosis is uncertain or she is not responding, and consideration of interventional-radiology drainage if still not responding — i.e. suspect a tubo-ovarian abscess that needs draining rather than more antibiotics.

    Part 4

    Case 3 — Ovarian Torsion

    The surgical emergency on this list.

    Case 3: Helen, 45

    Reading This Case — and What Rules Out the Others

    Onset during vigorous physical activity (stopped mid-golf-game) is the history feature she flagged in Part 1 as pointing here. Sharp, stabbing, radiating to the back, with vomiting and collapse — torsion hurts out of proportion and is strongly emetogenic. Then the exclusions: β-hCG <1 rules out ectopic; Hb 128 and stable rules out significant hemorrhage, which separates it from Case 4; temp 37.5 with no infective history makes PID unlikely. What is left is the 9 cm tender palpable mass — and a 9 cm ovary is exactly the size that torts.

    1
    The ovary rotates around both the infundibulopelvic (IP) ligament and the utero-ovarian ligament.
    2
    Compression of the IP ligament reduces venous outflow first, and eventually arterial inflow — because veins are thin-walled and collapse at lower pressures.
    3
    Arterial perfusion continues while outflow is blocked → the ovary becomes edematous and markedly enlarged.
    4
    That enlargement compresses the vessels further — the cycle tightens itself.
    5
    Ischemia → necrosis, infarction and local hemorrhage. Which is why the clock matters.
    Part 5

    Case 4 — Ruptured Hemorrhagic Ovarian Cyst

    Case 4: Lisa, 33

    Why This Isn't Case 1 — the Deliberate Near-Miss

    Notice how carefully this case is built to look like the ruptured ectopic: tenesmus, shoulder pain, lightheadedness, and a falling hemoglobin (110 → 78) are all Katie's findings, and for the same underlying reason — blood in the peritoneum. The one finding that separates them is β-hCG <1: she is not pregnant, so it cannot be an ectopic. That is the whole lesson — hemoperitoneum has a characteristic presentation independent of its cause, and β-hCG is what tells you which cause you are dealing with.

    Two more features that fit: the LMP just over 2 weeks ago puts her right at ovulation, which is when these cysts rupture, and the pain began suddenly after intercourse — the blunt-trauma mechanism on the next slide. The postural vitals (110/70 with HR 80 lying, 86/55 with HR 112 standing) are the finding to actually copy into practice: her supine numbers look almost normal, and the instability only shows when she stands.

    The Neat Ending — and a Conflict to Notice

    Ovulatory suppression prevents recurrence because these cysts rupture at ovulation — no ovulation, no corpus luteum cyst to rupture. It is the same "non-contraceptive benefit of a contraceptive" logic that runs through 4.10 and 4.13.

    But note the clash the case sets up without stating it: Lisa is nulligravid and trying to conceive. An OCP prevents recurrence by preventing ovulation — which is precisely what she does not want. That tension, between treating the condition and respecting the patient's goal, is the same judgement Dr. Arntfield frames as "ask what her goals are" and Dr. Black frames as fertility-sparing treatment in 4.13. Worth having an answer ready if it comes up in the small group.
