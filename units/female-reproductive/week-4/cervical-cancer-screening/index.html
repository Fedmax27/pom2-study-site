    Cervical Cancer Screening

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Ontario switched to primary HPV screening in March 2025. The sample is still taken the same way; what the lab does with it changed. Cytology is now only done if high-risk HPV is found.
    • Screen from age 25, then every 5 years if HPV-negative — up from every 3 years with cytology. Only asymptomatic, ever sexually active people with a cervix.
    • HPV causes virtually all cervical cancer. Low-risk (6, 11) → warts and LSIL only. High-risk (16, 18, 31/33, 45) → LSIL or HSIL, and cancer.
    • The one-way rule: low-risk HPV can produce LSIL but never HSIL and never cancer. High-risk HPV can produce either. So an LSIL result alone does not tell you which type caused it.
    • Cervical cancer is ideal to screen for: well-understood natural history, a detectable precursor, and a slow progression (10+ years) — so even a false negative gets caught at the next round.
    • Gardasil 9 is the current vaccine, given in grade 7 in Ontario. One dose ages 9–20, two from 21, three if immunocompromised.
    • Vaccinated people still get screened. The vaccine does not cover every high-risk type.
    • Why the switch: HPV testing is more sensitive, detects earlier, and has a very high negative predictive value. Its weakness is a lower positive predictive value — which is exactly why cytology is kept as the triage test.

    Learning Objectives

    • Describe the natural history and epidemiology of cervical cancer.
    • Explain the role of HPV and the impact of vaccination on cervical cancer and screening.
    • Know the eligibility for cervical screening and how samples are collected and evaluated.
    • Summarize the rationale for the shift from cytology-based screening to primary HPV screening.
    • Identify key cytology outcomes and the role of cytology in an HPV-based screening programme.
    • Recognize the potential of cervical self-sampling.

    Dr. Cady Zeman, cytopathologist at London Health Sciences Centre. 39 minutes. She opens by flagging that the landscape is changing fast and this recording was made in summer 2025 — which is unusually honest and worth respecting when you read the numbers below.

    Part 1

    Natural History

    Why this cancer, of all cancers, is the one we screen best.

    HPV → SIL → Cancer

    1
    HPV infection, acquired during sexual contact. Most resolve on their own with no change to the epithelium at all.
    2
    A minority produce epithelial changes — SILSquamous intraepithelial lesion. The current term. Older names for the same thing: CIN (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia) and dysplasia.. Most of these also resolve on their own.
    3
    A subset progresses to squamous cell carcinoma if untreated — typically over ten years or more.

    Terminology, all meaning the same thing: SIL (current) · CIN · dysplasia. Different eras, same epithelial changes.

    Why Cervical Cancer Is the Textbook Screening Target

    1. The natural history is very well understood.
    2. We can screen for precursors, not just the invasive cancer.
    3. Progression is slow.

    Point 3 does the heavy lifting: because it takes 10+ years, a false negative on one round gets caught on the next — as long as screening happens at regular intervals. Screening is not a once-and-for-all event.

    Epidemiology

    In CanadaWorldwide
    Most commonly diagnosed cancer16th4th
    Cause of cancer death17th4th

    The global burden is driven by very high rates in economically disadvantaged regions that lack screening programmes and vaccination access. And the Canadian success is not complete: there are still gains to be made in under-screened communities and among people facing barriers to screening — which is the argument self-sampling rests on, at the bottom of this page.

    Part 2

    HPV

    The types, the risk, the vaccine.

    Low Risk vs High Risk

    Low risk: HPV 6, 11High risk: HPV 16, 18, 31/33, 45
    ProducesWarts and LSILLSIL or HSIL
    Cancer?No — non-oncogenicYes — oncogenic
    The Consequence She Wants You to Draw

    Because both low-risk and high-risk HPV can produce LSIL, seeing "LSIL" on a report does not tell you which type caused it — not unless HPV testing has been done.

    But HSIL can only come from high-risk HPV, and HSIL is the lesion that becomes cancer. HSIL is what screening exists to find.

    How Common, and Who Is at Risk

    • At least 75% of people are exposed in their lifetime — though this may fall with vaccination.
    • Transmitted by sexual contact including intimate skin-to-skin contact. Condoms are only partially protective, because they do not cover all the skin involved.
    • Risk factors: multiple partners · early onset of sexual activity · a high-risk partner · cigarette smoking.
    Why Smoking, of All Things?

    The suggested mechanism is that smoking interferes with immune surveillance, so the body is less able to clear an HPV infection.

    That gives you something concrete to offer: a patient with persistent SIL who smokes can be counselled to quit, because it may improve her chance of clearing the infection. That is a real, actionable piece of advice — not just generic smoking-cessation boilerplate.

    Vaccination

    VaccineCoversNote
    CervarixHPV 16, 18The two commonest high-risk types — 70% of cervical cancers
    Gardasil (quadrivalent)HPV 16, 18 + 6, 11Adds the low-risk types responsible for 90% of genital warts
    Gardasil 9 (nonavalent)The above + 5 more high-risk typesAvailable since 2017; the one used in Canada today
    GroupSchedule
    Age 9–201 dose — recently reduced from two
    Age 21–262 doses
    Age 27 and older2 doses
    Immunocompromised, including people living with HIV3 doses
    Two "Why" Questions Worth Being Able to Answer

    Why grade 7 in Ontario? Two reasons: the risk of previous HPV exposure is low at that age, and preteens mount a better immune response than older teens — independent of exposure. That better response is also why one dose now suffices under 21.

    Why vaccinate an adult who's already been exposed, or who has had abnormal cytology? Because there are many HPV types. The vaccine likely covers types that person hasn't met yet. That is where the benefit lies.

    Vaccinated people are still screened. The vaccine is not perfect and does not cover every high-risk type, so SIL and cancer remain possible. Vaccination does not replace screening.

    Part 3

    Doing the Test

    Who gets screened, and how not to get your sample rejected.

    Eligibility

    Three conditions, and each one has a reason:

    CriterionWhy
    AsymptomaticThis is a screening programme. An abnormal-looking cervix or concerning symptoms needs investigation, not a Pap test. Do not let a normal screening result reassure you out of a proper work-up
    Ever sexually activeHPV is sexually acquired, so there is nothing to find before that
    Age ~25 (25 in Ontario)Screening younger does not prevent cancer in that age group — progression is too slow for it to pay off. She notes the age could move later still

    Collection

    ReminderDetail
    Know your collection systemThinPrep (used in Ontario): do NOT leave the broom/brush in the vial — the sample will likely be rejected. SurePath: the device may be left in
    LubricantLukewarm water is preferred. If gel is needed it must be water-based, applied sparingly to the outer blades, avoiding the tip. Lubricant interferes with cellularity and specimen adequacy
    LabellingTwo identifiers — full legal name and date of birth — plus the collection date
    ExpiryCheck the vial's expiry date before you use it. Expired vial = rejected sample
    RequisitionComplete it before sending
    Part 4

    Cytology

    What the pathologist sees, and the vocabulary of the report.

    The Outcomes

    ResultMeansClinician treats it as
    NILMNegative for intraepithelial lesion or malignancy. 95% of samplesNormal
    LSILLow grade squamous intraepithelial lesionLow grade
    ASC-USAtypical squamous cells of undetermined significance. Differential: normal or LSILLow grade
    ASC-HAtypical squamous cells, cannot exclude a high grade lesion. Differential: normal or HSILHigh grade
    HSILHigh grade squamous intraepithelial lesionHigh grade
    SqCCSquamous cell carcinomaHigh grade
    What ASC-US and ASC-H Actually Are

    They are the pathologist saying "I'm not certain." ASC-US means this might be normal, it might be LSIL. ASC-H means this might be normal, it might be HSIL — and because the downside of missing HSIL is so much worse, ASC-H is managed as a high grade result.

    The Pap test is a screening test for squamous abnormalities. It is not a good test for glandular abnormalities — but sometimes they turn up almost by accident, and they are often hard to classify. Terms you may see: AIS (adenocarcinoma in situ), atypical glandular cells, atypical endocervical cells, atypical endometrial cells.

    Cytology resultManagement under the old cytology-based programme
    NILMRepeat routine screening in 3 years
    Low grade (LSIL, ASC-US)Repeat at an interval; if persistent, refer to colposcopy
    High grade (ASC-H, HSIL, SqCC)Refer to colposcopy
    GlandularColposcopy, sometimes with endometrial sampling
    Part 5

    The Switch to Primary HPV Screening

    Ontario, March 2025. This is the part most likely to be examined.

    Why Change Something That Worked?

    1
    HPV is the cause. It makes more sense to test for the causative agent than for an intermediate step.
    2
    Cytology gets worse in a vaccinated population. Vaccination lowers the prevalence of SIL and cancer — and positive predictive value depends on prevalence. As prevalence falls, the PPV of cytology falls with it. The test that served for decades literally works less well now.
    3
    The evidence is overwhelming. From roughly 2006–2015 there was an abundance of randomised controlled trials and meta-analyses showing HPV screening outperforms cytology.
    HPV testing beats cytology onMeaning
    SensitivityDetects more high-grade lesions
    Earlier detectionFinds them sooner
    Long-term protectionAfter a negative HPV test, far fewer high-grade lesions turn up in later rounds than after a negative cytology
    Negative predictive valueVery high — which is what justifies the 5-year interval
    Cancer preventionGreater protection against cancer overall
    The One Catch — and Why Cytology Survives

    HPV testing has a lower positive predictive value. A positive HPV test does not mean you have a high grade lesion or cancer — there are more "false positives" than with cytology.

    So HPV screening has to be paired with a more specific test, and that test is cytology. HPV finds who might have a problem; cytology sorts out who actually does. That is the entire logic of the algorithm below.

    The Algorithm

    Ontario switched in March 2025, joining two other provinces; most others have plans to follow. The sample is still collected the same way — it is what the lab does first that changed.

    HPV resultNext step
    NegativeReturn to screening in 5 years
    Positive for HPV 16 or 18Reflex cytology is made up, but she goes to colposcopy regardless of the cytology result — these are the highest-risk of the high-risk types. The cytology is still informative, just not decisive
    Positive for another high-risk type — reflex cytology shows HSIL, ASC-H or glandular abnormalityColposcopy
    Positive for another high-risk type — reflex cytology NILM, ASC-US or LSILRepeat HPV testing in 1 year
    At the 1-year repeat: HPV negativeReturn to screening in 5 years
    At the 1-year repeat: HPV 16/18 positiveColposcopy
    At the 1-year repeat: other high-risk typeReflex cytology again, and the same loop repeats — eventually she either goes to colposcopy or returns to routine screening

    She notes that each jurisdiction will vary this theme, particularly in how it handles high-risk types other than 16 and 18, and that the number of repeat rounds may differ. The shape — HPV first, cytology as triage, 16/18 fast-tracked to colposcopy — is the part to hold onto.

    Part 6

    Self-Sampling

    The emerging tool, and an honest account of where the evidence sits.

    Collecting Your Own Sample

    Self-sample resultNext step
    HPV negativeRoutine screening in 5 years — same as provider-collected
    HPV 16 or 18 positiveDirectly to colposcopy. No cytology slide is made — this is a vaginal sample, not a cervical one, so there is no indication for cytology
    Other high-risk typeShe must come back for a provider-collected sample so cytology can be done, and then enters the same loop as above
    • Why do it: it increases access for under-screened and never-screened people — exactly the group where Canada's remaining cervical cancer burden sits. It also has high acceptability in the general population.
    • Australia has offered it to all screen-eligible people since 2022. British Columbia included specific populations in its rollout.
    Where the Evidence Actually Sits — Her Honest Answer

    Is self-sampling as sensitive as provider sampling? As of 2025, the jury is still out. The doubts are specific:

    1. Some of the original data is old. 2. Some meta-analyses showing equivalency may have included people with high viral loads, which would inflate self-collection performance. 3. Some population-based implementation studies have cast doubt.

    She expects forthcoming unpublished data to support equivalency, and describes her position as cautious optimism. This is a good example of a lecturer separating what we know from what we expect — worth imitating.

    Summary

    • Cervical cancer is preventable through vaccination and screening.
    • HPV is the cause.
    • Vaccination changed the screening landscape — and is a major reason for the move to primary HPV screening.
    • Primary HPV screening is superior, especially in vaccinated populations.
    • Self-sampling is promising but not settled.
    • Cervical cancer could become the first major cancer we eliminate — if the tools are actually used.

    Her recommended resource: Cancer Care Ontario's HPV Testing Implementation Resource Hub, which is current because Ontario has just made the transition.
