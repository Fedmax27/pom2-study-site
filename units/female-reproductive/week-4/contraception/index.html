    Contraception

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Always quote TYPICAL use failure rates, never perfect use. Per 1,000 women in the first year: no method 850 · condoms 180 · pill, patch and ring 90 · Depo 60 · copper IUD 8 · hormonal IUS 2 · implant and vasectomy 0.5.
    • LARCs (IUD, IUS, implant) are the most effective and the most continued — over 80% still using them at 3 years vs about 50% for the pill, patch, ring and Depo. Given free choice and good counselling, most people pick one (the CHOICE project).
    • The main mechanism is almost always the progestin (thick cervical mucus, suppressed ovulation). In combined methods the estrogen is mostly there to control nuisance bleeding.
    • Estrogen is the part with the contraindications: smoking >15/day over 35, uncontrolled hypertension, DVT/PE, migraine with aura, stroke or ischemic heart disease, lupus with antiphospholipid antibodies, current breast cancer. Progestin-only methods and IUDs are usually still fine.
    • Emergency contraception: the copper IUD is the most effective and works up to 7 days. Then ulipristal (Ella, up to 5 days, works during the LH rise), then levonorgestrel (Plan B, best within 72 h, useless once the LH rise starts).

    Why This Conversation Matters

    Part 1

    Natural, Barrier & Permanent Methods

    The least effective, the most used, and the irreversible.

    Natural Family Planning

    MethodHow it works, and the catch
    Basal body temperatureThermometer at the bedside, taken before getting out of bed. A rise of 0.2–0.5°C marks the LH surge and ovulation. The catch: by the time it rises, ovulation has already happened
    Rhythm / calendarNeeds a genuinely predictable cycle and a 14-day luteal phase; requires avoiding intercourse for many days
    Cervical mucusClear, stretchy mucus that doesn't break between finger and thumb (spinnbarkeit) means the LH surge. Same catch as temperature
    WithdrawalSperm can be present in pre-ejaculate, and timing errors are common
    Lactational amenorrheaNeeds all of: <6 months postpartum, fully or nearly fully breastfeeding with no gaps over 4 hours, and no return of menses. The catch: menses returning means she already ovulated — you can miss the boat

    Barrier Methods

    • Material matters: latex protects against STIs; polyurethane slips and breaks more; lambskin gives no HIV protection and limited STI protection (it's porous).
    • Nonoxynol-9 is not recommended. No added effectiveness, and it damages vaginal epithelium, increasing HIV transmission.
    • Condom use drops when a hormonal method is added, so counsel that condoms are for STIs as well as pregnancy.
    • Fit is a real barrier, and a solvable one. Standard condoms vary by only a couple of centimetres; companies like TheyFit/myONE ship 60 sizes to Canada with a printable sizer, so "they don't fit" is not a reason to go without.

    Permanent Contraception

    • Salpingectomy (removing the tubes) is increasingly done: it adds little time or risk, and about 30% of serous ovarian cancers may start in the fallopian tube, so it is risk-reducing.
    • Regret is one of the most common complications, especially when young — but a young patient who understands that it is not reversible, and knows the cost of IVF or reversal, should be able to access it.
    Part 2

    Hormonal Contraception

    Progestin-only first, then combined, then the contraindications that decide between them.

    The Medical Eligibility Criteria

    Progestin-Only Methods

    How progestin-only methods work: thicker cervical mucus, impaired sperm motility, less tubal cilia activity (so the ovum is more likely to undergo apoptosis in transit), and, depending on the method, suppressed ovulation. Because there's no estrogen, they can be used immediately postpartum and post-abortion, and VTE risk isn't an issue.

    Three Depo Myths, Corrected

    "You can only use it for 2 years." Not true. Bone mineral density falls in the first two years then stabilizes, the loss is reversible on stopping (up to 98% returns, even in those who used it to menopause), and the size of the drop is similar to what happens in a term pregnancy or while breastfeeding. No fractures or osteoporosis have been attributed to it.

    "You need bone density screening on it." Not true either.

    What is true: the return of fertility is delayed — an average of 9 months, longer the longer it's been used (the only reversible method where this happens). And Depo is the only contraceptive clinically proven to cause weight gain: about 7 lb in the first year, with early gain predicting more. Counsel that it's an appetite stimulant, so she can get ahead of it.

    Combined Hormonal Contraception

    A piece of history from the lecture: the pill was meant to be progestin-only. The original batch was accidentally contaminated with estrogen; when they made a clean progestin-only version, women reported bloating, breast tenderness and nuisance bleeding — so the estrogen went back in. 16% of Canadian women aged 15–49 use a combined method, and about 44% of those using prescribed contraception use the pill. Continuous or extended use, skipping the hormone-free interval, is safe.

    Part 3

    Intrauterine Contraception & LARCs

    The most effective reversible methods, and the ones patients stay on.

    Copper IUD vs Hormonal IUS

    Why the Hormonal IUS Beats the Copper IUD on Failure Rate

    Their main mechanisms differ. The IUS works through cervical mucus, so even if it sits low in the uterus it still works. The copper IUD needs copper ions distributed throughout the cavity, so it has to be properly positioned to be fully effective. That is thought to explain much of the 2-vs-8 difference.

    Emergency Contraception

    Use it as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse. If the last period is unknown or unreliable, confirm a negative pregnancy test first: EC is not an abortifacient and does not work once pregnant.

    The Contraceptive CHOICE Project

    7,486 participants at risk of unintended pregnancy in the Chicago area got proper counselling (30–60 minutes covering effectiveness, side effects, risks and benefits of all methods) and then their method of choice, free, for three years.

    The lesson: when cost is removed and counselling is accurate, most people choose a LARC, regardless of age or parity. The reasons they usually don't are provider bias, misinformation and cost — all three of which are on us.

    The Summary the Lecture Ends On

    • Most women need effective contraception for most of their reproductive life.
    • LARCs are highly acceptable and effective and should be offered preferentially, at any age and any parity.
    • Encourage condoms as backup and for STI protection — especially here, where London has some of the highest chlamydia rates in the country.
    • If she picks a less effective method or none, discuss emergency contraception and how to get it before she needs it.
    • Permanent contraception needs a conversation about regret.
    • Natural family planning beats no method, and may be the only acceptable option in some cultural or religious contexts.
    • Every contraception discussion should also explore future fertility plans and the effect of age on fertility (4.2).
    • Counsel with typical-use failure rates.
