    Approach to Sexually Transmitted Infection

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Most chlamydia and half of gonorrhea are asymptomatic. That is the whole argument for screening rather than waiting for symptoms.
    • One swab tests for both chlamydia and gonorrhea. A self-collected vaginal swab is as accurate as a clinician-collected one. Urine works but must be first-catch, with no voiding in the last 2 hours.
    • Untreated chlamydia is the big fertility story: up to 30% develop PID, leading to tubal scarring, infertility and ectopic pregnancy.
    • Syphilis stages: painless chancre (primary) → rash on palms and soles + condyloma lata (secondary) → latent → tertiary (neuro, cardiovascular, gummatous). Treat with penicillin G; if allergic, the only satisfactory answer is desensitize, then give penicillin.
    • PID is polymicrobial and is NOT always an STI. Fewer than 50% culture positive for chlamydia or gonorrhea. Normal vaginal organisms can ascend too — so never imply infidelity.
    • PID treatment runs 14 days, versus 7 days for uncomplicated chlamydia. Tubo-ovarian abscess is treated with IV antibiotics, not routine drainage.
    • Chandelier sign (cervical motion tenderness) + fever + discharge + raised WBC in a young sexually active woman = PID until proven otherwise — after a negative pregnancy test.

    Learning Objectives

    • Describe clinical symptoms and findings for various STI presentations.
    • Outline diagnostic criteria for common STIs.
    • Apply treatment regimens to common STIs.
    • Define PID, its treatment and clinical outcomes.

    Read This Before the Treatment Doses

    This deck is dated 2021, and STI treatment has moved since. Most notably, the gonorrhea dose on these slides (ceftriaxone 250 mg IM) is lower than current guidance — the CDC moved to 500 mg IM as a single dose in its 2021 update, and routine co-administration of azithromycin was dropped in favour of doxycycline when chlamydia has not been excluded. The doses reproduced below are the ones on the slides, because those are what the module is testing you on. Before you prescribe any of this for a real patient, check the current Canadian Guidelines on Sexually Transmitted Infections — which the public health module (4.7) tells you to bookmark for exactly this reason.

    Part 1

    The Bacterial STIs

    Chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis.

    What Counts as an STI

    Chlamydia

    • Most common STI worldwide — the deck's figure is 4 million women annually. London, Ontario has one of the highest chlamydia incidence rates in Canada.
    • Screen opportunistically. Routine physicals, Pap appointments and other visits are the moment to raise it — especially in people with HIV or who are immunocompromised.
    SpecimenNotes
    Cervical or vaginal swabMost accurate. A patient-collected vaginal swab is as accurate as a clinician-collected cervical swab — a finding that matters a lot for access
    UrineMust be first-catch, not the clean-catch you send for a UTI, and the patient must not have voided in the last 2 hours
    • Reportable disease — public health is notified.
    • Sexual partners must be contacted and treated. No sexual contact until both she and her partners have completed treatment.
    • Treatment: doxycycline 100 mg PO BID × 7 days, or azithromycin 1 g PO × 1.
    Concept Check

    When should a test of cure for chlamydia be performed?
    a) If the infected person is pregnant  ·  b) If asymptomatic at diagnosis  ·  c) If they have PID at diagnosis  ·  d) 2 weeks after treatment

    Answer: (a) pregnancy. Note why (d) is wrong — 2 weeks is too early, and you would get a false positive from dead organisms.

    Gonorrhea

    • Same swab as chlamydia — you do not need two samples. Urine rules are identical (first-catch, 2 hours).
    • Also ascends to cause PID, in 10–49% of women with cervical gonorrhea.
    • Reportable, and partners must be treated.
    The Co-Infection Rule — It Only Runs One Way

    If a patient is gonorrhea-positive, they usually also have chlamydia, so you treat for both. The inverse is not true: chlamydia commonly exists on its own.

    The deck's regimen: ceftriaxone 250 mg IM (or cefixime 400 mg PO) plus azithromycin 1 g PO × 1 for the chlamydia. See the dating caveat at the top of this page — the ceftriaxone dose has since increased.

    Syphilis

    • Condyloma lata: large flat warts on the genitals in secondary syphilis. Distinguish from HPV warts, which are cauliflower-like.
    • Diffuse lymphadenopathy is also part of the secondary picture.
    StageTimingFeatures
    Primary10–90 daysPainless chancre; highly contagious
    SecondaryWeeks–months; lasts 4–6 weeksFever, headache, malaise · rash on palms and soles · condyloma lata · lymphadenopathy
    LatentCan last 20+ yearsAsymptomatic carrier, serologically positive. Can be transmitted vertically to the fetus
    Tertiary1–30 years after primaryNeurosyphilis (general paresis, tabes dorsalis) · cardiovascular (aortitis) · gummatous (granulomatous nodular lesions in skin and bone). Life-threatening.
    One Claim Worth Flagging

    The recording states that syphilis is not sexually transmitted during the latent stage, only vertically. That is too absolute. The standard division is early latent (within about the first year), where relapses of infectious secondary lesions can occur and sexual transmission is possible, versus late latent, where it is generally not. Vertical transmission remains possible throughout. The slide itself only says "asymptomatic carriers, serologically positive" — it does not make the claim. Worth confirming with your tutor which version the exam wants.

    1
    Scrape the chancre and look under darkfield microscopy — or, far more commonly,
    2
    VDRL, a blood screening test.
    3
    If positive, confirm with a fluorescent treponemal antibody test.
    4
    Treat: penicillin G 2.4 million units IM as a single dose.

    There is no alternative regimen for penicillin allergy. The only satisfactory treatment is desensitization, followed by penicillin. Remember that one — it is unusual, and therefore examinable.

    Concept Check

    Which accurately describes secondary syphilis?
    a) Neurosyphilis with CNS involvement  ·  b) Formation of a painless ulcer (chancre)  ·  c) Asymptomatic and non-transmittable  ·  d) Condyloma lata, diffuse lymphadenopathy and a rash involving palms and soles

    Answer: (d). (a) is tertiary, (b) is primary, (c) describes latent.

    Part 2

    The Viral STIs

    Hepatitis B and HIV — short, but the serology is examinable.

    Hepatitis B

    MarkerMeans
    HBsAgPresent in both acute and chronic infection — on its own it does not distinguish them
    IgM anti-HBcDiagnostic of acute or recently acquired infection
    anti-HBsProduced after a resolved infection, and is the only HBV antibody marker present after vaccination
    HBsAg + anti-HBc, IgM anti-HBc negativeChronic HBV infection
    Total anti-HBc aloneCould be acute, resolved, chronic, or a false positive
    • No specific therapy — treatment is supportive.
    • Vaccination is preventative and part of routine immunization.

    Caption warning: the auto-transcript of this section repeatedly writes "HPV" where the lecturer says HBV, and garbles the antibody names. The table above is taken from the slide, not the transcript.

    HIV

    • Transmitted in: blood, semen, vaginal fluids, rectal fluids, breast milk. Not in saliva.
    • Acute HIV is a non-specific influenza-like illness, often short-lived and easily missed.
    • Untreated, it becomes a multi-year chronic illness with progressive CD4+ T cell depletion → AIDS.
    • Diagnosis: HIV-1/2 antigen–antibody combination immunoassay. Treatment: antiretroviral therapy.
    Part 3

    Pelvic Inflammatory Disease

    The biggest single block of this lecture, and the one with the most clinical nuance.

    The Case

    Work It Through

    17-year-old · unwell with fevers, chills, nausea · generalized pelvic pain · dyspareunia · foul-smelling discharge · β-hCG negative · WBC 22 · chandelier sign.

    The chandelier sign is cervical motion tenderness: moving the cervix on bimanual exam reproduces exquisite pain — supposedly enough to make the patient reach for the chandelier.

    Diagnosis: PID. Note the order of operations — the negative pregnancy test comes first, because ectopic pregnancy is the thing that kills her (see 4.9).

    What PID Is

    The definitional boundary matters: PID is a community-acquired infection initiated by a sexually transmitted agent. A pelvic infection or abscess after a D&C or other procedure is not PID — nor are infections from pregnancy or primary abdominal processes.

    The Counselling Point She Spends the Most Time On

    Fewer than 50% of women with PID culture positive for chlamydia or gonorrhea. Normal vaginal organisms — anaerobes, Gardnerella vaginalis, Haemophilus influenzae, enteric gram-negative rods, streptococci — can ascend and cause PID given the opportunity.

    So do not assume, or imply, that a patient or her partner has been unfaithful. Being told you are being tested for gonorrhea and chlamydia is alarming; frame it as ruling things out, not accusing.

    Because it is polymicrobial, treatment requires broad-spectrum coverage.

    Epidemiology and Presentation

    • ~100,000 cases annually in Canada. 10–15% of reproductive-age women will have at least one episode.
    • Symptoms: pelvic pain · dyspareunia · vaginal discharge · fever · cervicitis · cervical motion tenderness · pelvic mass · "stuck pelvis" from adhesions binding the uterus to the sidewalls.
    InvestigationWhy
    Pregnancy testAlways. Rules out ectopic
    CBC, electrolytesDegree of inflammatory response, baseline
    Vaginal, urine ± blood culturesBlood cultures if she is unwell
    UltrasoundUsually. Looking for tubo-ovarian abscess, which changes management
    MRIRarely, in unclear cases
    SurgeryIf the diagnosis is unclear or she is not responding. PID is largely a clinical diagnosis

    Treatment

    Admit if…Because
    Cannot rule out a surgical emergencyEctopic, appendicitis
    Failed oral therapy, or non-compliantNeeds IV and supervision
    Severely ill (nausea, vomiting, fever)Cannot tolerate oral medication
    Tubo-ovarian abscessNeeds IV antibiotics for penetration into the abscess
    PregnantMore serious — always inpatient
    Youth / adolescentOften, but not always. Usually driven by psychosocial factors — access to antibiotics, compliance
    The Counterintuitive One

    A tubo-ovarian abscess is treated with IV antibiotics, not with incision and drainage. That is the opposite of how you treat abscesses almost everywhere else in the body. Surgical or interventional-radiology drainage is only for patients who fail to respond.

    The number to hold onto: 14 days for PID, versus 7 days for uncomplicated chlamydia. That contrast is exactly the kind of thing an exam asks.

    Concept Check

    Which of the following is FALSE regarding PID?
    a) If left untreated, PID can lead to pelvic and liver adhesions, chronic pain and infertility  ·  b) Individuals with PID test positive for chlamydia and/or gonorrhea in the majority of cases  ·  c) PID should be treated as an inpatient in pregnancy  ·  d) PID is a polymicrobial infection

    Answer: (b) is the false one. Fewer than 50% culture positive. The other three are all true.
