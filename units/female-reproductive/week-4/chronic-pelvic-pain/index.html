    Clinical Approach to Chronic Pelvic Pain

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Definition of CPP: pain of at least 6 months' duration, below the umbilicus, severe enough to cause functional disability or require treatment. All three parts.
    • Acute pain is a protective mechanism. Chronic pain serves no physiologic purpose. Her two-line slide, and the conceptual heart of the lecture — it is why the goal shifts from "find and fix the lesion" to "reduce pain and restore function."
    • Pain intensity is often not proportional to tissue damage. Same lesson as endometriosis in 4.13, generalized.
    • Up to 50% of women with CPP have experienced some kind of abuse or trauma. Her slide ends with four exclamation marks on "take a thorough history."
    • Listen to the story. These women have often seen numerous specialists and tried many treatments without relief; many feel frustrated and hopeless by the time they reach you.
    • It is a big deal systemically: 10% of outpatient gynecology referrals, the indication for 40% of laparoscopies and 20% of hysterectomies for benign disease, and ~$88 million/year in US outpatient visits.
    • Pain can arise from five categories of structure: pelvic viscera · somatic structures · MSK and ligamentous · GI and urologic (referred) · and neurologic. Learn the seven evidence-backed common causes in the table below.
    • Five cases, five diagnoses: primary dysmenorrhea · endometriosis · adenomyosis · fibroids · myofascial pelvic pain.
    • Adenomyosis and myofascial pain are both clinical diagnoses — history and physical, not a test. Adenomyosis is a tender, enlarged, boggy uterus in a parous woman in her 40s–50s.
    • Myofascial pelvic pain needs a multidisciplinary approach — gynecology, physiotherapy, pain service, psychiatry/psychotherapy. And the honest goal is pain reduction and improved function, not necessarily elimination.

    Part 1

    What Makes Chronic Pain Different

    The Approach, and the Numbers

    CriterionRequirement
    DurationAt least 6 months
    LocationBelow the umbilicus
    SeveritySevere enough to cause functional disability or require treatment
    Why This Distinction Changes Management

    Acute pain is protective — it signals tissue damage and drives you to act, which is exactly the logic of 4.15, where the pain points at a ruptured ectopic or a torted ovary that needs fixing now. Chronic pain serves no physiologic purpose — it is no longer a reliable report about tissue. Two consequences follow, and both are on her slides: pain intensity is often not proportional to tissue damage, so a normal laparoscopy does not mean the pain isn't real; and the therapeutic goal shifts from finding a lesion to cure toward reducing pain and restoring function — which is exactly how she frames the goal in Case 5.

    This also explains the uncomfortable statistic above: if 40% of laparoscopies are done for CPP, a lot of them find nothing, and the diagnostic reflex of "look inside for the lesion" is a poor fit for a pain that isn't reporting a lesion.

    The Differential

    CategoryThe evidence-backed common causes
    GynecologicEndometriosis · chronic pelvic inflammatory disease
    Urinary tractInterstitial cystitis / painful bladder syndrome
    GastrointestinalIrritable bowel syndrome
    MusculoskeletalPelvic floor myalgia · myofascial pain (trigger points)
    NeurologicNeuralgia — especially iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, genitofemoral or pudendal nerves
    The Take-Away From That Table

    Only two of the seven best-evidenced causes are gynecologic. Two are musculoskeletal, one urologic, one GI, one neurologic. That is a strong argument against reflexively treating chronic pelvic pain as a gynecologic problem — and it is the structural reason Case 5 ends with a multidisciplinary team rather than an operation. It also explains her own remark on that case slide: "differentiating the cause of pain may be difficult for the gynecologist."

    Worth also noticing the mental health block in the full table — somatization, substance use, physical and sexual abuse, depression, sleep disorders. Given the 50% abuse/trauma figure, these are not afterthoughts in the differential.

    Part 2

    Case 1 — Primary Dysmenorrhea

    Case 1: Jessica, 19

    Why This Is Primary

    Everything in the history is consistent with a normal cycle producing pain: regular 29-day cycles, heavier flow in the first two days, and pain that lessens as the cycle goes on — i.e. the pain tracks the flow. Compare with Case 2 (the same patient six years later), where pain begins 2 days before menses and is worsening — the two features that mark it out as secondary. Dr. Weir makes the same primary-vs-secondary cut in 4.12: primary is functional and younger, secondary is structural and acquired, and therefore commoner with age.

    1
    Menses is a generalized inflammatory state.
    2
    Prostaglandins released from endometrial sloughing at the beginning of menses induce uterine contractions.
    3
    When uterine pressure exceeds arterial pressure, the myometrium's own blood supply is cut off → uterine ischemia.
    4
    Ischemia means anaerobic metabolites accumulate, and these stimulate type C pain neurons → dysmenorrhea.
    Why the Two Treatments Work, and Why Each One Works

    The mechanism tells you both drugs directly. NSAIDs work at step 2 — they inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, so there are fewer and weaker contractions and pressure never exceeds arterial pressure. Ovulatory suppression (e.g. the OCP) works upstream of step 1: as her sub-bullet says, hormonal medications that reduce menstrual blood flow improve dysmenorrhea — less endometrium to slough means less prostaglandin released. That is the same "thin the endometrium" logic as in 4.10 and 4.13, arriving at the same drugs from a third direction.

    Part 4

    Case 3 — Adenomyosis

    Case 3: Jessica, now 42

    Every Clause in This Case Is Doing Work

    Three successful pregnancies — adenomyosis is a disease of parous women; Dr. Weir's balloon model in 4.12 explains why it essentially requires a term pregnancy. Now 42 — it presents in the 4th and 5th decades. Periods less painful than before but much heavier — the endometriosis was treated, and this is a new problem dominated by bleeding rather than pain. Bleeding through her clothing despite continuous OCPs — failure of medical management, which in 4.12 is precisely the route by which adenomyosis gets diagnosed. A heavy pelvic sensation — the diffusely enlarged uterus. And no hot flashes or night sweats — that clause is there to rule out menopause as the explanation for her changing periods at 42.

    Part 5

    Case 4 — Fibroids

    The longest section of the deck, and the one that goes deepest into treatment.

    Case 4: Erica, 35

    Two Details That Decide the Management

    "20-week size, mobile uterus." Mobile matters — in 4.10 Dr. Arntfield teaches that benign enlargement stays mobile while malignancy fixes the pelvic structures. And at 35 with a mobile, slowly enlarging, presumably multiple-fibroid uterus, this is not the leiomyosarcoma pattern Dr. Weir warns about in 4.12 (single dominant rapidly growing mass in a peri/postmenopausal woman).

    "Wishing to achieve a pregnancy." This is the constraint that makes the treatment section meaningful. It rules out hysterectomy, and — as her own slide notes further down — pregnancy after uterine artery embolization is not advised. It also rules out the ovulatory suppression that would otherwise be first line. In practice it points at myomectomy, or hysteroscopic resection if the fibroid is submucosal.

    Part 6

    Case 5 — Myofascial Pelvic Pain

    Deliberately last. This is the case with nothing to find and nothing to cut out.

    Case 5: Mya, 23

    How This Case Excludes Everything Before It

    Read it as a systematic set of negatives. "Not associated with any time in her cycle" and "cycles are regular" — rules out dysmenorrhea, endometriosis, adenomyosis and fibroids, all of which are cycle-linked. Nulliparous at 23 — wrong demographic for adenomyosis and fibroids. Midline mobile uterus, no adnexal masses — no structural lesion. What remains is globally tender pelvis and pain so severe the speculum exam could not be done at all. That last detail is the finding, not a gap in the assessment.

    And note the functional cost, which is the definition's third criterion in action: it prevents her from working or attending school, and she is not sexually active because of the pain — five years of it, starting at 18.

    The Chicken-and-Egg Point, Which Is the Real Lesson

    Her first bullet says myofascial pain may develop in response to pain caused by gynecologic disease — or arise independently from faulty body mechanics. So it can be both a consequence of endometriosis or fibroids and a cause of pain in its own right, which means treating the gynecologic lesion may not resolve the pain, and the pelvic floor may need treating separately. That is why "differentiating the cause of pain may be difficult for the gynecologist," and it ties back to "pain intensity is often not proportional to tissue damage" from the start of the lecture: a woman with treated endometriosis and persistent pain has not necessarily failed treatment — she may have acquired a second, muscular pain generator.

    1
    The team: gynecologist + physiotherapist + pain treatment service + psychiatry/psychotherapy as needed.
    2
    Manual soft tissue release to reduce pelvic floor resting tone and tension — her slide calls this essential.
    3
    Local anaesthetic injection at trigger points where helpful.
    4
    Amitriptyline or gabapentin as pharmacological aids.
    5
    Psychotherapy and support — "invaluable," particularly given the history below.
    The Two Sentences to Take From This Lecture

    On prognosis, honestly stated: "Recovery can be a long process with patients realizing their pain may never be entirely eliminated; the goal is for pain reduction and improved function." For a 23-year-old who cannot work or attend school, improved function is the outcome that matters, and promising elimination would be both untrue and unkind.

    On the associated history: many of these patients have a history of sexual abuse, poor sexual experience, recurrent infection (yeast, UTI, PID), or endometriosis. That list closes the loop with the slide near the top of the lecture — up to 50% of women with CPP have experienced abuse or trauma — and it reframes Mya's "not sexually active due to the pain" as something to ask about gently rather than record and move past. It is also a coherent mechanism: repeated pain or trauma involving the pelvis produces protective guarding, and sustained guarding is the high resting tone that defines the condition.
