    Vulvar Lesions & Vulvar Cancer

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Vulvar skin is skin. Dr. Sovran's closing line and the organizing idea of the whole lecture. Everything that happens to skin elsewhere happens here — it just looks different, because the area is warm, moist, occluded and rubbed.
    • She names two conditions you are actually expected to diagnose and treat: lichen simplex chronicus and lichen sclerosus. Everything else is recognition and referral.
    • Separate the three lichens on three questions: Is there scarring? Is it in the vagina or mouth? Is there a cancer risk? The table below is the whole lecture in one grid.
    • Lichen sclerosus is common (~1 in 300), carries a ~4% lifetime squamous cell carcinoma risk, occurs in children (15% of cases), and is associated with hypothyroidism — so check a TSH annually. Treat it forever, symptoms or not, because treating it is what lowers the cancer risk.
    • Lichen planus is the one that gets into the vagina and the mouth. Speculum exam is mandatory, tell the patient to mention it to their dentist, and dilators or regular intercourse may be needed to keep the vagina patent — two opposing erosions can heal together and close it off.
    • Lichen simplex chronicus is the end stage of the itch–scratch–itch cycle. Architecture is normal, there are no scars. Treat the underlying cause and break the cycle.
    • When in doubt, biopsy — a 4 mm punch, and multiple biopsies from areas that look different. Vulvar cancer "can look like lots of things," and 50% of women with it are asymptomatic.
    • Look and feel. Cancers feel firm and are often tender to palpation — and don't skip the inguinal lymph node chain, which is the drainage basin.
    • Don't just do the Pap and declare her fine. Two of her cases are women who came in asymptomatic, for routine screening, and had obvious disease on the vulva if you looked with intent.

    Learning Objectives

    • Describe different lesions of the vulva.
    • List the various differential diagnoses for the various lesions.
    • Compare and contrast vulvar conditions with other skin lesions.
    • Describe the presentation of vulvar malignancy.
    • List benign masses of the vulva with clinical presentations.

    Part 1

    The Three Lichens

    The core of the lecture. Dr. Sovran teaches these as three cases, each opening with a "Your Diagnosis Is?" slide offering the same four choices — which is the point: they look alike until you know what separates them.

    The Comparison That Does the Work

    Her summary slide reduces the three to a 3×3 grid. Learn this first, then read the detail.

    Lichen sclerosus (LS)Lichen planus (LP)Lichen simplex chronicus (LSC)
    SymptomItch or burnItch or burn++++ Itch
    ScarringScarsScarsNo scar
    SiteNot in vaginaIn vagina AND mouthNot in vagina
    Cancer riskYes — ~4% SCCYes — 2–3% SCCNot intrinsically*
    TreatmentTopical superpotent steroidTopical superpotent steroid (+ vaginal)Treat the cause, then the skin

    *Her caveat, verbatim in substance: LSC does not carry a cancer risk of its own, "but prolonged inflammation in any system can technically increase your risk of cancer."

    The Single Most Useful Discriminator

    Architecture. LSC is itch and thickening with normal anatomy — labia minora still present, no adhesions. LS and LP destroy architecture: the clitoral hood agglutinates and buries the clitoris, the labia minora resorb and disappear, and the introitus narrows. So her clinical habit is worth copying — when the labia minora look absent, ask the patient directly: "do you remember ever having larger labia minora?" Some women simply have small ones, and that question separates normal variation from disease.

    Lichen Simplex Chronicus

    Essentially vulvar eczema — and, as she puts it, the end stage of the itch cycle.

    What her case looked like: a 36-year-old with a very itchy rash flaring with heat and friction, not responding to topical steroids, scratching all night, with hay fever and environmental allergies. On the photograph she points to the left interlabial sulcus being noticeably thicker with increased skin markings than the other side — the signature of rubbing. Other clues: a fractured hair pattern from scratching, hyperpigmentation from the thickening, and, decisively, normal architecture with both labia minora present. Her LSC was probably driven by underlying yeast.

    Why the Hyperpigmentation Is Not Lichen Sclerosus

    Her analogy: the vulva is a sweaty, occluded area, and chronically rubbed skin thickens — like fingertips going white and wrinkly after a long bath. The resulting hyperpigmentation is a consequence of thickening, not of sclerosis. Lichen sclerosus goes the other way: hypopigmented, thin, easily traumatized skin. Thick and dark = LSC. Thin, white and scarred = LS.

    Lichen Sclerosus

    Her case: a 60-year-old attending for a routine Pap, with hypothyroidism but otherwise well and with no complaints of her own. On the vulva: hyperpigmentation anteriorly, labia minora completely resorbed, petechial trauma at the introitus, and hypopigmented plaque. Adhesions of the clitoral hood with the glans buried beneath. A urethral caruncle alongside, consistent with being menopausal. Her line about it: "it's not okay to just do your Pap and be like, you're totally fine."

    • The figure-of-8 pattern — the classic distribution, running around the anterior vulva and around the perineal/perianal area, making an 8.
    • It occurs in children (15% of cases), so you cannot dismiss it in a child. Harder to spot: the colour change is subtler and the vulvar architecture is not yet fully developed, so "loss of architecture" is a difficult judgement. What she points to in the paediatric photograph is thickened skin around the anus and perineal body from rubbing. In children the presenting symptom is often painful defecation, with fissures as thin skin splits on passing stool, and rectal bleeding.
    • Associated with hypothyroidism → annual TSH.
    • Why we care: a ~4% lifetime squamous cell carcinoma risk if untreated. That single number is the reason to treat an asymptomatic patient.

    What the photo series shows, in her words: loss of labia minora with a few petechial spots from rubbing · ulcerations where it is unclear whether the cause is scratching or the disease itself · concurrent yeast in one image · varying degrees of hyperpigmentation near the perineal body. In advanced disease: complete loss of architecture, skin so thin it is easily traumatized, with ecchymoses and petechiae. One image also shows a rectocele, which she flags as unrelated to the skin disease — a useful reminder not to attribute every finding to the diagnosis you came for.

    Why Follow-Up Visits Are Part of the Treatment

    She lists three distinct jobs for the review appointment, which is more than "checking it worked": confirming the skin is actually progressing, confirming she is applying it correctly (a superpotent steroid used wrongly either fails or causes harm), and confirming the disease is controlled — because control, not symptom relief, is what lowers the SCC risk.

    Vulvar Melanosis

    Placed here deliberately, because it is what you see after the inflammation of lichen sclerosus — and because it mimics the thing you are most afraid of.

    • It is a post-inflammatory reaction, commonly following lichen sclerosus or another inflammatory vulvar condition.
    • The problem is that it can look like melanoma. Her practical answer: track it photographically so change is detectable, and biopsy if concerned. On a multi-spot lesion, biopsy the darkest area — the one most likely to be melanoma if it is.
    • Skin tone changes the presentation. She makes this point explicitly: in Caucasian women inflammation tends to add pigment (melanosis), whereas in Black women pigment tends to be removed after inflammation. Both directions can obscure the diagnosis, and both are worth expecting.
    • Photography has a second benefit — it lets the patient examine herself meaningfully between visits, because she knows what her own baseline looks like.

    Lichen Planus

    Her case: a 50-year-old with dyspareunia, otherwise healthy. Her line: you might glance and say the vulva looks normal, but "if you look with the intent of seeing what you need to see" there is a lacy white pattern on the vulva extending up toward the labia minora, and red erosions medially on the vestibule.

    Erosion vs Ulceration — Worth Fixing Now

    A distinction she stops to make, and it is generally useful: ulcerations are deep, extend to the dermis, and have a yellow base. Erosions are superficial and look red, "almost like you've shaved a layer of skin off." That missing layer is exactly why erosive lichen planus hurts and causes dyspareunia.

    • It gets into the vagina — unlike lichen sclerosus. So a speculum examination with routine monitoring is part of care, not optional.
    • It gets into the mouth — she asks patients to tell their dentist the diagnosis.
    • 2–3% squamous cell carcinoma risk. Less than lichen sclerosus, but real — her closing line makes a point of it: "lichen sclerosus is associated with cancer, but so is lichen planus."
    • The complication to understand mechanically: two erosions facing each other — one on each side of the vagina, or one on each labium minus — can heal together. That agglutination can close off the introitus and, at the extreme, make it impossible to have intercourse or even to void.
    Two Practical Points She Adds Out Loud

    1. If you cannot tell LS from LP, it matters less than you'd fear — "the treatments are the same": clobetasol 0.05% ointment daily until the visible signs resolve (red or white), then maintenance from twice weekly, titrated up or down. Biopsy still confirms it, but you are not paralysed while you wait.

    2. A cost workaround. Compounded vaginal hydrocortisone suppositories are expensive; she instead uses over-the-counter Anusol-HC suppositories — made for hemorrhoids — inserted vaginally, for much less money. And the reason dilators or regular intercourse are prescribed is not comfort, it is keeping the vagina patent against the agglutination described above.

    Part 2

    The Rest of the Differential

    Conditions to recognize, grouped the way she presents them: inflammatory and infectious lesions, then masses.

    Aphthous Ulcers

    Her case: a 17-year-old with a vulvar lesion for the third time, episodes years apart, each a day or two after strep throat. Initially not painful, then uncomfortable. The lesion is small — ~3 mm, dark, almost black, not raised but slightly depressed. That is the early presentation before it ulcerates.

    Vitiligo

    Vitiligo vs Lichen Sclerosus — the Discriminator

    Both present as white vulvar skin, and she says outright it can be hard. The answer is again architecture: vitiligo is depigmentation of normal skin — no scarring, no architectural loss. So you look for scarring to tell them apart. Two complications she is honest about: patients can have both, and in extensive vulvar vitiligo the architecture may be hard to read at all. A confirmatory clue in her photograph — the vulvar hair is depigmented too, not just the skin. Distribution helps as well: vitiligo elsewhere favours acral areas, around body orifices, and extensor surfaces.

    Seborrheic Keratoses and Molluscum

    Two counselling points she adds: molluscum is "sexually transmitted in a sense" — the lubrication of intercourse helps spread it from existing genital lesions — and tell her not to shave, because shaving spreads it. They are soft enough to be scratched off with a fingernail, which is also how they spread.

    Yeast Vulvitis

    Her headline: yeast vulvitis is often underdiagnosed, and — the thing people get wrong — you do not need vaginitis or clumpy white discharge to have it. It can be a purely vulvar, purely skin problem.

    Two Signs Explained

    Splash dysuria is not urethral pain. It does not hurt while the urine is coming out — it hurts when urine touches the broken vulvar skin. That tells you the problem is the skin, not the urinary tract, and it is a discriminator you can get from history alone.

    Satellite lesions are small separate red papules just outside the main confluent rash, which will eventually coalesce into it. They are what makes a confluent red rash candidal rather than simply irritant.

    The case worth remembering: a ~36-year-old with groin fissures, a red confluent rash with flaking edges, satellite papules, and lichenification from scratching — so she had both LSC and yeast vulvitis. She turned out to be diabetic and did not know it. Yeast vulvitis is a reason to check a glucose.

    • A cost tip she volunteers: the 150 mg fluconazole is the over-the-counter one and is significantly more expensive — she deliberately prescribes 100–200 mg doses because they are cheaper. On patients taking multiple cardiac drugs she drops to 100 mg twice weekly × 3–6 weeks instead.
    • Her compounding trick: hydrocortisone 1% + clotrimazole 1% mixed together — and she notes it makes an excellent diaper-rash cream.
    • Think of this diagnosis in anyone who wears a diaper — infants through incontinent adults. She flags it as useful in paediatrics, family medicine, geriatrics and internal medicine, not just gynecology.
    • Complicated cases exist — one of her patients has lupus and psoriasis and lives on suppressive fluconazole; another had erosions and lichenified groin skin from a tight, aggressively rubbing diaper strap.

    Labial Adhesions

    Not a disease of its own — the end result of anything that scars. In her photograph, whether the cause was lichen sclerosus or lichen planus is no longer determinable. Two things follow:

    • They may be completely asymptomatic, or cause real problems: if she is voiding through a tiny residual opening, she is at higher risk of voiding dysfunction and urine tracking back into the vagina.
    • They can be opened in clinic under local freezing — not an operating-room problem.

    Vulvar Masses

    Objective 5. Two benign entities, and the one referral confusion she wants you to avoid.

    Bartholin Gland Cyst and Abscess

    Why She Spends a Slide on Anatomy

    Because of a specific, common error: Bartholin abscesses get referred from the emergency department, and the frequent confusion is with a labial abscess. Location is what distinguishes them — so knowing where the gland sits and where it drains is the diagnostic step, before any imaging or procedure.

    Vulvar Cancer

    Objective 4. Her instruction is unusual and worth honouring: don't over-focus on the classification detail — focus on including it in the differential and biopsying properly.

    Squamous Cell Carcinoma and VIN

    HPV-relatedNon-HPV-related
    AgeYounger womenOlder women
    NumberMultiple lesionsSolitary lesion
    AppearanceFlat-topped red, brown or skin-coloured papules and plaquesPink, red or white papule, nodule or ulcer
    SiteVestibule, labia majora, perivulvar, perianalVestibule and labia minora
    BackgroundHPV infectionFrequently lichen sclerosus or lichen planus
    BiopsyIn-situ carcinoma (VIN); invasion occasionalOften already invasive
    Why This Table Is Clinically Loaded

    Two consequences fall straight out of it. The non-HPV pathway is the one that grows out of the lichens you just learned — which is the second reason to treat lichen sclerosus forever, and to biopsy any lichen sclerosus lesion that does not resolve or is refractory to therapy. And the non-HPV lesion is more often already invasive at biopsy, in an older woman, as a single lesion you might be tempted to watch. The HPV pathway, by contrast, is multifocal and recurrent — she notes of one VIN 3 case that being HPV-related gives it "a very high likelihood of recurring."

    Examination and Biopsy

    Additional points from her detailed examination slide: direct observation is best, with photographs for comparison; inspect and palpate the vulva and groin for masses, ulceration and colour change; most VIN lesions are multifocal and sit in the non-hairy part of the vulva; lesions are often raised or verrucous and white, but may be red, pink, grey or brown; macular lesions mostly occur on adjacent mucosal surfaces. And the mimics to know: lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, condyloma acuminata and condyloma lata can all mimic VIN. The rule that follows — if one of those is suspected clinically but the lesion does not completely resolve, or is refractory to therapy, biopsy it.

    What the malignancy photographs show, in her narration: VIN 3 with HPV — a verrucous growth, with two visibly different areas, so she would biopsy both. VIN 3 with HPV plus perianal SCC — a larger tumour next to a differently raised one; again two biopsies. Vulvar SCC with HPV, perianal — her description is that it "just looks like purple dots," which is precisely her point that it can look like anything. Differentiated VIN 3 on lichen sclerosus — needs biopsy and/or excision to exclude invasion, and she notes the clitoris lies directly beneath it. Lichen sclerosus with SCC — an ulcerative, advanced form, which she uses to make the point that not all of them are growths that stick out; some are ulcers, and you find them by feeling the tissue.

    Melanoma and Basal Cell Carcinoma

    On the melanoma photographs she draws the distinction from melanosis directly: yes, it looks like melanosis — but this one is a raised dark patch, and one area looks distinctly different from the rest, so multiple biopsies are appropriate. Her framing of BCC is the thesis of the lecture restated: "skin is skin — just because it's skin on the vulva doesn't mean it does not occur there."

    Extramammary Paget Disease

    The Association Not to Miss

    She raises something the slides only hint at: extramammary Paget disease may be primary or secondary, and the secondary form can be associated with another underlying malignancy — of the breast, or the genital or urinary tract. So the diagnosis obliges you to look further rather than treat it as an isolated skin lesion. It can also extend into the rectum, vagina or urinary tract directly.
