    Teratogenesis

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • A teratogen is a substance that may produce physical or functional defects in the human embryo or fetus after exposure in pregnancy.
    • Timing decides the damage. Weeks 1–2 are all-or-nothing; weeks 3–8 are organogenesis and the period of greatest sensitivity; after that exposure causes functional defects and growth restriction rather than major structural anomalies.
    • This is not a rare problem: 9 out of 10 pregnant women take at least one medication.
    • Four categories of teratogen: physical · infectious · maternal conditions · chemical.
    • Diabetes is a maternal-condition teratogen — neural tube, CNS, spine, cardiovascular and limb defects.
    • The counselling happens in three phases — pre-conception, antenatal and post-partum — and the pre-conception one matters most, because it is the only point at which you can still change the drug.

    What a Teratogen Is
    DefinitionA substance that may produce physical or functional defects in the human embryo or fetus after exposure in pregnancy
    Major anomalyAffects an infant's life expectancy, health status, physical or social functioning
    Minor anomalyLittle or no impact on health or on short- or long-term function
    Why it matters9 out of 10 pregnant women will take at least one medication

    Timing Is the Whole Concept

    Why Weeks 1–2 Are "All or Nothing"

    Before organogenesis begins, the conceptus is a ball of undifferentiated cells. An insult either kills enough cells that the pregnancy is lost, or the remaining cells replace what was damaged and development continues normally. There is no partially-formed organ to malform, because no organ has started forming. That is why the chart marks it "usually not susceptible" rather than "safe" — the risk is loss, not malformation.

    Two Things Students Skip in That Table

    Vitamins, minerals and herbal products are in the chemical category. "Natural" is not a safety claim — it is a category of exposure that gets missed because patients do not think of it as medication and so do not report it.

    Diabetes is a teratogen. Not a risk factor in the vague sense — poorly controlled maternal diabetes produces a specific and recognisable spectrum of defects, which is precisely why glycaemic control before conception is a preventive intervention. The pre-conception genetics counterpart is 4.16.

    The FDA Drug Risk Classification

    Read C as "We Don't Know," Not "Probably Fine"

    The gap between B and C is not a gap in safety — it is a gap in study. A category C drug has simply not been tested in the relevant way. The practical consequence is that "category C" is the start of a conversation about risk and benefit, not the end of one, and it is why the resources at the bottom of this page exist.

    Case

    30-year-old woman, previous renal transplant, on mycophenolate

    The numbers attached to that exposure:

    ~25% of those exposed will result in a malformation.

    ~50% spontaneous abortion.

    Why This Case and Not a Simpler One

    Because it is the hard version. She needs the immunosuppression — stopping mycophenolate risks the graft, and losing the graft is not a better outcome for a pregnancy. The teaching point is not "avoid teratogens"; it is that someone has to have this conversation before she conceives, when switching to an alternative agent is still possible. Once she is pregnant on mycophenolate, the 25% and 50% are already in play.

    Counselling, in Three Phases

    PhaseWhat you do
    Pre-conception Optimise disease management · optimise other considerations, e.g. 1 mg folic acid daily for antiepileptics · minimise the dose and the number of therapeutics · consider switching to an alternative medication · counsel appropriately as to potential teratogenesis
    Antenatal Potential dose increases
    Post-partum Potential dose adjustments · breastfeeding considerations · restarting medications · switching back to previous therapeutics
    The Antenatal Row Is Counterintuitive — Doses Often Go Up

    The instinct in pregnancy is to reduce drug exposure. But pregnancy changes pharmacokinetics substantially — plasma volume expands, renal clearance rises, and protein binding shifts — so a dose that was therapeutic before conception may become subtherapeutic. For conditions where loss of control is itself dangerous to the fetus (epilepsy being the obvious one), the dose is increased rather than cut. Then it is adjusted back down postpartum. The physiology behind this is Thursday's afternoon session on Physiological Changes in Pregnancy.
