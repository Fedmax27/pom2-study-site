    Hypertension in Pregnancy

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Hypertension: ≥ 140 systolic or ≥ 90 diastolic on two readings 15 minutes apart. Severe: ≥ 160 or ≥ 110 — treat urgently to bring it under 160/110, because the maternal fear is stroke.
    • 20 weeks is the dividing line. Before it = chronic (pre-existing) hypertension; after it, with normal BPs before = gestational. BP normally falls from 8–12 weeks and is back to baseline around 20–24, so early high BP was almost certainly there before pregnancy.
    • Preeclampsia = new hypertension plus proteinuria (or organ or placental dysfunction). Classically late, in first pregnancies. It is placental: delivery is the only cure. Eclampsia = seizures. HELLP can occur with a normal BP.
    • Oral first line: labetalol, nifedipine XL, methyldopa. Severe: IV labetalol, short-acting nifedipine, IV hydralazine. Seizure prophylaxis = magnesium sulphate (4 g load, then 1–2 g/h into the postpartum period).
    • ACE inhibitors and ARBs are out — toxic to fetal kidneys, so switch them before conception. Diuretics are a poor choice (they fight the physiology), not a dangerous one.

    ≥ or >, and What “Preeclampsia” Requires Now

    The slides write “>140 / >90”. The international definition is ≥ 140 and/or ≥ 90, so a reading of exactly 140/90 counts. The lecture teaches preeclampsia as hypertension + proteinuria, the classic definition. Current guidance (ISSHP 2021) is broader: new hypertension at ≥ 20 weeks with proteinuria, or other maternal organ dysfunction (neurological, pulmonary edema, low platelets or hemolysis, AKI, raised transaminases), or uteroplacental dysfunction (abruption, fetal growth restriction, abnormal Doppler, fetal death). Proteinuria is no longer mandatory. That lines up with the lecture's own “adverse conditions” list below.

    Why 20 Weeks — BP Across a Normal Pregnancy

    The Dip That Makes the Rule

    Pregnancy drops systemic vascular resistance and expands vascular capacity to hold the rising blood volume. Resistance falls faster than plasma and red-cell volume can fill it, so BP falls from about 8–12 weeks and comes back to pre-pregnancy levels around 20–24 weeks (by 28 at the latest). So a high BP before 20 weeks is appearing despite the normal dip. It was almost certainly there before she was pregnant, and is filed as chronic. (The module's knowledge check: gestational hypertension is the one diagnosed at 30 weeks.)

    In preeclampsia the normal relaxation fails: the vessels don't dilate, and there is an exaggerated vasoconstrictor response to vasoactive substances in end organs. The trigger is unknown but clearly comes from the placenta, since delivering the placenta is the only cure. After delivery a transient postpartum worsening can occur, presumably as the last of the placental factor is released, which is why magnesium runs on after birth.

    Complications

    Complications are maternal, fetal or placental (they overlap). How likely they are depends on pre-existing vs pregnancy-related disease, severity and comorbidities, and they are usually what decides when to deliver. The primary maternal worry is stroke: bring severe BP below 160/110 urgently, and investigate any neurological symptom for an intracranial event.

    Management

    Treatment depends on the category, severity, gestational age, comorbidities, and fetal and maternal status. The key idea: in chronic or gestational hypertension, the BP is the disease, so control it to protect the placenta and prevent progression. In preeclampsia, eclampsia and HELLP, the BP is a symptom. Treating it reduces maternal and fetal harm, but it doesn't treat the disease. Only delivery does.

    A Correction to the Module's Own Feedback

    The knowledge-check answer calls methyldopa both an “alpha blocker” and a “diuretic”. It is neither. Methyldopa is a centrally acting α2-agonist. Hydrochlorothiazide is the diuretic in that question, and labetalol is the combined α/β-blocker. The answer itself is right: the drug that is contraindicated in pregnancy is the ACE inhibitor.
