    OB Care Providers

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Three providers, one low-risk pregnancy. Family medicine OB (6–12 months extra training), midwives (4-year direct-entry degree), obstetricians (5-year residency; +2 years for MFM).
    • What separates them is the postpartum period, not the delivery. Family docs and midwives carry care to 6 weeks and look after the baby; the obstetrician hands off the baby at its first breath and often sees the patient only at the 6-week visit.
    • Midwives use informed choice, not informed consent — which means clients sometimes choose outside the usual recommendation, and that is within the model.
    • Obstetricians have no lactation training. Dr. Sovran says so outright about herself. Breastfeeding support comes from midwives and family physicians.
    • Appointment length is a real clinical difference: midwives get 30–90 minutes, can see clients at home, and visit three times in the first week postpartum.
    • Asking for help is not a failure. The stated goal of the whole module.

    The Family Doctor's Distinctive Advantage

    Continuity, and an understanding of the whole patient. A family medicine OB brings their entire primary-care knowledge base into antenatal, intrapartum and postpartum care — so they are better at recognising and treating depression, upper respiratory tract infections, ear infections and rashes as they arise in pregnancy and the newborn period. Nobody else in this table brings that.

    Midwifery Regulation Is Not Uniform Across Canada

    Midwifery is regulated everywhere except PEI and the Yukon — but funding and what the regulation covers vary province to province, and scope varies further by practice location and the individual midwife's training. Globally, midwives are recognised as essential to promoting maternal health and reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. More at the Canadian Association of Midwives; the Ontario scope document is the College of Midwives of Ontario consultation and transfer-of-care standard.

    When to Consult an Obstetrician

    Dr. Sovran is explicit that this is not an exhaustive list — it is meant to give a sense of the threshold.

    PhaseReasons to consult
    Antepartum Medically complicated patient — diabetes, essential hypertension, prior preeclampsia, elevated BMI, SLE and other medical conditions · fetal anomalies (to MFM) · atypical or abnormal antepartum fetal surveillance (e.g. abnormal BPP) · need for a planned caesarean section
    Intrapartum Fetal anomalies (MFM) · atypical or abnormal fetal surveillance · labour dystocia, failure to descend, assessment and rotation of fetal position · consideration of operative vaginal delivery or caesarean section · preeclampsia or eclampsia
    Postpartum Significant postpartum haemorrhage · third- or fourth-degree tear · preeclampsia developing postpartum

    Not every consult is a handover. Sometimes the obstetrical team will provide co-care through the pregnancy and delivery rather than a complete transfer — though preeclampsia usually does become a transfer of care.

    Preconception Counselling Is Underused

    MFM is involved in counselling medically complex patients to optimise health before conception — and Dr. Sovran's own view is that it is probably a very underutilised service. There is a lot of preventive medicine available. The difficulty is simply that pregnancy happens when it happens, not at the medically optimal moment.

    The Maternal Health Team

    We must work together as a team
    Communication is key — updating the board, or telling an obstetrician there is a potential concern, is what keeps everyone on the same page
    The goal is patient-centred, supportive care, which will always lead to the best outcomes
    We all have our own strengths and skill sets, and recognising and respecting that matters
    Asking for help is not a failure
    Asking Happens at Every Level — Including Between Consultants

    At LHSC there are two obstetricians and gynecologists on call every night, and Dr. Sovran notes it is not uncommon for them to ask each other for help. It happens with nursing staff too — shoulder dystocia or postpartum haemorrhage needs extra hands, immediately. The point of the module is that the integrated team is the unit of care, and all three providers share the same goal.
