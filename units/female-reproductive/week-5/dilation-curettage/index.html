    Dilation & Curettage

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • D&C = vacuum aspiration at ≤ 14+0 weeks, with a rigid plastic cannula on an electric suction machine. Done for first-trimester pregnancy loss and for therapeutic abortion.
    • Dilate to roughly the number of weeks, in millimetres — 8 weeks → 8 mm.
    • Cervical preparation makes it safer: chemical (misoprostol) or osmotic (laminaria, Dilapan). Not needed if the cervix is already open — incomplete or inevitable abortion.
    • The three most common complications are infection, retained products and hemorrhage.
    • Antibiotic prophylaxis is for therapeutic abortion, not for a missed abortion — the difference is chlamydia prevalence in the two populations, not the procedure.
    • Sharp curettage is what causes Asherman's. Use the suction curette alone where you can.
    • Post-abortal syndrome = pain with no bleeding. Blood is accumulating inside the uterus — re-evacuate immediately.

    What the Procedure Is
    DefinitionVacuum aspiration of a pregnancy ≤ 14 weeks and 0 days gestation
    HowAspiration of uterine contents with a rigid plastic cannula attached to an electric suction machine
    AnesthesiaLocal, with a paracervical block, with or without conscious sedation — or a general anesthetic, depending on the resources at your centre
    IndicationsFirst-trimester pregnancy loss — incomplete, inevitable or missed abortion · therapeutic abortion
    What It Is Not For

    The module's own knowledge check asks which conditions a D&C can treat. Ectopic pregnancy is not one of them — the pregnancy is not in the uterus, so evacuating the uterus achieves nothing (see 4.9). Nor is endometritis, which is treated with antibiotics. And beyond 14+0 weeks the procedure is a D&E, not a D&C.

    Cervical Preparation

    Peri-procedural cervical dilatation is usually required — but not for an incomplete or inevitable abortion, where the cervix is already open.

    MethodAgentTiming
    ChemicalMisoprostol — oral, buccal (inside the cheek) or vaginal1–12 hours before the procedure
    Osmotic / mechanicalLaminaria (a dried kelp / seaweed stick) or synthetic Dilapan, placed directly into the internal os~24 hours before the procedure

    The Procedure, Step by Step

    StepDetail — and why
    1. Bimanual examinationConfirms gestational age and uterine size, and whether the uterus is anteverted or retroverted — which is how you reduce the risk of perforation
    2. Paracervical blockOften lidocaine, sometimes with vasopressin to reduce blood loss, sometimes with bicarbonate to reduce the sting of the local
    3. Mechanical dilatationNot always needed if chemical or osmotic preparation was adequate. Avoiding it reduces discomfort and infection rates, because every instrument pass carries vaginal flora into a sterile cavity
    4. Suction curettagePlastic suction curette attached to the electric suction machine. Sharp curettage with a metal curette is unnecessary in most cases

    Complications

    ComplicationWhat it isManagement
    Cervical shockA vasovagal reaction as the paracervical block is placed. Lasts seconds to minutes. Only seen in a patient who is awake — not under general anestheticSelf-limited; supportive care
    Uterine perforation1 in 300 to 1 in 1000, depending on the operator's expertise. A hole in the uterus, most commonly at the fundus. Can occur during dilatation or during suctionComplete the procedure under ultrasound or laparoscopic guidance. A fundal perforation away from other organs usually needs only observation
    Visceral injuryRare. Bowel, bladder or major vessel — especially with suction or sharp curettage through a perforationMay require major surgery, possibly laparotomy, to repair
    InfectionAs for any surgical procedureAntibiotic prophylaxis for therapeutic abortion, but not for D&C for missed abortion or pregnancy loss. Practice varies
    Cervical traumaLacerations from the tenaculum (the instrument holding the cervix still for counter-traction) or from mechanical dilatorsGenerally minor — repaired with absorbable suture
    HemorrhageMore likely with larger gestational age or abnormal placentation (partial molar or molar pregnancy). May indicate uterine atony or a low implantation siteRepeat curettage may be necessary. Note general anesthesia is itself a risk factor, because it relaxes the uterus
    Hematometra (post-abortal syndrome)Rare but serious. Blood accumulates in the uterine cavity after the procedure. Presents with acute pain, sometimes urinary retention, and no vaginal bleedingImmediate re-evacuation of the uterus
    Retained products of conception< 1%. Usually small pieces of placenta or membrane. Presents with signs of infection and/or increased or prolonged bleedingSecondary D&C, or medical management with misoprostol to make the uterus contract and expel them
    Asherman's syndromeUncommon, but the one that matters to a patient who still wants fertility. Adhesions form within the endometrial cavity, sometimes causing secondary amenorrhea and infertilityRisk factors: sharp uterine curettage (8%) and repeat curettage — most often for retained products or retained placenta postpartum. Risk rises up to 30% by a third consecutive procedure within 24 hours. The role of infection is controversial
    Pain With No Bleeding Is the Red Flag

    Every other post-procedure complication announces itself with bleeding. Hematometra is the one that presents the opposite way — severe cramping pain, possibly urinary retention, and a dry pad, because the blood is trapped behind a closed cervix. It is easy to mistake for normal post-operative pain if you are waiting for bleeding as your cue.

    Why Antibiotics for One Indication and Not the Other

    The procedure is identical, so the difference is not surgical. It is epidemiological: rates of chlamydia are higher in the population presenting for therapeutic abortion than in the population presenting with a missed abortion, so the expected benefit of prophylaxis differs. (Chlamydia and its sequelae are in 4.6.)

    The most common complications after a D&C are infection, retained products and hemorrhage.
