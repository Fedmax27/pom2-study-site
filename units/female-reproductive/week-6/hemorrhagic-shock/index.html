    Hemorrhagic Shock

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Shock = cellular and tissue hypoxia. In hemorrhagic (hypovolemic) shock, lost volume means less preload, and less preload means less cardiac output.
    • Signs: tachycardia, tachypnea, oliguria, altered mental status, cool clammy skin, metabolic acidosis, rising lactate. Hypotension comes late.
    • Young, healthy and especially postpartum women compensate. They can lose a startling amount of blood and still look well.
    • ABCs and call for HELP → vitals → 2 large-bore IVs → fluids → O2 → Foley to urometer → CBC, group & screen, INR/PTT, fibrinogen, creatinine → blood products or the massive transfusion protocol → find and fix the cause → ICU if she needs pressors.
    • Prevent the lethal triad: acidosis, hypothermia (< 35 °C), coagulopathy. Each one makes the other two worse.

    What Shock Is

    Shock is a state of cellular and tissue hypoxia from reduced oxygen delivery, increased oxygen consumption, or inadequate oxygen use.

    Hypovolemic shock is due to reduced intravascular volume. That means reduced preload, and so reduced cardiac output. It is either hemorrhagic or non-hemorrhagic (e.g. fluid losses). This lecture is about hemorrhagic shock.

    Feature of shockWhy it happens
    TachycardiaThe first compensation: keep cardiac output up as stroke volume falls
    HypotensionCompensation has failed. A late sign.
    OliguriaThe kidneys are underperfused, so a Foley catheter is the running score
    Abnormal mental statusThe brain is underperfused: anxious, then confused, then lethargic
    TachypneaBreathing off the acid
    Cool, clammy, cyanotic skinSympathetic vasoconstriction diverts blood to the core
    Metabolic acidosis, hyperlactatemiaHypoxic tissues switch to anaerobic metabolism

    Why Obstetric Patients Fool You

    They Look Well Until They Don't

    Obstetric and gynecologic patients are mostly young and healthy, and they compensate well despite large losses. This is especially true right after delivery. Blood volume expands in pregnancy precisely so she can afford the blood loss of birth. A normal blood pressure does not mean a small bleed. Don't overlook the young, healthy patient: she may have lost far more than she looks. Watch the pulse, the breathing, the urine and her mind.

    Management

    You can't do this alone. Nursing, anesthesia and often the ICU or internal medicine: a team approach, in ABC order.

    StepDetail

    Prevent the Lethal Triad

    ArmWhy it's lethalWhat to do
    1. AcidosisClotting enzymes work badly in acid, especially the calcium-dependent complexes. At pH 7.0, recombinant factor VIIa loses about 90% of its activity.Maintain tissue perfusion: oxygen, IV fluids, red cell transfusion
    2. HypothermiaBelow 35 °C, clotting drops off significantlyWarmed IV fluids, a Bair Hugger (forced warm-air blanket), a Level 1 warmer (rapid fluid warmer)
    3. CoagulopathyMostly dilution by IV fluid and transfused red cells. It worsens progressively at 10, 20, 30 units.Limit the blood loss (fix the cause) and give the right products, not just crystalloid and red cells
    Why It's a Triad

    Each arm feeds the others. Bleeding causes hypoperfusion, and hypoperfusion causes acidosis. Cold fluids and an open abdomen cause hypothermia. Acidosis and cold both cripple the clotting cascade, and poor clotting means more bleeding. Crystalloid and red cells alone dilute whatever clotting factors are left. So the resuscitation has to warm, perfuse and replace factors at the same time, and the source has to be controlled.

    The Case: Jenna

    The module asks you to commit to an answer before revealing each step. Try it here too: read each box, decide, then read on.

    In the emergency department

    Jenna, 21, brought in by ambulance after a syncopal episode. Right lower quadrant pain all day, getting worse. Conscious but sleepy and pale; rousable.

    PMHx healthy · PSHx laparoscopic appendectomy at 18 · Meds levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena) · Social smoker, 2 pack-years; no alcohol or drugs · Gyne menarche at 12; periods irregular since the IUD went in 2 years ago; one male partner, no condoms.

    Vitals: HR 110, BP 90/50, RR 15, SpO2 98% RA · Bloods: Hb 90, WBC 7, platelets 220, β-hCG pending, blood group A negative · Exam: pale, 56 kg; abdomen mildly distended, tender everywhere, worst in the RLQ; rebound and peritoneal signs; cervical motion tenderness.

    Leading diagnosis?

    Ruptured ectopic pregnancy. A woman of reproductive age with pain, syncope, peritonism and falling hemoglobin has an ectopic until proven otherwise. An IUD with a positive pregnancy test is an ectopic until proven otherwise, and smoking is a risk factor (4.9). The appendix is already out.

    What do you order?

    Abdominal/pelvic ultrasound: blood pooled by the liver and in the pelvis; normal ovaries with a probable right corpus luteum cyst; a 4 cm right adnexal mass with a fetal heartbeat. Plan: consult gynecology, NPO, IV normal saline.

    Then her vitals change: BP 70/40, HR 135. She can't stand without feeling faint. What do you do?

    NowJenna's version
    A, BAlert, no difficulty breathing, SpO2 95% on room air, so no oxygen needed yet
    CTwo large-bore IVs; fluid bolus (NS or Ringer's lactate); Foley for urine output
    HelpUrgent gynecology consult: update them that she is now unstable
    BloodsCBC, lytes, BUN, creatinine, PTT, INR, fibrinogen, group and screen and crossmatch; repeat whenever her status changes

    Resolution

    Gynecology comes quickly and consents her for a laparoscopic salpingectomy. She is booked as an “A” case and is in the OR within 2 hours. Anesthesia follows her bloodwork. Her hemoglobin falls to 50 and she receives 2 units of packed red cells. About 2 L of blood is evacuated from her abdomen.

    Reading Jenna Against the Table

    At arrival (HR 110, BP 90/50, anxious and sleepy) she already looks like class II–III. By HR 135 and BP 70/40 she is class III or worse. The 2 L in her abdomen is roughly half her circulating volume: blood volume is roughly 70 mL/kg in adults (the table's 750 mL ≈ 15% implies the same figure), which gives about 4 L at 56 kg. That arithmetic is added here. It shows the lecture's point: a 21-year-old can lose half her blood and still be “alert, breathing fine, 95% on room air”. Resuscitation bought the time; salpingectomy fixed the cause. She is also Rh negative with a pregnancy, so she needs Rh immunoglobulin (4.9). The case doesn't mention it.
