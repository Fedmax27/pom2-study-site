    Osteoporosis: Epidemiology, Assessment & the Treatment Gap

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Three ways in to the diagnosis, any one is enough: a fragility fracture (excluding skull, hands, feet, ankles, and anything with real trauma), a T-score < −2.5, or a 10-year fracture risk > 20%.
    • T-score compares you to a young adult — it makes the diagnosis. Z-score compares you to your own age group — it flags secondary causes.
    • The treatment gap is the whole point: only 15% are treated after a fragility fracture, against 80% who get a beta blocker after a heart attack. A fracture is to osteoporosis what an MI is to cardiovascular disease — we just don't act like it.
    • 29% are dead one year after a hip fracture. This is not a quality-of-life disease.
    • Start with a bisphosphonate. Go anabolic first only for severe or multiple vertebral fractures with a T-score ≤ −2.5 — and always follow anabolic therapy with an antiresorptive, or the gains are lost.

    Learning Objective

    • Explain the epidemiology of osteoporosis, including the treatment care gap.

    The assessment and treatment sections below go beyond that single objective, following the structure of the lecture notes. See the source line at the bottom for which parts came from the slides and which from the Canadian guideline.

    Definition: Three Doors In

    Osteoporosis is not one number. Any one of these establishes the diagnosis:

    RouteCriterionThe catch
    ClinicalPresence of a fragility fractureExcludes fractures of the skull, hands, feet and ankles, and any fracture with significant trauma. A hip fracture from a standing-height fall counts; a skull fracture does not.
    DensitometricBMD T-score < −2.5A number, not a symptom — and most people who fracture are not below this line.
    Risk-based> 20% 10-year fracture riskApplies in the absence of both a fragility fracture and a T-score < −2.5 — the third door for people the first two miss.
    Why Three Definitions Rather Than One

    Each catches a different failure of the others. A T-score alone misses the majority of people who actually fracture, because bone quality and fall risk are not measured by densitometry. A fracture alone is a diagnosis made too late. The risk-based door exists to identify the person who has not yet fractured and whose density is not yet dire, but whose combined risk factors already put them where treatment helps.

    The gap has been widening. Treatment rates were higher a decade ago; as bisphosphonates fell out of favour — driven largely by fear of the rare harms covered below — prescribing declined without the risk of fracture declining with it.

    What undertreatment costs

    Note the 1-year and 5-year hip figures are not a contradiction — they come from different cohorts and different eras. The point they share is that hip fracture mortality is measured in tens of percent, not fractions of one.

    Clinical Presentation

    It is a silent disease. Most patients have no symptoms until they fracture — which is exactly why case-finding depends on risk factors and densitometry rather than on how the patient feels.

    • Vertebral fractures are graded by the loss of vertebral body height. Moderate and severe grades are the ones that feed prediction models — and many are found incidentally, never having caused a recognised episode of back pain.
    • Accumulated vertebral fractures produce the kyphotic spine — the visible end-stage of a disease that was silent throughout.

    Physical signs worth looking for

    SignThreshold
    Prospective height loss (measured over time)> 2 cm
    Historical height loss (versus recalled peak height)> 6 cm
    Rib-to-pelvis distance< 2 finger-breadths
    Occiput-to-wall distance> 5 cm

    All four are surrogates for the same thing — vertebral height that has already been lost. Falls risk assessment belongs in the same examination.

    Assessment: T-Score, Z-Score and Who to Scan

    T vs Z — Different Comparators, Different Jobs

    T-score compares the patient's bone density to a healthy young adult at peak bone mass. That is the comparison the diagnostic threshold of −2.5 is built on, because the question it answers is "how much bone have you lost from your best?"

    Z-score compares them to people of their own age and sex. Losing bone with age is expected, so a normal Z-score is unremarkable — but a markedly low Z-score means the patient is losing bone faster than their peers, which points at a secondary cause rather than ordinary ageing. That is the score that should prompt you to go looking.

    Who gets a BMD scan

    AgeIndication
    ≥ 70Screen — no risk factors required
    65–69With 1 risk factor
    50–64With a prior fracture or ≥ 2 risk factors

    The risk factors: previous fragility fracture after age 40 · glucocorticoids (> 3 months in the last year at prednisone > 5 mg/day) · ≥ 2 falls in the last year · parent who fractured a hip · BMI < 20 kg/m2 · a condition causing secondary osteoporosis · current smoking · alcohol ≥ 3 drinks/day.

    Blood work

    A short, targeted panel — aimed at excluding secondary causes and at making sure the treatment you are about to give is safe:

    TestWhy
    Calcium, corrected for albuminScreens for hyperparathyroidism and malignancy — and hypocalcemia must be corrected before starting an antiresorptive. See corrected calcium.
    CBCAnemia or cytopenias point toward myeloma and other marrow disease
    CreatinineRenal function constrains drug choice — bisphosphonates are contraindicated at low clearance
    Alkaline phosphataseRaised in Paget's disease, osteomalacia, and bone metastases
    TSHThyrotoxicosis causes bone loss — see subclinical hyperthyroidism, which is explicitly associated with it
    25-OH vitamin DDeficiency must be corrected alongside any pharmacotherapy

    Either way, the number that matters downstream is the 10-year major osteoporotic fracture risk, and the intervention threshold is 20%.

    Treatment 1: Lifestyle Comes First

    This applies to everyone, treated or not.

    Recommendation
    ExerciseBalance, functional training and progressive resistance training, more than twice weekly — to reduce falls and maintain physical function and bone strength. Weight-bearing is the operative principle.
    Falls preventionA formal falls-risk assessment. Most fractures require a fall; removing the fall removes the fracture.
    CalciumMeet the RDA from food where possible — women > 50 and men > 70: 1200 mg/day; men 50–70: 1000 mg/day. If dietary intake is adequate, supplementation is not recommended to prevent fractures.
    Vitamin DRDA 600 IU/day (51–70) and 800 IU/day (> 70). Because that is hard to reach from diet, a minimum 400 IU daily supplement is suggested alongside vitamin D-rich foods.
    The Nuance People Get Wrong

    Calcium and vitamin D are not a treatment for osteoporosis — they are the nutritional floor that the actual drugs are built on. Supplementing calcium in someone already meeting the RDA through diet does not prevent fractures, and the trials that established every drug below gave their participants a minimum of roughly 400 IU of vitamin D and up to 1000 mg of calcium. That is the context in which those drugs work, not a substitute for them.

    The Two Harms That Created the Gap

    These are the adverse effects that made prescribing fall — worth knowing precisely, because the fear of them is out of proportion to their frequency.

    Osteonecrosis of the jawAtypical femoral fracture
    Caused byBisphosphonates and denosumabBisphosphonates and denosumab
    WhereJaw — typically after dental extraction or with poor dental healthSubtrochanteric — below the lesser trochanter, in the femoral shaft
    Warning signExposed bone, non-healing dental socketNew thigh or groin pain, often prodromal and often bilateral
    FrequencyRare — and the reason the drug holiday exists, since risk rises with cumulative duration
    Keeping the Risk in Proportion

    Both harms are real, both are rare, and both scale with how long treatment continues — which is precisely why the guideline builds in a defined treatment period and a drug holiday rather than treating indefinitely.

    Set against them: a 29% one-year mortality after hip fracture, and a 12.1% lifetime hip fracture risk over the age of 50. The treatment gap exists because the rare, nameable, drug-attributable harm feels more salient than the common, diffuse, disease-attributable one. Recognising that asymmetry is the actual clinical skill this lecture is teaching.
