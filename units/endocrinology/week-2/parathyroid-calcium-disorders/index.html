    Hyperparathyroidism, Hypoparathyroidism & Disorders of Calcium

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Corrected Ca (mmol/L) = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 − albumin [g/L]). Hypercalcemia = corrected Ca > 2.55; treat acute hypercalcemia (if Corr Ca > 3) and severe hypocalcemia (if Corr Ca < 1.9 with symptoms) as admissions.
    • Split hypercalcemia into PTH-mediated (primary/tertiary hyperPTH, FHH) vs. PTH-independent (malignancy, vitamin D excess, and a long minor list) — >90% of cases are primary hyperPTH or malignancy.
    • Fractional excretion of calcium separates the two PTH-mediated look-alikes: FeCa < 0.01 → FHH (kidney "thinks" calcium is normal, keeps reabsorbing it); FeCa > 0.02 → primary hyperPTH (kidney senses the excess correctly, more spills into urine).
    • Split hypocalcemia by phosphate: PTH-deficient or PTH-resistant → high phosphate (PTH normally wastes phosphate renally); vitamin D-deficient → low phosphate (vitamin D drives absorption of both, and 2° hyperPTH from the low calcium then wastes what phosphate there is).
    • Pseudohypoparathyroidism 1a (PTH-resistant, maternal GNAS mutation) and pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism (same Albright's phenotype, paternal mutation, hormones work fine) look alike but aren't — imprinting decides which genes actually get expressed in the kidney.

    • Describe the clinical presentation of primary hyperparathyroidism and primary hypoparathyroidism.
    • Explain the differential diagnosis of hypercalcemia and the etiologies of hyperparathyroidism.
    • Explain the differential diagnosis of hypocalcemia and the etiologies of hypoparathyroidism.
    • Describe the acute and chronic treatment of hypercalcemia and hypocalcemia, accounting for the underlying etiology.

    Calcium Homeostasis: The Numbers

    The full loop — parathyroid, bone, kidney, gut and vitamin D — is covered in Drugs Affecting Vitamin D & Calcium Homeostasis: falling plasma Ca++ triggers PTH, which pulls calcium from bone, reclaims it in the kidney, and (via vitamin D, made from sunlight or diet) absorbs more from the gut; rising calcium then shuts PTH off. What that lecture skips is the numbers.

    Serum calcium exists in three forms, and only one of them is biologically active:

    Corrected Calcium

    A lab reports total calcium — ionized plus protein-bound plus complexed. Since roughly 40% rides on albumin, a low albumin drags the total down while the active ionized fraction is unchanged; a high albumin does the reverse. Correcting avoids calling a hypoalbuminemic patient hypocalcemic, or missing real hypercalcemia.

    The Formula

    Corrected Ca (mmol/L) = Measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 − Albumin [g/L])

    A change in albumin of 1 g/L shifts measured calcium by 0.02 mmol/L, in the opposite direction — so the formula adds back what albumin took away (or subtracts what it artificially added).

    Worked example: Measured Ca 2.6 mmol/L, albumin 20 g/L. Corrected Ca = 2.6 + 0.02 × (40 − 20) = 2.6 + 0.4 = 3.0 mmol/L — a value that looked borderline is actually significant hypercalcemia once the low albumin is accounted for.

    When the true value matters and albumin is markedly abnormal, an ionized calcium measured directly is more reliable than any correction formula.

    Hypercalcemia: Definition & Presentation

    • Hypercalcemia = corrected total calcium > 2.55 mmol/L (or an elevated ionized calcium).
    • Symptoms depend on both the absolute value and the rate of rise — a slowly-developed hypercalcemia can be markedly elevated and still nearly asymptomatic, while a rapid rise to a similar level can be florid. Mild hypercalcemia is commonly asymptomatic and picked up incidentally.

    The classic mnemonic is "stones, bones, groans, and psychiatric overtones" — each with a mechanism worth knowing, not just memorizing:

    SystemFindingsWhy
    Renal ("stones")Nephrolithiasis, polyuria (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus), acute/chronic renal insufficiencyExcess filtered calcium supersaturates urine with calcium salts; calcium also activates the calcium-sensing receptorA G-protein-coupled receptor that lets tissues directly sense extracellular calcium — expressed in the parathyroid and, importantly, along the kidney tubule in the collecting duct and thick ascending limb, which blunts the response to ADH and impairs the countercurrent gradient — the kidney literally can't concentrate urine as well.
    Skeletal ("bones")Bone pain/tenderness, cystic bone lesions, skeletal demineralization, spontaneous fracturesDirect consequence of the resorption driving the hypercalcemia in the first place — PTH or PTH-like signaling pulling calcium out of bone faster than it's replaced.
    GI ("groans")Abdominal pain, pancreatitis (acute/chronic), constipation, vomiting, anorexiaCalcium reduces smooth muscle excitability (constipation); high intraductal/intracellular calcium can trigger premature intra-pancreatic trypsinogen activation, precipitating pancreatitis.
    CardiovascularShort QT interval, arrhythmias, hypertensionExtracellular calcium speeds the plateau (phase 2) of the cardiac action potential — shortens the ST segment specifically, which shortens the QT.
    Neuro/psychiatric ("psychiatric overtones")Confusion, personality change, depression, apathyHigh extracellular calcium raises the threshold for neuronal depolarization, generally reducing excitability — the opposite direction from hypocalcemia's tetany.

    Etiology: PTH-Mediated vs. PTH-Independent

    The cleanest way to organize the differential is by whether PTH is driving it — and a single PTH level splits the whole list in two:

    PTH-mediatedPTH-independent
    Primary hyperparathyroidismHypercalcemia of malignancy
    Tertiary hyperparathyroidism (chronic renal failure)Vitamin D intoxication / chronic granulomatous disease
    Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)Medications; hyperthyroidism, acromegaly, pheochromocytoma, adrenal insufficiency, immobilization, parenteral nutrition, milk-alkali syndrome

    Primary hyperparathyroidism and malignancy account for >90% of cases. Malignant hypercalcemia works two ways: bony metastases releasing calcium locally (e.g. breast), or a humoral factor secreted systemically — PTHrP (lung, kidney, adult T-cell leukemia; acts on the PTH receptor), lymphokines (myeloma), prostaglandins, or vitamin D-like steroids (lymphoma).

    Minor causes (<10%): exogenous vitamin D; granulomatous disease (sarcoid, TB — activated macrophages make their own 1α-hydroxylase, producing calcitriol outside PTH control); immobilization with high bone turnover (Paget's, thyrotoxicosis); FHH (inactivating calcium-sensing receptor mutation); milk-alkali syndrome; and thiazides (reduced renal calcium excretion, usually transient).

    Within primary hyperparathyroidism specifically: parathyroid adenoma (80%), parathyroid hyperplasia, parathyroid carcinoma (rare), and familial syndromes (familial isolated hyperparathyroidism, hyperparathyroidism-jaw tumour syndrome, MEN syndromes).

    Telling Primary HyperPTH and FHH Apart

    Case — 65F, Osteoporosis Work-Up

    Back pain, radius/ulna fracture at 52, ulna fracture at 61, hysterectomy at 35. Thyroid and renal function normal. Initial Ca 2.95 mmol/L, albumin 45 g/L. Repeat Ca 2.73 mmol/L, phosphate 0.69 mmol/L, PTH 14 pmol/L (ref. 1.6–6.9), 25-OH vitamin D 70 nmol/L.

    Correcting for her high-normal albumin: 2.73 + 0.02 × (40 − 45) = 2.63 mmol/L — still hypercalcemic, and a reminder the correction runs both directions. Elevated PTH, low-normal phosphate (PTH wastes phosphate), sufficient vitamin D: textbook primary hyperparathyroidism.

    Both primary hyperPTH and FHH present with hypercalcemia and a PTH that's elevated or inappropriately normal — the fractional excretion of calcium (FeCa), from a 24-hour urine collection, is what separates them:

    Fractional Excretion of Calcium

    FeCa = (Urine Ca × Serum Cr / 1000) / (Serum Ca × Urine Cr) — from a 24-hour urine collection. It answers one question: given how high the calcium is, is the kidney letting a normal amount spill into urine, or is it hanging onto calcium it shouldn't?

    In FHH the mutated receptor needs a higher calcium before it registers as high, so the kidney keeps reabsorbing as though calcium were normal and little reaches the urine. In primary hyperparathyroidism the sensing is intact, so more calcium escapes despite PTH's reabsorptive pull.

    Treatment of Acute Hypercalcemia

    Inpatient admission is generally required if corrected Ca > 3 mmol/L.

    1
    Volume repletion — normal saline, 4–6 L deficit.

    Hypercalcemia itself causes volume depletion (the nephrogenic-DI-like polyuria above), which further impairs renal calcium clearance — rehydrating alone starts to help.

    2
    Forced diuresis — 4–10 L NS plus furosemide plus K⁺, only once well hydrated.

    Loop diuretics block the Na-K-2Cl transporter in the thick ascending limb, which (among other things) disrupts the lumen-positive gradient that normally drives passive calcium reabsorption there — more calcium is forced into urine. Never given before volume is repleted, or it just worsens the depletion.

    3
    IV bisphosphonate (zoledronate).

    Shuts down osteoclast-mediated bone resorption — the definitive fix for most causes, but takes 2–4 days to take full effect.

    4
    Calcitonin.

    Works within hours (inhibits osteoclasts and increases renal calcium excretion directly) but tachyphylaxis sets in within 48 hours — useful as a bridge while the slower bisphosphonate takes effect, not as monotherapy.

    5
    Prednisone 40–60 mg/day, then taper — for myeloma or vitamin D intoxication specifically.

    Glucocorticoids suppress the extra-renal 1α-hydroxylase activity that granulomatous tissue and some lymphomas use to overproduce calcitriol, and have direct cytotoxic/anti-cytokine effects in myeloma — this is a cause-specific step, not a general one.

    Treatment of Primary Hyperparathyroidism

    PresentationApproach
    Mild, asymptomatic, age > 50No treatment (monitor) or medical therapy — bisphosphonates, denosumab, estrogen, or cinacalcet (a calcimimetic that increases the CaSR's sensitivity to calcium, directly opposing the FHH mechanism above)
    Symptomatic, or meets a surgical indication belowParathyroidectomy

    General measures regardless of severity: adequate hydration, and avoid thiazides and lithium — both raise calcium further (thiazides reduce renal excretion as above; lithium alters the calcium-sensing receptor's set-point similarly to FHH, raising PTH).

    Indications for surgery: serum calcium > 0.25 mmol/L above the upper limit of normal · BMD T-score < −2.5 at the lumbar spine, femoral neck, total hip, or 1/3 radius · vertebral fracture · creatinine clearance < 60 mL/min · 24-hour urine calcium > 10 mmol/day with increased stone risk · nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis present · age < 50.

    Hypocalcemia: Etiology by Lab Pattern

    Hypocalcemia results from failure to secrete PTH, failure to respond to PTH, vitamin D deficiency, failure to respond to vitamin D, or (rarely) acute complexation of calcium. In practice, phosphate is what splits these into recognizable buckets, because PTH and vitamin D pull phosphate in opposite directions:

    Why Phosphate Splits This Way

    PTH wastes phosphate at the kidney (inhibiting proximal tubule reabsorption). Remove PTH (hypoparathyroidism) or make the kidney deaf to it (pseudohypoparathyroidism) and phosphate reabsorption goes unchecked — it climbs as calcium falls. Vitamin D deficiency is the mirror image: calcitriol absorbs both, so both fall, and the compensatory secondary PTH rise wastes what phosphate is left. Same low calcium, opposite phosphate.

    Vitamin D deficiency is more common than either PTH disorder — causes include malabsorption (it's fat-soluble), renal or liver failure (both metabolic steps live there — see the vitamin D lecture), certain drugs (phenytoin accelerates hepatic vitamin D catabolism), rare dietary deficiency, and inadequate sun exposure (classically causing rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults).

    Minor/other causes: hypoalbuminemia (falsely low total calcium — see corrected calcium above), hypomagnesemia (alcohol use, malabsorption), pancreatitis (calcium is saponified by fatty acids released from peripancreatic fat necrosis), tumor lysis/rhabdomyolysis/sepsis, and massive transfusion (citrate anticoagulant in banked blood chelates calcium).

    Pseudohypoparathyroidism

    Type 1a is true PTH resistance — the kidney has normal or high PTH but doesn't respond, so calcium stays low and phosphate stays high, just like hypoparathyroidism, except PTH itself is elevated (appropriately trying to compensate, unsuccessfully). It carries a recognizable phenotype called Albright's hereditary osteodystrophy: round facies, short stature, and short 4th/5th metacarpal and metatarsal bones.

    Pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism has the identical Albright's phenotype but no PTH insensitivity — calcium and phosphate are normal. The two are genetically related, and the distinction comes down to a single, high-yield genetics concept:

    Why the Same Mutation Gives Two Different Diseases

    Both come from an inactivating GNAS mutation — the gene encoding Gsα, the protein PTH's receptor signals through. GNAS is genomically imprintedOnly one parent's copy of certain genes is active in certain tissues — the other is epigenetically silenced, whether or not it is mutated in the renal tubule: only the maternal copy works there.

    • Maternal mutation → the kidney's only working copy is gone → PTH resistance → PHP 1a.
    • Paternal mutation → the kidney is unaffected → normal calcium, but the Albright's phenotype still appears in non-imprinted tissues → pseudopseudohypoparathyroidism.

    Because Gsα is shared machinery, PHP 1a often shows resistance to TSH and gonadotropins too.

    Diagnosing Primary Hypoparathyroidism

    • Diagnosis: persistent hypocalcemia with a low or inappropriately-normal PTH (it should be climbing in response to low calcium — a "normal" PTH here is actually a failure to respond appropriately).
    • After neck surgery, hypocalcemia must persist 6 months before it's labeled permanent hypoparathyroidism — many cases from parathyroid gland stunning/devascularization recover within that window.
    CauseShare
    Post-surgical (thyroid/parathyroid/neck surgery)~75% — the leading cause overall
    Non-surgical: autoimmune, infiltrative, post-radiation, functional (magnesium insufficiency or excess), metastatic, genetic~25% combined

    Symptoms of Hypocalcemia

    Low ionized calcium lowers the threshold for neuronal and muscle membrane depolarization — the opposite direction from hypercalcemia — which is why the clinical picture is one of hyperexcitability rather than lethargy.

    TimingFindings
    Acute, severeTetany — perioral paresthesias, carpopedal spasm, laryngeal spasm, seizures, palpitations
    Acute, mildChvostek's and Trousseau's signs (below)
    ChronicEmotional instability, anxiety, "brain fog," paresthesias, cataracts, dry skin, brittle nails
    OvertreatmentMovement disorders, nephrolithiasis (from iatrogenic hypercalciuria)

    Two bedside signs worth knowing precisely, since they're easy to blur together:

    • Chvostek's sign: tap the facial nerve just anterior to the ear, over the parotid gland. A positive test twitches the ipsilateral corner of the mouth (or more of the face). Sensitive but not very specific — present in a meaningful minority of normocalcemic people too.
    • Trousseau's sign: inflate a BP cuff on the upper arm above systolic pressure and hold for ~3 minutes. Ischemia unmasks neuromuscular hyperexcitability in a hypocalcemic patient, producing carpal spasm — wrist and MCP joints flexed, fingers extended, thumb drawn into the palm (the classic "obstetrician's hand"). More specific for hypocalcemia than Chvostek's.

    Treatment of Hypocalcemia

    Acute treatment is reserved for corrected Ca < 1.9 mmol/L with neurological symptoms (tetany, seizures, carpopedal spasm) or cardiac symptoms (prolonged QT — the mirror image of hypercalcemia's short QT, same phase-2 mechanism running in reverse).

    Acute Treatment

    Calcium gluconate: 1–2 g of 10% solution in 50 mL of 5% dextrose IV over 15–20 minutes, then 1–3 mg/kg/hr elemental calcium. Cardiac monitoring, inpatient. Gluconate rather than chloride peripherally: chloride is far more concentrated and causes tissue necrosis if it extravasates, so it is reserved for central access.

    Always check and correct magnesium first (or alongside). Magnesium is required for PTH to be released from the parathyroid and for PTH's receptor to signal properly at target tissue — hypocalcemia driven or worsened by hypomagnesemia will not correct with calcium alone until magnesium is repleted.

    Chronic TreatmentDetails
    Active vitamin DCalcitriol 0.25 mcg twice daily — monitor calcium and phosphate; target 25-OH-D > 75 nmol/L
    Calcium supplements1–2 g elemental calcium daily, in divided doses
    Thiazide diureticsReduce renal calcium excretion, lowering nephrolithiasis risk on replacement therapy
    Recombinant human PTH (rhPTH)Approved for hypoparathyroidism in the US and Europe
    Why Aim Low-Normal, Not Fully Normal

    Without PTH's phosphate-wasting, replacing calcium and active vitamin D drives both up together — and past a threshold calcium phosphate precipitates into soft tissue. Hence every part of the monitoring: keep calcium low-normal rather than fully normal, watch the calcium×phosphate product (< 4.4 mmol2/L2), and screen directly — renal ultrasound for nephrocalcinosis, head MRI for basal ganglia calcification.
