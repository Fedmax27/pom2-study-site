    Clinical Presentation & Evaluation of Thyroid Dysfunction

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • TSH is the case-finding test. A normal TSH effectively excludes primary thyroid dysfunction — the one exception is suspected central disease, where you must order FT4 as well because TSH is unreliable.
    • Thyrotoxicosis = too much thyroid hormone. Hyperthyroidism = thyrotoxicosis caused by the gland over-producing it. Not the same word, and the difference decides the treatment.
    • Split thyrotoxicosis by radioiodine uptake: high = increased synthesis (Graves, toxic nodule) → reduce production. Low = leakage or exogenous (thyroiditis) → supportive only, then watch for a hypothyroid phase.
    • Levothyroxine dosing is weight-based (~1.5 mcg/kg/day) with a 1-week half-life — so recheck TSH at 4–6 weeks after any change, never sooner, and expect the requirement to change if weight does.
    • Subclinical hypothyroidism (high TSH, normal FT4) has no proven treatment benefit outside pregnancy. If a therapeutic trial doesn't improve symptoms, stop it.

    Learning Objectives

    • Describe how and when to screen for and confirm the diagnosis of thyroid dysfunction, including the typical presentations of hyper- and hypothyroidism.
    • Explain the testing of thyroid function with TSH and free hormones.
    • Develop an initial investigation and management plan for a patient with thyrotoxicosis, including iodine's role in thyroid imaging.
    • Propose a treatment and monitoring plan for a patient with hypothyroidism, including the half-life and action of levothyroxine.

    Framing & Relevance

    Thyroid disease presents as a structural problem (benign or malignant, functional or non-functional) or a functional one (hyper- or hypothyroid) — the same fork used for any gland in the approach to endocrine disease. This lecture is the functional half.

    DefinitionPrevalenceWomen : Men
    HypothyroidismToo little thyroid hormoneUp to 2% (subclinical up to 10%)5–8 : 1
    ThyrotoxicosisToo much thyroid hormone, from any source——
    HyperthyroidismThyrotoxicosis specifically from thyroid hormone productionUp to 1.5%5 : 1

    The thyrotoxicosis/hyperthyroidism distinction isn't pedantry — a thyroiditis patient is thyrotoxic but not hyperthyroid, and giving them an antithyroid drug treats a production problem they don't have.

    Thyroid Function Tests

    TSH is the case-finding test. Order it when history, examination, an associated condition or high-risk status raises suspicion; if it is normal, you can generally stop. If it is abnormal, the free hormones tell you what kind of abnormality it is.

    PatternTSHFT4 / FT3Interpretation
    Normal↔↔Euthyroid
    Thyrotoxicosis↓↑Overt — confirm with repeat TSH plus FT4 and FT3
    Subclinical hyperthyroidism↓↔Same causes as overt; see below
    Primary hypothyroidism↑↓The gland has failed
    Subclinical hypothyroidism↑↔Usually TSH < 10–20; see below
    Central hypothyroidism↔ or ↓↓Pituitary/hypothalamic — TSH alone will miss it
    Central hyperthyroidism↔ or ↑↑Rare (e.g. TSH-secreting adenoma)
    The Trap in the Central Patterns

    In both central patterns the TSH is normal or moves in the "wrong" direction, because the problem is the pituitary that produces TSH rather than the gland responding to it. A patient with a known pituitary mass or other pituitary hormone deficiencies who develops fatigue, constipation and cold intolerance therefore needs TSH and FT4 — ordering TSH alone can return a reassuring number in a genuinely hypothyroid patient.

    The TSH-first pathway and its four common outcomes are also drawn as an algorithm in Thyroid & Parathyroid Physiology.

    The Diagnostic Pathway

    The same skeleton applies to both halves of this lecture, and the first step is not a test:

    1
    Is the patient stable? ABCs.

    Vital signs first. Thyroid disease rarely presents urgently, but when it does it is immediately dangerous — see the urgent presentations below.

    2
    History — symptoms, severity, risk factors.

    Use the clues. Severity and acuity narrow the differential as much as the symptoms themselves.

    3
    Physical exam — signs, severity, complications.

    Focused first, general if the picture is unclear.

    4
    Investigations, limited by what the history and exam actually found.

    Laboratory medicine, then imaging only where it changes management.

    Choosing Wisely framing worth carrying: order the least invasive test first, and only order a test that will change management — not for the learning experience, and not in anticipation of what a preceptor might want.

    Thyrotoxicosis: Presentation

    Urgent presentations (uncommon, but do not miss): thyroid storm / haemodynamic instability, high-output heart failure, visual loss from Graves' orbitopathy, and mental-health crisis exacerbated by thyrotoxicosis.

    SymptomsSigns
    Heat intolerance · increased sweating · tremor · palpitations · hyperactivity · insomnia · emotional lability and anxiety · weight loss despite increased appetite · hyperdefecation · heart failure (edema, orthopnea, PND) Eye signs · tremor · soft, warm skin · tachycardia · increased cardiac output · heart failure

    Confirmation: case-find with TSH (low suggests thyrotoxicosis), then confirm with a repeat low TSH plus elevated FT4 and FT3. Many labs add the free hormones reflexively when TSH is low.

    Thyrotoxicosis: Mechanism Decides Everything

    There are only three ways to become thyrotoxic — the gland makes too much, a damaged gland leaks what it already made, or the hormone comes from outside the body. Radioiodine uptake separates them, and the split drives the entire treatment plan:

    Eye signs: which are Graves', which are just thyrotoxicosis

    This distinction is commonly tested and easy to blur:

    • Any thyrotoxicosis (adrenergic, from any cause): stare (decreased blink), lid retraction, lid lag.
    • Graves' specifically (true orbitopathy): proptosis, periorbital edema, inflammation, diplopia, and visual loss — an ocular emergency. Also Graves'-specific: dermopathy (pretibial myxedema, peau d'orange) and thyroid acropachy (clubbing).

    Graves' orbitopathy is caused by increased volume of the retro-orbital connective tissue and extraocular muscles — inflammation, edema, glycosaminoglycan accumulation and increased adipocytes. The major modifiable risk factor is smoking.

    When to reach for a test

    TestValueCost / caveats
    Clinical assessment (pre-test probability of Graves')Demographics, family history of autoimmunity, acuity, severity, symmetric goiter, orbitopathy ± dermopathy — often enough on its ownFree

    In practice: if the clinical picture is classic Graves' (symmetric goiter, orbitopathy, family history), you may not need either test. When the picture is not classic — an asymmetric gland, no eye signs, no family history — the uptake scan is what tells you which mechanism you're dealing with.

    Subclinical Hyperthyroidism

    • Low TSH with normal FT4 and FT3; same causes as overt disease.
    • Rarely an artefact — assay interference can give an inaccurate TSH, so check for interfering substances and repeat TSH with free hormones.
    • Not benign: associated with bone loss and atrial fibrillation.
    • Treat if older and/or TSH is clearly suppressed. Monitor if younger and/or TSH is only borderline low.

    Before You Start an Antithyroid Drug

    Because the serious adverse effects are haematological, hepatic and teratogenic, get a baseline CBC and liver enzymes, and a pregnancy test in anyone who could be pregnant — both to interpret later abnormalities against a baseline and because pregnancy changes the drug choice.

    In pregnancy the agents swap by trimester: PTU in the first trimester, because methimazole in early pregnancy is associated with a characteristic embryopathy (aplasia cutis, choanal and esophageal atresia); then switch to methimazole for the remainder, because PTU carries the greater hepatotoxicity risk with longer use.

    Counsel every patient to report fever or sore throat urgently — that is how agranulocytosis announces itself.

    Hypothyroidism: Presentation

    Urgent presentation is myxedema coma — very rare, but characterised by obtundation and hypothermia, with hypotension, bradycardia, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia and hypoventilation.

    Symptoms (non-specific)Signs (not sensitive)
    Cold intolerance · weight gain despite decreased appetite · fatigue · shortness of breath · slow mentation · apathy · constipation Cool, pale skin · bradycardia · diastolic hypertension with a narrow pulse pressure · decreased cardiac output · slow relaxation phase of deep tendon reflexes

    Both lists are too non-specific to diagnose on clinically — which is exactly why the diagnosis rests on TSH rather than on how convincing the story sounds.

    When to check a TSH

    Because it's hard to diagnose clinically, the indications to test are broad: compatible symptoms, dyslipidemia, anemia, hyponatremia, personal or family history of autoimmune disease, susceptible populations (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome), previous thyroid disease or therapy, relevant drugs, and pregnancy. Add FT4 whenever pituitary or hypothalamic disease is suspected.

    Confirmation: a repeat elevated TSH with a low FT4.

    Hypothyroidism: Differential

    CategoryCauses
    Autoimmune destructionHashimoto's thyroiditis — by far the most common cause
    DrugsLithium, amiodarone
    Removal or destructionThyroid surgery, radioactive iodine, radiation
    Central (reduced pituitary/hypothalamic stimulation)Trauma, surgery, tumour, infiltrative disease

    Hashimoto's — risk factors are female sex, family history of autoimmune disease (especially thyroid) and iodine load. Symptoms are those of hypothyroidism generally: highly variable and non-specific. Signs are insensitive: goiter, bradycardia, diastolic hypertension, delayed reflex relaxation.

    Why Antibodies Rarely Help Here

    Anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are sensitive for Hashimoto's but less specific, and they almost never change clinical decisions in established hypothyroidism — the treatment is levothyroxine either way. Ultrasound is likewise not sensitive, specific or required; reserve it for a nodule found on examination. Practically: ask about past thyroid treatment, consider pituitary disease if the risk is significant, and otherwise assume Hashimoto's.

    The one place antibodies do earn their place is subclinical disease, where anti-TPO positivity predicts progression and can tip a treatment decision.

    Subclinical Hypothyroidism

    • Elevated TSH with normal FT4 and FT3 — usually TSH < 10–20.
    • May resolve spontaneously, especially if TSH < 10 and the patient is elderly.
    • No evidence of benefit from treatment — the exception is pregnancy.
    • Consider treating if TSH > 10 (high risk of progression), anti-TPO positive (higher risk of progression), or as a therapeutic trial for possible hypothyroid symptoms.
    How a Therapeutic Trial Ends

    A trial is a question, not a commitment. If levothyroxine normalises the TSH but the patient feels no different, the symptoms were not from the thyroid — the correct next step is to stop the levothyroxine and monitor for progression to overt hypothyroidism, not to escalate the dose chasing a symptom the drug has already failed to fix.

    Treating Hypothyroidism

    Replacement with oral levothyroxine (L-T4), once daily, titrated to a normal TSH.

    ParameterValue / rule
    Estimated requirement~1.5 mcg/kg/day — weight-based
    Half-life1 week
    MonitoringTSH every 4–6 weeks until normal
    Starting doseStart low and titrate up if elderly or with heart disease
    Why the 4–6 Week Interval Is Not Negotiable

    With a one-week half-life, levothyroxine takes roughly five weeks to reach a new steady state after any dose change — and TSH then needs time to re-equilibrate to that new level. A TSH drawn at two weeks is measuring a system still in transit, and will tempt you into a second dose change that the first one hadn't finished making. Change the dose, then wait.

    The same weight-based rule explains a common scenario in the other direction: a patient stable on levothyroxine who loses a significant amount of weight is now over-replaced on an unchanged dose, and can turn frankly thyrotoxic. The fix is to reduce the dose — and then, again, recheck at 4–6 weeks.

    Patient instructions that actually matter

    • Take ≥ 1 hour before and ≥ 2 hours after eating.
    • Separate by at least 4 hours from calcium, iron, magnesium and multivitamins — these bind it and blunt absorption.
    • Any time of day is fine so long as it is consistent, remembered, and separated from food and supplements — bedtime often works well.
