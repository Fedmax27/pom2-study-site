    Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • PMOS is the new name for PCOS (2026 global consensus). The "cysts" are really stalled follicles, and it's a whole-body endocrine and metabolic disorder, not just an ovary problem. Know both names. Up to 1 in 5 women of reproductive age.
    • Diagnosis (the triad): hyperandrogenism (clinical signs or a raised testosterone) + ovulatory dysfunction (oligomenorrhea or polycystic ovaries on ultrasound) + other disorders excluded. An ultrasound is not required.
    • Always exclude the mimics: pregnancy (β-hCG), prolactin, TSH, non-classic CAH (17-OHP), and an androgen-secreting tumour (fast onset, testosterone > 5 nmol/L). Ask about a partner's testosterone gel.
    • PMOS testosterone is normal or only a little high (e.g. 2.1 nmol/L, with normal female < 2). Insulin resistance drives much of it: central fat, acanthosis nigricans, skin tags.
    • Lifestyle for everyone, then treat the symptom that bothers her most: COCP or cyclic progestin (cycles, and endometrial protection: at least 4 bleeds a year), letrozole for fertility, spironolactone/cyproterone (with contraception) for hirsutism, minoxidil for hair loss. Keep screening for diabetes, BP, lipids and endometrial cancer.

    Learning Objectives

    • List the implications of a PMOS diagnosis for fertility, metabolic syndrome, diabetes risk, cancer risk and emotional health.
    • Distinguish treatment options for PMOS: lifestyle and medications.

    What's in a Name: PCOS → PMOS

    The old name, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), is still everywhere: older papers, charts, conversations. Recognize both. The name changed because the old one was wrong in three ways:

    Old wordThe problem
    "Poly-cystic"They aren't cysts. They're arrested antral follicles: developing eggs that stalled. They only look cystic on ultrasound
    "Ovary syndrome"The ovary is one target, not the only source. It involves hypothalamic GnRH, pituitary LH excess, insulin resistance and fat tissue making androgens
    New: "Polyendocrine Metabolic"Captures the many axes involved (HPO axis, insulin, adrenal androgens, adipokines) and the long-term cardiometabolic risk

    Source cited in the module: Teede HJ et al., Global Name Change Consortium, Lancet 2026;407:2329–39.

    Who gets it, and why

    More than 170 million women worldwide, up to 20% of those of reproductive age. No single cause has been found; it's best seen as several overlapping mechanisms:

    MechanismWhat happens
    Ovarian dysfunctionToo much ovarian stroma making testosterone
    HPO axis dysfunctionLH ↑, FSH ↓. The high LH:FSH balance over-stimulates the theca cells to make androgen
    Insulin resistanceThe ovaries have insulin and IGF receptors, so high insulin pushes them to make more androgen
    Excess fat tissuePeripheral fat converts adrenal hormones to testosterone

    The LH-drives-theca part is the two-cell model from 2.6: LH makes the theca cells produce androgen, and FSH makes the granulosa cells convert it to estrogen. Tip the balance toward LH and androgen is left over.

    The module's current diagnostic triad:

    1
    Hyperandrogenism, on clinical exam or labs (↑ testosterone).
    2
    Ovulatory dysfunction: oligomenorrhea or polycystic-appearing ovaries on ultrasound.
    3
    Other disorders excluded.

    The difference that matters: under Rotterdam, a woman with irregular cycles and polycystic ovaries but no androgen excess still counts. Under the module's triad, hyperandrogenism is required. Other sources often use Rotterdam, so know both.

    Exam

    • Hirsutism. The traditional tool is the modified Ferriman–Gallwey score: 9 body areas (upper lip, chin, chest, upper and lower abdomen, arm, thigh, upper and lower back), each graded 0–4, for a maximum of 36. A score of 8 or more meant hirsutism, but that cut-off came from white women. The lecture suggests lower cut-offs for some Asian populations (as low as about 2), and higher ones (around 9) for women of Mediterranean or South Asian background, whose normal scores run higher.
    • The Endocrine Society's definition is patient-centred: hirsutism is unwanted sexual hair growth, enough to cause her distress. The score is not the be-all and end-all.
    • Acne: persisting into adulthood, beyond what's usual in her family, especially on the chest and upper back.
    • Androgenic alopecia: diffuse thinning over the vertex and frontal scalp, a widening part.

    Labs: testosterone is the one

    TestosteroneMeans
    < 2 nmol/LNormal female range (most labs)
    Slightly high, e.g. 2.1–2.5 nmol/LFits PMOS. It can also be normal
    > 5 nmol/L, approaching the male range (low-normal male is about 8)Too high for PMOS: think tumour

    DHEA (an adrenal androgen) and androstenedione exist too, but the module says not to worry about them here. All women normally make some androgen.

    Ovulatory Dysfunction

    Oligomenorrhea is diagnosed from the history.

    AskLook for
    Age at menarche, and the pattern after itIrregular in the first year or two is normal. Did they become regular through the late teens?
    Cycle length (one period to the next) through adulthoodOligomenorrhea: cycles longer than 35 days, or fewer than 9 periods a year
    Recent oral contraceptive useIrregular periods after stopping the pill usually weren't caused by it: the pill was hiding an irregularity that was already there
    Family historyIrregular periods, subfertility

    Ultrasound: polycystic-looking ovaries (the classic "string of pearls") are follicles stuck at an early stage. They support the diagnosis, but they aren't required, and their absence doesn't rule PMOS out. They also turn up soon after menarche and in some women with perfectly normal cycles, so on their own they mean little.

    Excluding the Mimics

    "Put on your detective hat." Anything that can cause androgen excess or irregular periods has to be ruled out before you say PMOS.

    MimicClueTest
    Exogenous testosteroneTaking it, or exposure to a partner's topical testosterone; supplements with hidden androgensA careful medication, supplement and social history
    Androgen-secreting tumour (adrenal or ovarian; also cortisol-and-androgen tumours, as in Cushing's)Rapid onset over weeks to monthsTestosterone markedly raised (> 5, toward the male range) → image the adrenals and ovaries
    Non-classic CAHSubtle; looks just like PMOS (classic CAH would have been found in childhood)17-hydroxyprogesterone: high, because it sits upstream of the partly-blocked enzyme (3.9)
    PregnancyNew amenorrheaSerum β-hCG first, always
    Hyperprolactinemia (e.g. a prolactinoma)Galactorrhea. Prolactin switches off the gonadal axisProlactin: > 20 = hyperprolactinemia. PMOS itself can nudge it to about 25–30; a tumour is usually far higher (90–100+)
    Thyroid diseaseThe module describes thyrotoxicosis: palpitations, tremor, hair loss, rapid weight lossTSH (low, with high free T4)
    Just after menarche, or approaching menopauseIrregularity is expected in the first 1–2 years, and in the late 40s to early 50sHistory; ask when the women in her family went through menopause

    Two notes. The video calls this mimic "hypothyroidism" but describes hyperthyroid symptoms and labs, and the module's summary slide lists hyperthyroidism; either way, the test is a TSH. The prolactin pituitary side is in 3.2.

    Insulin Resistance: Not a Criterion, but Almost Always There

    Insulin resistance isn't in the current criteria (new guidelines due in the next year or two may change that), but most patients with PMOS have it, and it drives much of the long-term risk.

    WhereLook for
    HistoryA gentle weight history (did the periods become irregular after weight gain?) · diabetes or prediabetes · obstructive sleep apnea (snoring, daytime sleepiness) · metabolic (fatty) liver disease · family history of type 2 diabetes and gestational diabetes
    ExamCentral, visceral fat: waist-to-hip ratio > 0.85 in women (> 0.95 in men), the "apple" shape, with ethnic variation in cut-offs · acanthosis nigricans: dark, velvety skin on the back of the neck and underarms (also groin and inner thighs) · skin tags
    LabsHemoglobin A1c · lipid panel (high triglycerides)

    Not everyone with PMOS is overweight: a patient with a healthy BMI can still have it. And take the weight history kindly; it's a hard topic for many patients.

    Myths, Risks & Treatment

    What the diagnosis means for her future, and how to treat what matters most to her.

    Myth-Busting: What to Tell Patients

    The module's exercise. Patients will ask you these, so here are the answers.

    StatementTrue?
    Women with PMOS are more prone to subfertility and early miscarriageTrue
    Women with PMOS are more likely to have metabolic syndrome (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, high lipids)True
    Women with PMOS are more prone to endometrial cancerTrue
    Women with PMOS are at higher risk of mood disordersTrue
    Women with PMOS cannot conceiveFalse: harder, but very possible
    Women with PMOS cannot lose weightFalse: a balanced diet and exercise work
    Women with PMOS have an increased appetite and are always hungryFalse
    Eating organic or vegan helps, because food additives cause PMOSFalse

    Also more common with PMOS, from the module's wrap-up: central fat, gestational diabetes and frontal (androgenic) hair loss. Adrenal hypertrophy is not.

    Treatment

    Everyone: lifestyle

    • Diet: high fibre, low fat, lower carbohydrate.
    • Exercise: at least 30 minutes, 5 days a week (the module favours muscle-based exercise). About 150 minutes a week overall.
    • Modest weight loss is enough. Losing just 10–20 lb has let some of Dr. Hashmi's patients conceive.
    • Be a coach, not a critic. Celebrate any weight loss, and never body-shame. It sounds obvious; the module's point is to remember it when you're running late.

    Then: treat whatever bothers her most

    Her main concernOptionsWatch out for
    Irregular periods, no pregnancy plans soonCombined oral contraceptive (COCP): regular cycles, protects bone density, can help mood. Or cyclic progestin: a progestin-only pill for 2 weeks every 2–3 months, triggering a withdrawal bleed at least 4 times a yearCOCP side effects: migraines; worse mood or anxiety in some
    Wants to get pregnantNo pill. Lifestyle and modest weight loss, then refer to OB/GYN for letrozole (an aromatase inhibitor) to induce ovulation. Metformin: conflicting evidence; generally harmless but not routine. Surgery (ovarian drilling, partial resection, thermal ablation) is rare now, for refractory cases—
    HirsutismTemper expectations first. Surface methods (shaving, sugaring, epilation, waxing) are fine and do not make hair grow back thicker, which is a myth. Laser: semi-permanent to permanent. Anti-androgens: spironolactone, cyproterone (alone or in the COCP Diane). The COCP itself reduces hair. Eflornithine cream (Vaniqa): slows hair growth at the follicle; not hormonal; modestAnti-androgens need contraception: they could feminize a male fetus. "Success" = shaving every few days instead of daily
    Hair loss (alopecia)Minoxidil (Rogaine) 5% foam on the scalp · anti-androgens · COCP · hair transplant (the most effective, but expensive and not covered)—

    And always: manage the risks she won't ask about

    RiskWhat to do
    Metabolic (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease)Screen periodically for diabetes, BP and lipids · keep counselling on diet, at least 150 min/week of exercise and modest weight loss · raise bariatric surgery if she meets the BMI criteria: it restores cycles and fertility and lowers metabolic risk
    Endometrial cancerMake sure she has at least 4 withdrawal bleeds a year (COCP or cyclic progestin) · periodic pelvic ultrasound to check for endometrial thickening
    Why Irregular Periods Raise Endometrial Cancer Risk

    No ovulation means no corpus luteum, and no corpus luteum means no progesterone. The endometrium sits under estrogen alone, which keeps it growing and never lets it shed on schedule. Months of unopposed growth can become hyperplasia, then cancer. A progestin (in the COCP, or given cyclically) turns the lining over, and the withdrawal bleed clears it out. That's why the target is a number of bleeds per year, not a regular calendar. The cycle itself is covered in 3.3. Mechanism added to explain the module's recommendation.

    The Case: Ms. X

    Story30 years old: excess facial hair and unpredictable periods. Menarche at 12, irregular in the first year, then regular, but over the past year only about 4 periods. Dark terminal hair on the sides of the face, chin, chest and arms for 7 years; she now waxes twice a month instead of monthly (the change began a year ago). Gradual 30 lb weight gain since high school. No galactorrhea, no medications or supplements. Sedentary since college. Married 8 months ago and wants children. Family history: gestational diabetes in her mother and sister, type 2 diabetes in her father
    ExamBP 135/85, HR 83, BMI 30 with central fat, acanthosis nigricans on the neck and both axillae, significant hirsutism (chin, cheeks, trunk). No abdominal striae
    Labsβ-hCG negative · testosterone 2.1 nmol/L (mildly high) · prolactin 15 (normal) · TSH 0.6 (normal) · lipids normal · fasting glucose normal
    DiagnosisPMOS: hyperandrogenism (clinical and lab) + oligomenorrhea + mimics excluded
    "But Doc, Don't I Need an Ultrasound?"

    No. She already meets all three parts of the triad. A pelvic ultrasound is not needed to diagnose PMOS; that's the key teaching point of the case.

    Her plan: she's tearful because she hopes to conceive, so start with reassurance: conceiving is very possible, even spontaneously. If she isn't trying in the next six months or so, she needs effective contraception. Tell her cosmetic hair removal is fine and won't make it worse. Make a concrete diet and activity plan aiming for modest weight loss. Offer a COCP if she doesn't want pregnancy soon, and eflornithine cream for the hirsutism, warning her it takes time to work. Above all: stay kind.
