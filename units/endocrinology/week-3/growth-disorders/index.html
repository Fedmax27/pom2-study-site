    Disorders of Growth: Normal Growth & Short Stature

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Measure properly, plot it, and calculate the mid-parental height first. Boys: (mom + dad + 13 cm) ÷ 2. Girls: (mom + dad − 13 cm) ÷ 2. Lots of "short" kids are just growing along their parents' line.
    • Short stature = height below the 3rd percentile (2 SD or more below the mean). The number that really worries you is growth velocity: under 4.5 cm/yr between age 4 and puberty, or crossing percentiles.
    • Normal growth velocity = normal variant. Familial short stature: bone age normal, puberty on time, short parents. Constitutional delay: bone age delayed, puberty late, a parent who was a late bloomer.
    • Look for general pediatric causes first (poor nutrition, celiac, Crohn's, kidney disease, genetic syndromes). In these, weight drops first and height follows.
    • Endocrine causes are rare. The clue is a short, chubby child: weight is kept or rising while height falls off. GH deficiency is the most common one. Every short girl gets a karyotype, because short stature may be the only sign of Turner syndrome.

    Learning Objectives

    • Describe normal growth during childhood.
    • Identify normal variants of growth in childhood.
    • Recognize common general pediatric causes of short stature.
    • Identify the most common endocrine causes of short stature.
    • Identify common genetic causes of short stature.
    • Describe the clinical features and complications of Turner syndrome.

    Normal Growth: Three Phases

    PhaseWhat drives itHow fast
    InfancyMostly nutrition. Thyroid hormone and bone metabolism helpFastest: about 24 cm in year 1, then 10 cm in year 2
    ChildhoodGrowth hormone is the main driver. Thyroid hormone and nutrition still matterSlow but steady: about 5–6 cm/yr (4.5–6 cm/yr from age 4 to 10). The longer this phase lasts, the taller the final height
    PubertySex steroids plus bigger, more frequent GH pulsesThe growth spurt: girls 8–12 cm/yr, boys 10–14 cm/yr

    Mid-parental height

    Dr. Gallego's own warning: many referrals for short stature are children growing exactly where their parents' heights say they should. Calculate the mid-parental height before you refer.

    Height age and bone age

    Bone ageThink ofWhy
    DelayedConstitutional delay, poor nutrition, chronic disease, GH deficiency (and hypothyroidism)The whole child is running on a slower clock, so growth is still "available"
    NormalFamilial short stature, Turner syndromeThe skeleton is maturing on time; the child is just built small
    AdvancedPrecocious puberty, hyperthyroidismSex steroids (with GH) push the growth plates to mature, and close, early

    When Should You Worry?

    Red flagCut-off from the lecture
    Low growth velocityFrom age 4 to puberty: less than 4.5 cm/yr
    Crossing percentilesCrossing lines on the chart after 18 months and before puberty. Crossing is normal in the first 18 months, and upward crossing is expected in the pubertal spurt

    Normal Variants

    Short, but growing at a normal speed. Not a disease.

    Familial Short Stature vs Constitutional Delay

    Familial short stature (FSS)Constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP)
    HeightAt or below the 3rd percentileAt or below the 3rd percentile (weight too)
    Growth velocityNormal (maybe low-normal)Normal (maybe low-normal)
    PubertyOn timeLate
    Bone age= chronological ageDelayed, closer to the height age
    FamilyShort parents: the child tracks their targetA parent who was a late bloomer
    Final heightShort, but on the parental targetNormal: they catch up late

    The one-line test: bone age. On time means familial short stature. Delayed means constitutional delay. The delayed-puberty side of CDGP is covered in 3.4.

    Part 3

    Endocrine Causes

    Rare, but real. The clue is the weight: endocrine kids keep gaining weight while their height falls behind.

    Growth Hormone Deficiency

    • The most common endocrine cause of short stature. About 1 in 3,500–4,000 children.
    • Caused by too little GHRH (hypothalamus) or a pituitary problem.
    • It can be isolated or come with other pituitary hormone deficiencies.

    Causes

    TypeExamples
    GeneticMutations in GHRH, GH-1, PIT-1, PROP-1 · GH receptor defect = GH insensitivity (Laron syndrome) · IGF-1 gene deletions or mutations · IGF-1 receptor defects · growth plate disorders
    Congenital structuralEctopic or hypoplastic pituitary. Usually with midline defects and multiple pituitary deficiencies
    AcquiredSellar/parasellar tumours (e.g. craniopharyngioma) · cranial irradiation · head injury

    What congenital GH deficiency looks like

    WhenFinding
    At birthNormal birth weight and length. Growth in the womb depends on maternal nutrition, not GH
    NewbornNeonatal hypoglycemia · prolonged jaundice (especially with TSH or ACTH deficiency too) · micropenis (especially if gonadotropins are also low)
    After birthSevere postnatal growth failure
    Face and headPoorly developed nasal bridge, prominent forehead · midline defects (high or cleft palate) · irregular, late teeth · late closure of the anterior fontanelle · high-pitched voice
    OtherThin hair, poor nail growth
    Why a Newborn With No GH Gets Hypoglycemic

    GH and cortisol are both counter-regulatory hormones: they raise blood sugar between feeds. A newborn without GH (and often without ACTH as well, when several pituitary hormones are missing) cannot defend their glucose. That is why the lecture pairs neonatal hypoglycemia with micropenis and prolonged jaundice: together they point to the pituitary in a baby who looks normal-sized at birth. The adrenal side of this is on the adrenal page.

    Testing for GH deficiency

    Treatment: recombinant GH

    • Subcutaneous injection, 6–7 days a week, until growth is complete.
    • Expensive (the slide says about US$10,000–30,000 a year). The dose is by weight and by indication; Turner syndrome uses a higher dose than GH deficiency.
    • Indications from the lecture: GH deficiency · SGA with no catch-up by age 2 · Turner syndrome · Prader–Willi syndrome.

    Other Endocrine Causes

    Hypothyroidism: the lecture's case

    A 12-year-old girl: her height velocity has dropped, but her weight has held on the 50th percentile. She has very dry skin, fatigue, hair loss and brittle hair and nails.

    Part 4

    Turner Syndrome

    The genetic cause of short stature you cannot miss in a girl.

    Turner Syndrome and Growth

    • Why they're short: some make normal GH but the bone doesn't respond properly (a mild skeletal dysplasia). Others have low GH and bone resistance.
    • Many girls are referred for short stature alone, with none of the classic dysmorphic features. So: karyotype any short girl.

    GH treatment in Turner

    • Approved in Canada.
    • Start as soon as growth failure shows, often around age 4–6.
    • Gain: about 5–7 cm of adult height over about 8 years of treatment. Taller parents, better response.
    • Dose: 0.35–0.375 mg/kg/week, roughly double the GH-deficiency dose.

    The Rest of Turner Syndrome

    SystemFeatures
    OvariesComplete gonadal failure in 90% (streak ovaries). Thelarche 10–15%, menarche 1% (usually mosaics), pubarche 100% (the adrenals are normal), infertility > 99%. Most need estrogen replacement to go through puberty
    DysmorphicNeck webbing, short 4th metacarpals, increased carrying angle (cubitus valgus), nail dysplasia
    HeartCongenital heart disease, especially coarctation of the aorta
    KidneyRenal anomalies, e.g. horseshoe kidney
    AutoimmuneThyroid disease (about 10%), type 1 diabetes, celiac disease
    MetabolicMetabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes later
    OtherHearing problems · learning difficulties (non-verbal, arithmetic, visuospatial) · more ADHD
    NewbornLymphedema of the hands and feet (the lymphatics are underdeveloped before birth; it improves over months), hypoplastic nails, webbed neck

    Putting It Together: Evaluating a Short Child

    • Accurate measurement, plotted on a growth chart.
    • Mid-parental height, from the parents' heights.
    • Bone age to judge the growth potential.
    • Look for general causes first (chronic illness).
    • Endocrine causes are rare, and the child is usually small and "chubby, or at least not thin".
