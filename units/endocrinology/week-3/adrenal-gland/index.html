    The Adrenal Gland: Insufficiency & Overproduction

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • The adrenal gland exists to get you through a disruption of homeostasis. "Stress" is not a feeling — it is any change in the environment that exceeds your ability to respond while staying the same. An exam, a laparotomy and a bereavement all qualify, and the axis answers all three the same way: with cortisol.
    • The zone tells you the disease. Glomerulosa → aldosterone → Conn. Fasciculata → cortisol → Cushing. Reticularis → androgens. Medulla → catecholamines → pheochromocytoma. Only the glomerulosa is off the ACTH circuit — and almost every distinction on this page follows from that.
    • Insufficiency: primary = the gland failed (cortisol and aldosterone go, ACTH climbs → hyperpigmentation, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia); secondary = the signal failed (cortisol goes, aldosterone stays → no hyperkalemia, no pigmentation). Serum cortisol is the test; ACTH comes second to say which.
    • Adrenal crisis kills, sometimes at home. Fluids and 100 mg IV hydrocortisone go in on suspicion, before any confirmatory result.
    • Overproduction, three flavours: aldosterone → hypertension + hypokalemia + alkalosis, screen with the aldosterone : renin ratio. Catecholamines → spells of headache/palpitations/sweating, test fractionated plasma free metanephrines, and alpha-block before beta-block. Cortisol → Cushing, where the rhythm is lost and dexamethasone no longer suppresses; diagnosis needs two positive screening tests.

    Physiology: What Is the Adrenal Gland Actually For?

    The lecture opens with the question, and the answer is short: to regulate the response to stress. That only helps once you pin down what stress is, because the list of things that count looks incoherent — an exam, asking someone out, surgery, sepsis, a divorce, a bereavement, being told you are terminal.

    The Definition That Makes the List Make Sense

    Stress is a disruption caused by changes in the environment which exceeds the ability to respond while staying the same. It is a homeostasis word, not an emotional one. That is why the psychological and the physiological items sit on the same list — the axis does not distinguish between them, and it cannot. It has one output.

    This matters clinically: it is why a patient with no cortisol reserve can be tipped into a crisis by gastroenteritis, a dental extraction, or a death in the family — and why "stress dosing" is prescribed for all of them.

    One gland, four hormones

    Layer (outside → in)ProductWhat controls itJob
    Zona glomerulosaAldosterone (mineralocorticoid)Renin–angiotensin II and K+ — not ACTHSalt and volume
    Zona fasciculataCortisol (glucocorticoid)ACTHThe stress hormone; raises blood glucose
    Zona reticularisAndrogens (DHEA, DHEAS)ACTHSex-steroid precursors
    MedullaCatecholaminesSympathetic preganglionic fibres — it is effectively a sympathetic ganglionFight or flight

    GFR from the outside in — Salt, Sugar, Sex. The one fact worth carrying out of this table is that the outermost zone is not on the ACTH circuit.

    The HPA axis and its rhythm

    Hypothalamus (CRH) → anterior pituitary (ACTH) → adrenal cortex (cortisol). Cortisol then feeds back negatively on both — the loop that every disease on this page breaks somewhere different.

    Cortisol is secreted in a diurnal rhythm: highest in the early morning, nadir at night. Two clinical consequences, running in opposite directions:

    • For insufficiency: the rhythm tells you when to draw the blood — a 6–9 AM cortisol, when a healthy axis should be at its loudest.
    • For Cushing: loss of the rhythm is the abnormality. A midnight cortisol that should be at its nadir but is not is one of the earliest signs of excess.
    Part 1

    Too Little — Adrenal Insufficiency

    An endocrine emergency that presents as fatigue. The whole problem is that the chronic form looks like nothing, and the acute form kills quickly.

    Primary vs Secondary: Where the Lesion Sits

    TypeDefinition
    Primary
    (Addison's disease)    		Inability of the adrenal cortex to synthesise and secrete glucocorticoids (cortisol) and mineralocorticoids (aldosterone).
    Secondary
    (central)    		Impaired adrenal stimulation — abnormal ACTH and/or CRH from the pituitary or hypothalamus. Often a consequence of glucocorticoid use. The gland is intact but never gets the message.
    Critical illness–relatedCortisol production inadequate for the metabolic demand of critical illness. The axis is not destroyed; it is outrun.

    Either of the first two can present for the first time as an acute adrenal insufficiency — an Addisonian crisis. The chronic and acute forms are not different diseases; the crisis is what the chronic disease does when it meets a stressor.

    Who Gets It, and Why

    PrimarySecondary
    Peak age at diagnosis30–50 years50–59 years
    Worldwide prevalence93–140 per million150–280 per million
    Driven mostly byAutoimmune adrenalitisExogenous steroid use

    Both show a female predominance. Addison's disease runs an annual incidence of about 0.6 per 100,000 and a prevalence of 4–11 per 100,000 in the US. Note that secondary insufficiency is the more common of the two, and that is almost entirely a prescribing story.

    Etiology of primary adrenal insufficiency

    The deck's own instruction is worth repeating verbatim: know the main categories and one example from each — know the principle, not all the details.

    CategoryExamples
    Autoimmune
    80–90% in the West    		Isolated autoimmune adrenalitis; polyglandular autoimmune syndrome types I and II
    HereditaryCongenital adrenal hyperplasia (commonest, usually found in early childhood); congenital adrenal hypoplasia; familial glucocorticoid deficiency or resistance; adrenoleukodystrophy and adrenomyeloneuropathy; defective cholesterol metabolism
    InfectiousTB and disseminated fungal infection (histoplasmosis, paracoccidioidomycosis); HIV/AIDS; syphilis; African trypanosomiasis
    Metastatic cancerLung, breast, stomach and colon primaries; lymphoma

    About 40% of Addison's appears with a polyglandular autoimmune syndrome, so finding it obliges you to look for its company — autoimmune hypothyroidism, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anemia, alopecia, hypogonadism. In PGA type I, adrenal insufficiency is present in 100%, autoimmune thyroid disease in 70%, type 1 diabetes in 50% and hypogonadism in 5–50%. That is why TSH belongs in the follow-up of anyone with autoimmune adrenal insufficiency.

    Clinical Features: Chronic Disease vs Crisis

    The Screening Labs — and Why Each One Moves

    None of these makes the diagnosis. What they do is raise the question, and the pattern they form tells you which half of the axis to suspect before the cortisol comes back.

    Hover or tap any value for the mechanism behind it.

    LabPrimary (Addison's)Secondary (central)
    Sodium ↓ LowTwo hits at once: no aldosterone means the kidney wastes sodium and the patient is genuinely volume-depleted, and no cortisol means no brake on ADH, so water is retained on top of it.     Potassium ↑ HighAldosterone normally drives potassium secretion in the cortical collecting duct. Without it, potassium is retained. This is the single most useful discriminator on the panel.     Bicarbonate / pH ↓ BothAldosterone also drives H⁺ secretion in the collecting duct.     Glucose ↓ LowCortisol is a counter-regulatory hormone: it drives gluconeogenesis and opposes insulin. Remove it and fasting glucose falls. Hypoglycemia is more prominent in children and in secondary insufficiency.
    Calcium ↑ HighUncommon but real, and the mechanism is not fully settled. Volume depletion lowers GFR and increases proximal tubular calcium reabsorption, with a contribution from increased bone resorption. It corrects quickly on hydrocortisone — which is itself the clue.
    Creatinine ↑ Renal insufficiencyPre-renal. Salt wasting and vomiting drop the effective circulating volume and the kidney is simply underperfused — which is why it improves with fluids and steroid rather than with anything renal.
    ACTH ↑↑ HighNo cortisol means no negative feedback, so the corticotrophs run unopposed. The same POMC surge that raises ACTH also produces the hyperpigmentation — ACTH and MSH share a precursor, and ACTH itself activates the melanocortin-1 receptor on melanocytes. Normal or ↓The corticotroph is the lesion. Beware the word "normal": an ACTH sitting mid-range alongside a frankly low cortisol is inappropriately normal, and counts as low.
    Aldosterone ↓ Low, with renin ↑The zona glomerulosa is destroyed along with the rest of the cortex. The kidney senses the low volume and pours out renin, but there is no gland left to answer it — low aldosterone with high renin is the signature. This is why mineralocorticoid replacement is usually unnecessary in secondary insufficiency.
    CBC Eosinophilia, lymphocytosis, mild normocytic anemiaCortisol normally suppresses eosinophils and lymphocytes — it is what makes steroids useful in asthma. Take the cortisol away and both counts drift up. Unexplained eosinophilia in a hypotensive patient is a genuine, under-used clue.
    Reading the Panel in One Line

    Hyponatremia with hyperkalemia is primary until proven otherwise. Hyponatremia without hyperkalemia, in someone whose skin is a normal colour, is the central picture — and the next question is almost always "what steroids have you been on?"

    The lecture slide marks aldosterone as raised rather than normal in secondary insufficiency. The defensible statement is that it is preserved: the zona glomerulosa is not ACTH-dependent, so a central lesion leaves it and the renin–angiotensin system working. What matters is the contrast — low in primary, not low in secondary.

    Diagnosis: Cortisol First, ACTH Second

    Serum cortisol is the best blood test. It answers the only question that matters urgently — is there enough cortisol? — and it answers it straight away. ACTH is ordered afterwards, and it answers a different question: primary or secondary.

    Serum cortisol, drawn 6–9 AMInterpretation
    > 450 nmol/LAdrenal insufficiency ruled out
    80–450 nmol/LIndeterminate — go to a cosyntropin stimulation test
    < 80 nmol/LDiagnostic of adrenal insufficiency
    • Timing is for stable outpatients. In a symptomatic patient with a possible adrenal crisis, draw the cortisol at any time of day — and do not let the draw delay treatment.
    • A high ACTH with a low cortisol means primary. A low or inappropriately normal ACTH means secondary.
    • Stop estrogen at least 6 weeks before testing. It raises cortisol-binding globulin, which raises measured total cortisol and can falsely reassure you.
    • The 250 µg cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test asks the gland directly: given a maximal signal, can you make cortisol? A rise above 450 nmol/L is normal. Specificity 95%, sensitivity 97% for primary insufficiency — but it is much weaker for secondary, because a recently damaged pituitary leaves an adrenal that has not yet atrophied and can still answer a pharmacological dose.

    Treatment, Stress Dosing and Follow-up

    The adrenal crisis

    1
    ABCs.

    It is a shock state before it is an endocrine problem.

    2
    Send electrolytes and glucose — and, if it costs nothing, cortisol and ACTH.

    Worth having, because after the first dose of steroid they are uninterpretable for days. But drawing them must not delay giving anything.

    3
    Fluids, fast: 2–3 L of normal saline with dextrose, through two large-bore IVs.

    These patients are salt-depleted and hypoglycemic at the same time, which is why the fluid choice covers both.

    4
    Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV bolus, then 50–100 mg every 6–8 hours.

    Hydrocortisone is chosen because at these doses it has meaningful mineralocorticoid activity too — it replaces both arms at once.

    5
    Subacutely: taper fluids and steroid, add a mineralocorticoid, and find the precipitant.

    A crisis almost always has a trigger — infection, a missed dose, surgery, gastroenteritis that stopped the tablets going in. Fludrocortisone is added only once the hydrocortisone dose falls, because high-dose hydrocortisone is already doing that job.

    Chronic replacement

    DrugDoseNotes
    Hydrocortisone10–20 mg AM and 5–10 mg PMSplit to imitate the diurnal rhythm — the bigger dose on waking
    Prednisone (alternative)5–7.5 mg each morningLonger acting, so once daily
    Fludrocortisone (Florinef)0.05–0.1 mg once dailyThe mineralocorticoid. Often not required in secondary AI — aldosterone was never lost

    Stress dosing — the part that prevents deaths

    SituationWhat to do
    Surgery (scaled to type and duration)Hydrocortisone 50–100 mg one hour before — the "on call" dose — then 50–100 mg every 6–8 hours
    Intercurrent illness (flu, fever)The rule of 3s: triple the dose for three days
    Vomiting or severe diarrheaInjection, not tablets. An oral dose that is not absorbed does not exist — this is the route into a crisis

    Finding the right dose: over- and under-treatment

    There is no blood test that tells you the replacement dose is right — a cortisol level on treatment reflects the last tablet, not the adequacy of the regimen. Dosing is titrated clinically, and the signs run in two directions:

    Under-treatment with glucocorticoidsOver-treatment with glucocorticoids
    Symptoms of adrenal insufficiency returningWeight gain
    Constitutional symptoms — malaise, fatigueHyperglycemia
    Poor tolerance of ordinary daily stressHypertension
    Orthostatic drop in blood pressureOsteopenia

    The over-treatment column is simply Cushing syndrome arriving by prescription — a useful way to remember it, and a reminder that iatrogenic hypercortisolism is the commonest cause of Cushing's there is.

    • Ask at every visit about coping with daily stress, extra doses taken, and adverse events or hospitalisations.
    • Measure weight/BMI and orthostatic blood pressures — the cheapest test of mineralocorticoid adequacy.
    • Check TSH in autoimmune adrenal insufficiency, and look after the underlying HPA-axis disease in secondary insufficiency.
    • Re-teach stress dosing with the family present, and confirm the injectable Solu-Cortef kit and the identifiers — MedicAlert, wallet card, a letter from the physician, and a plan for travel.
    Part 2

    Too Much — Aldosterone

    Hypertension that will not respond to three drugs, with a low potassium and no edema. The commonest curable cause of high blood pressure.

    Primary Hyperaldosteronism

    Uncontrolled, persistent overproduction of aldosterone by one or both adrenal glands, with two defining features: production is not suppressible, and it is no longer renin-dependent. It accounts for 5–10% of all hypertension — far commoner than the "rare endocrine cause" framing suggests — and it can be unilateral or bilateral, a distinction that decides the treatment entirely.

    When to consider it

    TriggerWhy it points here
    Severe or treatment-resistant hypertension (> 3 drugs)Nothing in a standard regimen blocks the mineralocorticoid receptor — the drugs are aimed at the wrong mechanism
    Low K+ — spontaneous, or thiazide-induced with inappropriately high urinary K+ lossAldosterone drives K+ out through the urine. "Inappropriately high" is the key word: the kidney should be conserving potassium, and is not
    Hypertension plus an adrenal incidentalomaThe mass may be the source — see the incidentaloma work-up
    No edemaNot an indication so much as confirmation that the picture fits
    Two Things This Diagram Explains

    Why there is no edema, despite all that salt retention. After the first few days of sodium retention, volume expansion and higher renal perfusion pressure trigger a natriuresis that overrides the mineralocorticoid signal — aldosterone escape. Sodium balance is re-established at a higher blood pressure but a near-normal body sodium content. The hypertension persists; the edema never arrives. Edema in a hypertensive, hypokalemic patient should push you toward the states where escape fails: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome.

    Why "secondary" hyperaldosteronism looks different. In renal artery stenosis, diuretic use or heart failure the drive comes from renin itself — aldosterone is high but renin is also high, so the ratio stays normal. That is the whole discriminating power of the ratio.

    Screen, Confirm, Localise

    Three steps, in order, each answering a different question. Imaging first is the classic error.

    1 — Screening: the aldosterone : renin ratio

    PatternAldosteroneReninRatio
    Primary hyperaldosteronism↑↓ suppressed↑ raised
    Secondary hyperaldosteronism↑↑ also highNormal
    Essential hypertensionNormalNormalNormal

    Cut-offs vary between laboratories, because aldosterone and renin are measured in different units and by different assays — renin activity versus direct renin concentration, ng/dL versus pmol/L. A ratio quoted without its units and reference range is meaningless. Know the principle, not the number.

    The drugs that will lie to you

    Most antihypertensives move renin, and because renin sits in the denominator, moving it moves the ratio:

    The one class that must always come off is the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone) and amiloride/triamterene — withdraw for 4 weeks. Verapamil slow-release, hydralazine and the α1 blockers (prazosin, doxazosin, terazosin) are the usual substitutes if blood pressure will not tolerate a washout.

    2 — Confirmation: proving it cannot be suppressed

    A raised ratio is a screening result, not a diagnosis. The confirmatory test asks the question directly — give a stimulus that should switch aldosterone off, and see whether it switches off. The options are a saline infusion test (2 L normal saline), fludrocortisone suppression, or a high salt load. All three work by expanding volume, which in a normal person shuts the RAAS down.

    Reading This Result

    Aldosterone did not fall — it rose, 737 → 866 pmol/L, against an abnormal threshold of about 270 pmol/L (10 ng/dL). Two litres of saline is a substantial volume load; a normal gland answers it by shutting off. This one did not notice.

    Renin was already suppressed and went lower still (< 1.2), so the ratio more than doubled, to 722 against an abnormal cut-off of 144.

    Cortisol halved, 352 → 180. That is the point of measuring it: it is the internal control. Cortisol falling across the morning is the normal diurnal decline, which tells you the samples were drawn and handled properly and the aldosterone result is not an artefact.

    One exception worth knowing: with spontaneous hypokalemia, an undetectable renin and a frankly high aldosterone, the diagnosis is already made and confirmatory testing adds nothing.

    3 — Localisation and treatment

    • Adrenal CT first — chiefly to exclude a large mass that might be an adrenocortical carcinoma, and to see the anatomy.
    • Adrenal vein sampling is what actually decides the side. Aldosterone and cortisol are measured from each adrenal vein and a peripheral vein, cortisol correcting for how well each vein was cannulated. A nodule on CT is not proof that that nodule is the source — non-functioning nodules are common, and small aldosterone-producing adenomas can be invisible.
    • One-sided overproduction → unilateral adrenalectomy. Hypokalemia normalises in essentially everyone and blood pressure improves in all, with cure in roughly a third to a half.
    • Bilateral → medical treatment with a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist. Operating does not help.

    Worth chasing, rather than just treating the blood pressure: aldosterone excess damages heart, kidney and vasculature beyond what its blood pressure alone would predict, and in unilateral disease surgery offers something no antihypertensive can.

    Part 3

    Too Much — (Nor)adrenaline

    Rare, episodic, and lethal when missed on an anaesthetic table. Plus what to do with the adrenal mass nobody went looking for.

    Pheochromocytoma

    Catecholamine-secreting tumours arising from chromaffin tissue, which exists in two places:

    Site of originNameNote
    Adrenal medullaPheochromocytomaMakes adrenaline as well as noradrenaline — only the medulla has the enzyme, and it needs the high local cortisol draining from the cortex to express it
    Sympathetic ganglia, neck to pelvisParagangliomaOutside the gland, so noradrenaline only — no cortisol bathing it

    Rare: about 1 per 100,000 per year, and fewer than 0.2% of people with hypertension. Rarity is what makes it dangerous — it is never at the front of the differential.

    What patients notice

    Spell-related (paroxysmal)Chronic (sustained)
    The classic triad: headache, palpitations (tachycardia), sweating — with pallorSustained hypertension
    Anxiety, diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea, blood pressure spikesAnxiety, fatigue, headache, cold hands, fever, nausea, vomiting, sweating
    Entirely normal between spellsOrthostatic hypotension — in a hypertensive patient, an odd combination that should prompt the thought
    Why a Hypertensive Patient Drops Their Pressure on Standing

    Chronic catecholamine excess does two things at once: sustained vasoconstriction contracts the plasma volume, and the adrenergic receptors that would normally defend blood pressure on standing become desensitised. A volume-depleted patient with blunted reflexes has nothing left to respond with.

    The same physiology drives the surgical rule below — remove the tumour, the catecholamine drive vanishes, and an under-filled patient with down-regulated receptors can collapse. That is why alpha blockade and volume repletion come before anyone operates.

    Biochemistry: test the metabolites, not the hormones

    Why Metanephrines Beat Catecholamines

    Catecholamines are released episodically. If the patient is not having a spell when the blood is drawn, the level can be normal — and a rule-out test that depends on catching the tumour in the act is not a rule-out test.

    Metanephrines are produced continuously inside the tumour itself by ongoing intracellular metabolism, independent of secretion. They leak out steadily whether or not the patient is symptomatic — which is exactly why the sensitivity is so much higher.

    Who to test — and what a positive result is worth

    Genetics

    30–40% arise in association with a familial syndrome — MEN 2A and 2B, von Hippel–Lindau, neurofibromatosis, or succinate dehydrogenase mutations. Hence the single most actionable rule here: genetic testing is offered to every patient diagnosed, regardless of age or family history.

    The classic rule of 10s — 10% extra-adrenal (paragangliomas), 10% bilateral, 10% malignant. The lecture's own caveat on the last one is that it is likely less than 10%.

    Localisation, and the one thing never to do

    Do Not Biopsy It

    In a report of 20 patients whose pheochromocytoma was diagnosed at the time of biopsy, 14 (70%) had a biopsy-related complication — severe hypertension, haematoma, severe pain, an inadequate sample, a technically harder operation, conversion to an open procedure, or a delay to surgery.

    The rule: do not biopsy an enlarged adrenal gland until pheochromocytoma has been excluded biochemically. Putting a needle into a catecholamine reservoir does exactly what you would expect.

    Preoperative blockade

    OrderDrugsWhy
    1. Alpha blockade — always firstPhenoxybenzamine · prazosin · doxazosinRelieves the vasoconstriction and lets the contracted plasma volume re-expand — what protects the patient once the tumour is out
    2. Beta blockade — only afterPropranololAdded to control the reflex tachycardia that alpha blockade produces
    Why the Order Is Not Negotiable

    Circulating catecholamines act on both receptor families at once: β2 receptors mediate vasodilatation, α1 receptors vasoconstriction. Block beta first and you remove the vasodilating counterweight while leaving alpha-mediated vasoconstriction entirely unopposed — systemic vascular resistance climbs and the patient can go into a hypertensive crisis.

    The same logic makes labetalol a poor first choice despite having both actions: its beta effect is roughly five times its alpha effect, so it behaves much like a beta-blocker.

    Key messages

    • Rare, but important to recognise — the clinical problem is that it never looks likely enough to test for.
    • Once diagnosed, surgery after combined blockade, alpha first and beta added later.
    • Unrecognised, it causes hypertensive crisis and complications such as myocardial infarction. The classic disaster is an undiagnosed patient going to theatre for something else.
    • Benign or malignant? The only absolute criterion for malignancy is distant metastasis. The PASS score grades histology — < 4 behaves benignly, > 4 has potential for aggressive behaviour — but it stratifies risk, it does not make the call.

    The Adrenal Incidentaloma

    An adrenal mass found on imaging done for something else. They are common, and getting commoner as scanning does:

    FindingFrequency

    Over the age of 707%

    1 — Is it functioning? Exclude overproduction of…Test

The lecture's own bottom line still holds: the only absolute criterion for malignancy is the presence of distant metastasis. And the rule from the section above applies here too — never biopsy an adrenal mass until pheochromocytoma has been excluded.

    Part 4

    Too Much — Cortisol

    The same axis as Part 1, driven the other way. Back to the HPA axis one last time — because every diagnostic test for Cushing is a test of one of its two normal properties.

    Cushing Syndrome: Overexposure to Glucocorticoids

    Recall from the physiology section what cortisol is for: raising blood glucose — gluconeogenesis, muscle catabolism for amino acids, inhibition of GLUT-4. Every feature below is one of those effects running unchecked for years.

    Beyond the habitus: menstrual irregularity · glucose intolerance and frank diabetes · cardiovascular disease (death, myocardial infarction, stroke, thromboembolism) · fungal infections · androgen excess (sideburns, oily skin, acne, increased libido) · neuropsychological change (lability, depression, irritability, anxiety, panic attacks) · growth retardation in children.

    Which signs actually move the needle

    Most of these features are common in people who do not have Cushing's. The likelihood ratios sort the discriminating ones from the merely associated:

    SignLikelihood ratio of a positive result
    Ecchymoses10 – 11.3
    Osteopenia or fracture8 – 13.8
    Proximal muscle weakness8 – 12.6
    Hypertension4.35 – 5.29
    Edema2.82 – 3.88
    Striae (purple, > 1 cm)2.72 – 2.91
    Plethora2.51 – 3.03

    The three at the top are the ones worth hunting for — easy bruising, osteopenia or fracture, and proximal weakness. Note where obesity sits: near the bottom. The feature everyone associates with Cushing's is one of the least discriminating, because it is so common without it. L. Nieman, Handbook of Diagnostic Endocrinology.

    Diagnosis: Three Things Go Wrong with Cortisol

    Three abnormalities, and each screening test is built to catch a different one:

    What is wrongThe test that catches itResult suggesting Cushing
    Increased cortisol production24-hour urinary excretion of cortisolAbove the upper limit of normal
    Lack of diurnal rhythmLate-night salivary cortisol> 4 nmol/L at the time it should be at its nadir
    Lack of suppressibility1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppressionCortisol > 50 nmol/L at 08:00–09:00

    What dexamethasone is actually testing

    That is the logic of the whole test: dexamethasone is a potent synthetic glucocorticoid that the pituitary reads as cortisol, but which the cortisol assay does not detect. In a healthy axis it switches ACTH off and measured cortisol falls. It only fails to suppress when cortisol production has escaped pituitary control — either because the source is autonomous (adrenal), or because the ACTH source itself is a tumour that ignores feedback.

    Then: ACTH-Dependent or Independent?

    Diagnosis comes first, cause second — and the branch point is a single measurement.

    The Answer to the Slide's Question

    Exogenous glucocorticoids — by a wide margin. Prescribed steroid for asthma, rheumatological or inflammatory disease is the commonest cause of Cushing syndrome seen in general practice, and it is the one route on the diagram that does not involve a tumour at all. Always ask about steroids — including inhaled, topical and injected — before working anyone up.

    Among endogenous causes, the commonest is a pituitary corticotroph adenoma — Cushing disease — at roughly 60–80%, with adrenal causes and ectopic ACTH making up the remainder.

    This is also the loop back to Part 1: a patient on long-term steroid has Cushing syndrome and a suppressed axis at the same time, and stopping the drug abruptly gives them an adrenal crisis.

    Treatment and follow-up

    • Surgical removal of the culprit tumour where possible.
    • Cushing is not "cured" — it goes into remission. Recurrence is possible even after 10 years, so follow-up does not stop.
    • Monitor clinically, and biochemically with 24-hour urine cortisol and late-night salivary cortisol — the same two properties of the hormone that made the diagnosis.
