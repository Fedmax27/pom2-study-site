On This Page

    Anatomy of the Assigned-Female Pelvis & Perineum & Sexual Function

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • The uterus normally sits anteverted and anteflexed, resting on the bladder. The cervix is the least mobile part, held by the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments. The speculum blades sit in the anterior and posterior fornices.
    • The uterine artery (from the internal iliac) runs in the cardinal ligament and crosses over the ureter: "water under the bridge". Find the ureter before you clamp in a hysterectomy. The ovarian artery comes straight off the aorta, in the suspensory ligament, because the ovary started out high.
    • Pelvic pain line = the lower edge of the peritoneum. Pain from above it (fundus, body, tubes, ovaries) travels with the sympathetics to T11–L2 and is felt in the lower trunk and back. Pain from below it (cervix, upper vagina) travels with the parasympathetics to S2–S4 and is felt in the buttock and back of the leg. The perineum is pudendal nerve (S2–S4).
    • The pouch of Douglas (rectouterine pouch) is the lowest point of the peritoneal cavity. Fluid collects there when upright, and you can reach it through the posterior fornix (culdocentesis).
    • Two fascial pouches, two injuries. Straddle injury tears the spongy urethra, so urine fills the superficial pouch and tracks up the abdominal wall (never the thighs). Pelvic fracture tears the membranous urethra, so urine fills the deep pouch and can rise into the pelvis. And: internal hemorrhoids bleed but don't hurt; external ones hurt.

    Learning Objectives

    • Identify the uterus, cervix, uterine tubes, ovaries, bladder, vagina and vulva.
    • Describe the structures that support and stabilize the uterus and vagina, and the complications when they fail.
    • Trace the arterial supply, venous drainage and lymphatic drainage of the pelvis and perineum.
    • Describe the nerve supply to the female pelvis and why it makes diagnosis tricky.
    • Describe the fascial layers of the perineum and how they decide where fluid collects.
    • Compare male and female erectile bodies and external genitalia, and their role in the sexual response.
    • Build an approach to a 20-year-old woman with right lower quadrant pain.

    Dr. Martin's workshop runs for over three hours: lectures, lab videos and knowledge checks. It builds on the P1 pelvis and perineum. The embryology behind all of this (why the ovary's blood supply comes from the aorta, why the round ligament ends in the labia majora) is the next lecture, 3.7.

    A Note on the Words Used

    The lecture opens by explaining its language. Sex refers to genes, chromosomes, anatomy and their products. Gender refers to the psychological, social and cultural roles a society calls masculine or feminine. Both come in more than two versions, and they combine into a person's self-identification (pronouns, language, presentation).

    ApproachWhen to use itExample
    Person-first languageOne-on-one: use the person's own identificationAsk for, and use, their pronouns
    Avoid binary shortcutsGeneral speech"Assigned female at birth" instead of "born female"; "all sexes" instead of "both sexes"; "phenotypic" instead of "normal" development; "they" when you don't know
    Anatomy-first languageBig general statements, when identity is unknown"Anyone with a cervix needs screening" instead of "women need screening"; "people with a prostate" instead of "men"

    In this course, "female" and "male" mean the assigned-female-at-birth and assigned-male-at-birth phenotypes. The same distinction comes up again in 3.8.

    Part 1

    The Organs

    Vagina, uterus and cervix, uterine tubes and ovaries: what each looks like, what it does, and how you examine and image it.

    The Layout

    The Vagina

    • A musculomembranous tube, 7–9 cm long, from the cervix to the vaginal opening in the vestibule (between the labia minora).
    • It lies between the urethra and bladder in front and the rectum behind.
    • Three jobs: receives the penis, carries menstrual flow, and is the birth canal.
    • The cervix pokes into the top of the vagina, leaving a gutter around it: the fornix. It has an anterior, a posterior (the deepest) and two lateral parts.

    The Uterus and Cervix

    LayerWhat it is
    EndometriumInner mucous lining. Shed in menstruation; where the blastocyst implants
    MyometriumThick smooth muscle carrying the vessels and nerves. Hormones make it contract in menses and labour
    PerimetriumOuter serous coat

    The histology of each layer across the cycle is in 2.7.

    Examining the cervix

    Why it matters: the lecture quotes a lifetime cervical cancer risk of about 1 in 145. Before 1940 it was the leading cause of cancer death in North American women. Deaths fell sharply once the cervix could be looked at and sampled routinely. Screening itself is a later lecture.

    Position: anteverted and anteflexed

    • Anteverted: the cervix tips forward from the vagina.
    • Anteflexed: the body bends forward on the cervix.
    • Together they put the uterus on top of the bladder. That is why pregnant people need to pee so often.
    • The top of the uterus moves around. A very full bladder can push it back toward retroversion.
    • The position is also support: when abdominal pressure pushes down, the bladder is underneath (Part 2).

    Shape and size across a lifetime

    StageBody : cervixWhy

    Child1 : 1No hormones
    Puberty2 : 1Hormones return; still growing to adult size
    Adult, nulliparous2 : 1
    Adult, multiparous3 : 1Never shrinks fully back after pregnancy
    Postmenopause1 : 1Hormones gone again

    In pregnancy

    Uterine Tubes and Ovaries (the Adnexa)

    Adnexa means "adjacent structures": everything lateral to the uterus.

    OrganJobs
    Uterine tubeCatches the oocyte from the peritoneal cavity (the fimbriae sweep it in), is the usual site of fertilization, and carries the embryo to the uterus
    OvaryDevelops oocytes and releases them into the peritoneal cavity, not straight into the tube; makes the reproductive hormones

    From sperm to implantation

    • Sperm survive 3–5 days in the tract, so insemination usually happens before ovulation.
    • The ovary releases the oocyte; the fimbriae draw it in.
    • Fertilization in the ampulla. The embryo moves medially along the tube.
    • Implantation in the endometrium 6–8 days later.

    Part 2

    What Holds It All Up

    Dynamic support from muscle, passive support from ligaments and position. When these fail, you get incontinence and prolapse.

    Supports of the Uterus and Ovaries

    TypeWhat
    Dynamic (muscle)Pelvic floor and urogenital diaphragm. Both meet at the perineal body
    PassivePosition: anteverted/anteflexed on the bladder · endopelvic fascia: cardinal and uterosacral ligaments · broad ligament · round ligament

    The cervix is the least mobile part of the whole tract. It is the hub the endopelvic fascia ligaments anchor to.

    LigamentRuns from → toKey point
    Cardinal (transverse cervical)Cervix and vagina → lateral pelvic wall, at the base of the broad ligamentCarries the uterine vessels. Holds the uterus up by the cervix
    UterosacralCervix → mid-sacrumStops the cervix prolapsing into the vagina. Surgical landmark: the ureter runs just lateral to it
    BroadA double layer of peritoneum draped over the uterus to the pelvic wallsThree parts: mesometrium (uterus), mesovarium (ovary), mesosalpinx (tube; salpinx = trumpet)
    Round ligament of the uterusUterotubal junction → deep ring → inguinal canal → labia majoraAnchors the uterus forward. Stretches a lot in pregnancy: round ligament pain
    Ligament of the ovaryOvary → uterusWith the round ligament, the female remnant of the gubernaculum
    Suspensory ligament of the ovaryOvary → lateral pelvic wallCarries the ovarian vessels (it's a "ligament" because it's covered in peritoneum)
    Why the Round Ligament Ends in the Labia Majora

    In both sexes a cord called the gubernaculum tethers the gonad and pulls it down as the embryo grows. In males it drags the testis through the inguinal canal into the scrotum. In females the middle of the cord gets stuck to the side of the uterus, splitting it into two: the ligament of the ovary (ovary to uterus) and the round ligament, which carries on through the inguinal canal into the labia majora, the female homologue of the scrotum. The full story is in 3.7.

    Clinical: these supports are often stretched or damaged in pregnancy and childbirth, which leads to incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse.

    Peritoneal Pouches and the Posterior Fornix

    The peritoneum drapes down over the bladder, dips between the bladder and the uterus, goes up and over the uterus, dips again behind it, then climbs up over the rectum. The two dips are the pouches.

    The Pelvic Floor

    MuscleFromTo
    Levator ani (puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus)Body of the pubis, tendinous arch of the obturator fascia, ischial spinePerineal body, coccyx, anococcygeal ligament
    CoccygeusIschial spineSacrum and coccyx
    • The urogenital hiatus is the front gap in the floor that lets the urethra and vagina through. The perineal membrane closes it from below (Part 5).
    • The pelvic walls: obturator internus (sides), piriformis (back, leaving through the greater sciatic foramen with the sacral plexus roots). The floor is levator ani + coccygeus.
    • You contract the floor every time you cough, sneeze or bear down, to stay continent.
    • In the lab, the floor is often thin and fatty in older donors, which is a picture of why pelvic floor weakness is so common.

    Two Clinical Consequences

    Hysterectomy: the uterine artery has to be clamped, and the ureter lies right under it. The surgeon must positively identify the ureter first so it isn't tied or cut. That is the whole point of "water under the bridge".

    The ovary's supply is high. Gonads develop on the posterior abdominal wall and bring their vessels (and nerves and lymphatics) with them as they descend. That is why the ovarian artery comes off the aorta near the kidneys, and why ovarian lymph drains to nodes beside the aorta (below).

    The uterus has a huge blood supply for a reason. Each cycle the endometrium is rebuilt with fresh blood and shed if there's no pregnancy. If an embryo implants, the vessels dilate and engorge to build and feed the placenta for the rest of the pregnancy.

    Vessels of the Perineum

    The deep dorsal vein is squeezed shut during an erection. The swollen erectile bodies press it against the tight deep fascia around them (called Buck's fascia in the penis; the clitoris has the same layer). Blood can't leave until the erection ends. More in Part 7.

    Lymphatic Drainage

    NodesDrain
    Superficial inguinalSkin of the perineum
    Deep inguinalErectile bodies, external genitalia
    External iliacUpper vagina, cervix, lower uterine body
    Internal iliacMid-upper vagina, cervix, uterine body (and male cavernous bodies)
    SacralLower vagina
    Lumbar / pre-aorticOvaries, uterine tubes, fundus

    The chain runs superficial inguinal → deep inguinal → external iliac → common iliac → lumbar/pre-aortic → cisterna chyli → thoracic duct → back to the blood.

    The Lecture's Knowledge Check: Cervical Cancer

    Where does cervical cancer spread first? Follow the blood supply. The cervix is fed by the uterine artery, a branch of the internal iliac, so it drains to the internal iliac nodes.

    But the uterine artery runs out along the cardinal ligament to the lateral pelvic wall, where the external iliac nodes sit, so the cervix drains there too. In practice you check both. The general rule: nodes near a drainage path can pick up lymph even if they aren't the "textbook" group.

    Part 4

    Nerve Supply

    Autonomic nerves for the organs, the pudendal nerve for the perineum, and one line that decides where visceral pain is felt.

    Sympathetic and Parasympathetic

    SympatheticParasympathetic
    Starts atIntermediolateral horn, T11/T12–L1/L2 (thoracolumbar only)S2–S4 (craniosacral only)
    RouteThrough the sympathetic chain without synapsing → lumbar splanchnic nerves → synapse in prevertebral ganglia / inferior hypogastric plexus → postsynaptic fibres to the organsPelvic splanchnic nerves → synapse in the inferior hypogastric plexus → postsynaptic fibres to the organs
    EffectsUterine contraction, vasoconstrictionUterine inhibition, vasodilation

    The inferior hypogastric plexus is mixed: both systems run through it. And in the uterus hormones call the shots, so the autonomic effects are secondary. If the autonomic basics are rusty, the lecture points you back to the Foundations nervous-system module.

    The Pelvic Pain Line: Where Visceral Pain Goes

    Above the pelvic pain lineBelow the pelvic pain line
    OrgansFundus and body of the uterus, uterine tubes, ovariesCervix, upper vagina
    Pain travels withSympathetics (via the hypogastric plexuses)Parasympathetics (pelvic splanchnic nerves)
    Spinal levelT11–L1/L2S2–S4
    Felt inLower trunk, flank, backButtock, perineum, back of the thigh and leg

    This is the "challenge with diagnosis" in the objectives. The brain maps visceral pain onto the skin of the same spinal level, so pelvic organ pain turns up as back pain, flank pain or leg pain, and you have to think backwards from the dermatome to the organ.

    Somatic Supply: the Pudendal Nerve

    NerveSupplies
    Inferior rectal (pudendal branch)Crosses the ischioanal fat to the external anal sphincter, anal mucosa and perianal skin
    Dorsal nerve of the clitoris/penis (terminal pudendal branch)The main sensory nerve of the clitoris/penis
    Ilioinguinal (anterior labial/scrotal branches)Front of the labia majora / scrotum

    The lowest part of the vagina and rectum and the whole perineum have somatic (normal, sharp, well-localized) sensation through the pudendal nerve. Everything above has visceral sensation, which is dull and poorly localized.

    The Perineum

    The diamond below the pelvic floor: its fascial layers, its two pouches, and the erectile tissue and external genitalia that live in them.

    The Diamond and Its Two Triangles

    Fascial Layers and the Two Pouches

    Layer / spaceWhat it is and what's in it
    Pelvic floorFloor of the pelvis = roof of the perineum. Has the urogenital hiatus at the front
    Deep perineal pouch (urogenital diaphragm)Between the pelvic floor and the perineal membrane; really a region, not a space. Urethra (and vagina) pass through. External urethral sphincter (and vaginal sphincter), deep transverse perineal muscles, dorsal neurovascular structures of the erectile tissue, bulbourethral glands (males)
    Perineal membraneCloses the urogenital hiatus from below. Only in the urogenital triangle; merges with the perineal body at the back
    Superficial perineal pouchBetween the perineal membrane and Colles' fascia. Erectile bodies and their muscles, pudendal nerve branches, internal pudendal vessel branches, greater vestibular glands (females). In males it includes the scrotum and penis
    Colles' fasciaContinuous with Scarpa's fascia of the abdominal wall in front (and dartos in the scrotum). Does not extend into the thighs or back into the anal triangle
    Where Does the Urine Go? The Lecture's Two Cases

    A 17-year-old with a straddle injury (swelling in the groin): the spongy urethra tears, below the perineal membrane. Urine and blood fill the superficial pouch. Colles' fascia is fused to the thighs and to the back of the membrane, and continuous with Scarpa's fascia in front, so the fluid spreads into the scrotum and penis and up the anterior abdominal wall, but never into the thighs or the anal triangle.

    A 26-year-old after a car crash with a pelvic fracture and free fluid on the FAST scan: the membranous urethra tears as it passes through the perineal membrane. Fluid fills the deep pouch. Its roof, the pelvic floor, has a hole in it (the urogenital hiatus), so fluid rises up into the pelvic cavity. This stays under the peritoneum, not inside the peritoneal cavity.

    The same anatomy is why catheterizing, especially a male urethra with its bends, has to be done gently.

    Catheterizing a Female Patient

    The external urethral orifice is small and tightly closed. The catheter tip tends to slip into the larger vaginal opening just behind it. If that happens, the catheter is contaminated with vaginal flora and is no longer sterile. Throw it out and open a fresh one; pushing a dirty catheter into the bladder invites a UTI.

    Male review, briefly: penis = root, body, corona and glans; its dorsal surface faces forward when flaccid. The scrotum is a fibromuscular skin sac for the testes. Its dartos muscle wrinkles the skin (thicker skin, less surface area) and works with cremaster (which lifts the testes) to keep them at the right temperature. The raphe down the middle is the seam where the folds fused in development. The membranous urethra is only about 1 cm long, as it crosses the urogenital membrane.

    Part 6

    The Anal Triangle

    Fat, a canal full of nerves and vessels, and the venous plexus that gives you hemorrhoids.

    The Ischioanal Fossa

    • Pudendal (Alcock's) canal: a tunnel inside the obturator internus fascia, on the side wall of the fossa. It carries the internal pudendal vessels and the pudendal nerve, which give off the inferior rectal branches as they pass.
    • Ischioanal fat is among the last fat to go in starvation. Without it the rectum loses support, so rectal prolapse is relatively common in starvation.

    Rectal Vessels and Hemorrhoids

    Hemorrhoids are varicose veins of this valveless plexus. Anything that raises the pressure in it can cause them: portal hypertension, IVC obstruction, or anything raising pelvic venous pressure, such as pregnancy (the big uterus presses on the veins).

    Part of the canalUpper 2/3Lower 1/3
    EmbryologyHindgut endodermProctodeum ectoderm
    NervesAutonomic (pelvic plexus)Somatic (pudendal / inferior rectal nerve)
    Venous plexusInternal rectal plexusExternal rectal plexus
    HemorrhoidsInternal: painless but bleed (blood on the toilet paper)External: painful, itchy, and bleed
    Part 7

    Functional Sexual Anatomy

    How the nerves, vessels and erectile tissue work together.

    Arousal and Erection

    MechanismTriggered byPathway
    PsychogenicSounds, sights, smells, imaginationBrain → sacral cord → pelvic splanchnic (parasympathetic) nerves. A sympathetic contribution is possible but unproven
    ReflexogenicTouchSensory fibres → sacral cord → parasympathetic output (a spinal reflex)
    1. Parasympathetic signal → the helicine arteries (end branches of the deep artery, from the internal pudendal) uncoil and dilate.
    2. Blood pours in and the erectile tissue engorges.
    3. The swollen bodies press the deep dorsal vein against the tight deep (Buck's) fascia, so blood can't get out.
    4. Ejaculation (or orgasm) is a sympathetic event: coordinated contraction of the reproductive tract.
    5. Detumescence: the parasympathetic drive stops, the arteries constrict again, and blood drains out through the veins.

    The lecture uses the male system because much less is known about the anatomy, physiology and biology of the female sexual response; there are real gaps in the research.

    Erectile dysfunction

    CauseTreatment
    CNS or endocrine disorders, including low testosteroneTreat the cause
    Nerves fail to stimulate the tissue, or vessels don't dilateOral medication that increases blood flow to the erectile tissue (the slide shows Cialis and Viagra)
    Lesion of the prostatic plexus or cavernous nervesPills can't work without the nerve signal, so surgical options: penile pump, or semi-rigid or inflatable prosthesis
    Why Radical Prostatectomy Causes ED

    The cavernous nerves run through the prostatic plexus on the surface of the prostate. Taking the prostate out for cancer very often damages them. With the parasympathetic signal cut, the arteries never get the "dilate" message, so loss of erection is a frequent result of radical prostatectomy.

    The sexual response cycle

    StageWhat happens
    1. DesireWanting to take part, from outside or inside stimuli
    2. ArousalCentral (thoughts, fantasies) · non-genital peripheral (salivation, sweating, skin vasodilation, nipple erection) · genital (clitoral, labial and vaginal engorgement; penile erection)
    3. OrgasmAn altered state of consciousness; male ejaculation
    4. ResolutionReturn to baseline; the cycle can start again

    There are several models of this cycle. The lecture's point is not the model itself but which anatomy and which nerves are active at each stage.
