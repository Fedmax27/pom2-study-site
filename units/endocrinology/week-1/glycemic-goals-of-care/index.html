    Glycemic Goals of Care in Adults with Diabetes

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Most patients with T1DM/T2DM should target A1c ~7.0% — but individualize. See DCCT & UKPDS for the trial evidence behind that number.
    • Limited life expectancy or high hypoglycemia risk (dementia, falls, recurrent lows) → liberalize the target, up to 8.5%.
    • A1c is the best-validated tool for chronic complication risk, but SMBG stays essential — it prevents/confirms hypoglycemia and gives real-time feedback A1c can't.

    Learning Objectives

    • Understand why patient-centered glycemic goals matter in diabetes management.
    • Outline the factors that justify tighter vs. more liberal glycemic targets.
    • Review the different types of glycemic targets and the tools used to set them.

    Why Glycemic Goals Matter

    Glycemic control drives outcomes on two timescales: short-term (symptomatic hyperglycemia, DKA, HHS, and hypoglycemia) and long-term (microvascular complications — retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy — and, more so in T1DM than T2DM, macrovascular complications). The target you set also drives real decisions: which drug(s) to start, how to dose them, and how closely to monitor.

    • Regardless of treatment, an A1c > 7% is associated with more microvascular and cardiovascular complications. Both fasting and post-prandial glucose independently correlate with complication risk, with some evidence that post-prandial glucose is the stronger independent risk factor for CV complications.
    • Tight control (major trials: DCCT, UKPDS, ACCORD, ADVANCE) improves microvascular outcomes — but increases hypoglycemia risk. A very tight target (A1c 6.0%) isn't ideal for older/frail patients, long-standing diabetes, advanced CAD, or a history of severe hypoglycemia.

    Drug choice for reaching a given target is its own topic — see the glucose-lowering drugs lecture.

    Tools for Setting Glycemic Goals

    Self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) — capillary fingerstick, or interstitial fluid via CGM/flash monitoring. Checking frequency is individualized (type of diabetes, therapy, hypoglycemia risk/awareness, occupation, illness). Usual targets:

    CheckTarget
    Fasting4–7 mmol/L
    2 hours post-meal5–10 mmol/L

    A1c (glycated hemoglobin) estimates mean plasma glucose over the preceding 8–12 weeks (~50% of the value reflects the last 30 days) — so it lags behind real changes in therapy. Several things can throw off its accuracy independent of true glycemic control:

    FactorIncreases A1cDecreases A1c
    ErythropoiesisB12/Fe deficiency, decreased erythropoiesisEPO/Fe/B12 therapy, reticulocytosis, chronic liver disease
    Altered hemoglobinVariable: fetal Hb, hemoglobinopathies, methemoglobin
    Altered glycationChronic renal failure, ↓↓ erythrocyte pHASA, vitamin C/E, hemoglobinopathies, ↑ erythrocyte pH
    Erythrocyte destructionSplenectomyHemoglobinopathies, chronic renal failure, splenomegaly, rheumatoid arthritis, HAART/ribavirin/dapsone
    Assay interferenceHyperbilirubinemia, carbamylated Hb, ETOH, chronic opiatesHypertriglyceridemia

    Symptoms are also a practical guide:

    HyperglycemiaHypoglycemia (BG ≤ 4.0 mmol/L on insulin/secretagogue)
    Polyuria, nocturia, polydipsia, weight loss, fatigue — can progress to DKA or HHSGraded asymptomatic → mild → moderate (autonomic ± neuroglycopenic, self-treatable) → severe (needs assistance). Symptoms: sweating, dizziness, tachycardia, hunger, confusion, seizure, coma.

    Why A1c Is Still the #1 Tool

    A1c is the best-validated marker of control — its relationship to chronic structural complication risk has been directly assessed in randomized trials, unlike day-to-day SMBG readings. Diabetes Canada's bottom line: target A1c ≤ 7.0% for most people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes to reduce microvascular risk, and CV risk if implemented early in the disease course.

    That said, SMBG stays essential even with A1c as the gold standard — many patients can't reach target without it. It's how hypoglycemia gets prevented and confirmed, it gives real-time feedback on diet/exercise changes, it guides medication and bolus-insulin dosing, and it's necessary whenever something on the table above makes the A1c itself unreliable.

    Setting Targets: Guiding Principles

    • Minimum goal for everyone: free of symptomatic hyper- and hypoglycemia.
    • Targets are individualized, not one-size-fits-all.
    • Most patients with T1DM or T2DM should aim for A1c ~7.0%.
    • Most won't hit ≤ 7.0% — but the risk relationship is continuous, so any reduction (e.g. 9.5% → 8.5%) still helps.
    • Weigh tighter control (less structural complication risk) against hypoglycemia risk, cost, and feasibility, together with the patient.
    • Limited life expectancy (< ~2 years): favor liberal targets aimed mainly at avoiding symptomatic hyper-/hypoglycemia.
    • Hypoglycemia is the main danger of insulin/secretagogues — severe lows can be fatal. Allow a higher target (up to 8.5%) with hypoglycemia unawareness, recurrent lows, or comorbidities that make hypoglycemia especially dangerous (dementia, seizure disorder, frailty, fall risk).

    Applying the Principles: Three Cases

    Case 1 — 35M, Type 2 DM × 2 Years

    Hypertension on amlodipine; metformin monotherapy for diabetes; no retinopathy/neuropathy/nephropathy.

    Target A1c: ≤ 6.5%. Young, healthy, short disease duration, no complications yet to weigh against, and low hypoglycemia risk (not on an agent that causes it) — tight control is both achievable and worth it here.

    Case 2 — 52F, Type 1 DM × 30 Years

    CAD, hypertension, dyslipidemia, neuropathy; on multiple daily insulin injections; active, functional, no hypoglycemia unawareness or history of severe lows.

    Target A1c: ≤ 7.0%. Longstanding disease and existing complications don't automatically loosen the target — she's still functional with no frailty or elevated hypoglycemia risk, so the standard target still applies.

    Case 3 — 85F, Type 2 DM with Severe Dementia

    Dependent for all ADLs/IADLs; regimen includes gliclazide (a secretagogue) plus metformin, canagliflozin, and weekly semaglutide; daughter administers all medications.

    Target A1c: 7.1–8.5%. Dementia, functional dependence, and a hypoglycemia-causing agent all point toward liberalizing the target — the risk of a severe low now outweighs the long-term benefit of tighter control.
