    Approach to Endocrine Disease

    The Framing Question

    Does my patient have an endocrine problem? Every endocrine gland problem breaks down the same way — think about structure, then think about function. It can sometimes be both.

    Structure vs. Function

    Adrenal Glands (example)
    Structure — Masses
    Benign: adenoma (functioning or non-functioning), myelolipoma, "all the rest"
    Malignant: metastases to adrenal, malignant pheochromocytoma, adrenal cortical carcinoma
    Function
    Failure/insufficiency: ↓ cortisol & aldosterone ("Addison's"), CAH, androgens, catecholamines
    Overproduction: cortisol (Cushing syndrome), aldosterone (1° hyperaldosteronism), catecholamines (pheochromocytoma), androgens

    Eponyms worth knowing cold

    EponymMeans
    Cushing DiseaseACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma
    Addison DiseaseAdrenal cortex destruction → primary adrenal failure
    Conn SyndromeAldosterone-secreting adrenal adenoma

    Note the distinction: Cushing Disease is a specific pituitary cause, while Cushing Syndrome is the umbrella term for any cause of cortisol excess.

    Structural Problems: Masses

    Two questions for any mass: benign or malignant? and, if benign, where is it, and what does it press on? A pituitary mass makes this concrete — the local effects depend entirely on which direction it grows:

    Direction of growthStructure affectedClinical finding
    Superior (optic chiasm)Optic chiasmAssess visual fields — classic finding is bitemporal hemianopsia or bitemporal superior quadrantopsia
    Lateral (cavernous sinus)CN III (oculomotor)Assess EOMs. Complete palsy: dilated non-reactive pupil, eye "down and out," ptosis
    CN IV (trochlear)Difficulty moving the affected eye down while adducted
    CN VI (abducens)Difficulty with lateral movement of the affected eye
    CN V1 / V2 (trigeminal)Altered sensation of forehead/cheek on the affected side
    Inferior (sphenoid sinus)Sphenoid sinusCSF rhinorrhea

    Functional Problems: The HPA Axis as a Model

    Every functional endocrine problem is a feedback loop. Take the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis:

    Systemic stress (cold, hypoglycemia, fear, etc.)
    Hypothalamus → CRH
    Anterior pituitary → ACTH
    Adrenal cortex → Cortisol

    Cortisol then negative-feedbacks both the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary to shut the axis back down.

    What does cortisol actually do?

    SystemEffect
    Glucose↑ hepatic glucose production, gluconeogenesis, glycogen synthesis; inhibits glucose uptake/use in peripheral tissue
    CardiovascularMineralocorticoid effects (K⁺ out / Na⁺ in) — maintains BP
    Fat↑ lipolysis, central fat deposition
    BoneInhibits osteoblasts, activates osteoclasts, ↓ GI calcium absorption
    MuscleSkeletal muscle catabolism
    Connective tissue / skinInhibits fibroblasts, ↓ collagen synthesis
    Immune functionAnti-inflammatory (↓ IFN, IL-12, TNF)

    Stimulation & Suppression Tests: A Reality Check

    • Dynamic testing isn't always needed — diagnosis often comes from history, exam, imaging, and a "static" hormone level alone.
    • There are many suppression/stimulation tests, and many ways to get them wrong.
    • Deciding which test to order — and running it accurately and safely — isn't expected of a generalist, with two exceptions:
    TestUsed to screen for
    1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression testCushing syndrome (hormone excess → try to suppress it)
    250 µg ACTH stimulation testAcute/subacute adrenal failure (hormone deficiency → try to stimulate it)

    General rule: suspect a deficiency → stimulation test. Suspect excess → suppression test.

    Where Does This All End Up? Treatment 101

    Endocrine gland problem
    Structure
    Benign → don't surgically remove unless it's causing local problems
    Malignant → surgically remove
    Function
    Hyperfunction → suppress/ablate, or surgically remove
    Hypofunction → replace the missing hormone(s)
