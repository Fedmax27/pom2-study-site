Stabilization of the Acutely Ill Patient

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the elements of the primary survey for a critically ill or injured patient.
  • List signs of airway compromise and know when definitive airway management is needed.
  • Distinguish respiratory distress from respiratory failure.
  • Describe stabilization techniques for a patient in respiratory distress.
  • List the signs of shock and describe stabilization measures.
  • Explain how shock treatment changes depending on the underlying cause.

The ABCDs, Under Pressure

A critically ill patient presents with a derangement somewhere in the ABCDs: airway, breathing, circulation, or disability. These aren't equally urgent — airway obstruction is generally the most rapidly lethal, followed by respiratory failure, then shock — so the primary survey is worked in that order, treating each derangement as it's found rather than waiting to finish the whole exam first. (Cardiac arrest is the one exception: current resuscitation guidelines start compressions before airway and breathing — CAB instead of ABC — because circulation to the brain and heart takes priority when there's no pulse at all.)

Before the Patient Arrives

A heads-up from EMS is a chance to assign roles. The team leader stays hands-off the patient and instead directs traffic — delegating the primary and secondary survey, airway management, vascular access, medication administration, documentation and timekeeping, and (in an arrest) CPR quality.

Equipment gets set up ahead of time using OMI:

  • Oxygen — nasal cannula, face mask, bag-valve-mask, and intubation equipment within reach.
  • Monitor — cardiac monitor, pulse oximeter, and a defibrillator.
  • IV — peripheral IV supplies, with advanced vascular access kits ready if time allows.

Airway

Assess with a quick look, listen, and feel:

  • Look — tongue, lip, or neck swelling; check the mouth for foreign bodies, loose teeth, vomitus, or secretions (look, but don't blindly finger-sweep).
  • Listen — noisy breathing is obstructed breathing. Snoring or gurgling suggests mild-to-moderate obstruction; stridor (a high-pitched inspiratory sound) suggests high-grade obstruction and is an emergency.
  • Feel — palpate the neck and chest for crepitus (pneumothorax, laryngeal injury), swelling, or masses.

When to Secure a Definitive Airway

  • High-grade airway obstruction, or obstruction that looks imminent
  • Inability to maintain or protect the airway over time
  • Severe hypoxia refractory to less invasive measures
  • Severe hypercarbia refractory to less invasive measures

LEMON — Predicting a Difficult Airway

  • Look externally — facial dysmorphism, micrognathia, obesity, dental abnormalities, a short/thick neck, or facial/neck trauma and swelling.
  • Evaluate 3-3-2 — mouth opening (3 fingerbreadths between incisors), mentum to hyoid bone (3 fingerbreadths), hyoid bone to thyroid notch (2 fingerbreadths).
  • Mallampati score — useful in a cooperative patient; based on how much of the posterior pharynx is visible.
  • Obstruction — check for a foreign body and listen for stridor.
  • Neck mobility — assess range of motion, keeping in mind trauma patients may be immobilized.

When the airway looks difficult, get backup equipment ready — including a surgical cricothyrotomy kit — and call for extra hands before giving sedatives or paralytics, not after.

Case — Airway

He's mumbling but protecting his airway — no stridor, no drooling, no swelling. No immediate airway intervention is needed, though his mental status is worth watching closely as things progress.

Breathing

Assess with a look, listen, count, and monitor:

  • Look — respiratory effort (rate, depth, accessory muscle use, tripod positioning) and overall work of breathing.
  • Listen — confirm bilateral breath sounds and note their quality (wheezing, crackles).
  • Count — take the respiratory rate yourself; documented rates are frequently inaccurate.
  • Monitor — oxygen saturation under 96% is abnormal; under 90% is potentially life-threatening.

An apneic or agonally breathing patient needs assisted ventilation right away — a bag-valve-mask hooked up to 10–15 L/min of oxygen. BVM ventilation is for apneic or agonal patients only, never for someone breathing spontaneously on their own.

If bagging isn't going well: optimize the patient's position (sniffing position, shoulder roll if needed), optimize your own position (stand squarely at the head of the bed), switch to a two-person technique, and add an oropharyngeal airway.

Noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (CPAP/BiPAP) improves oxygenation, reduces the work of breathing, recruits collapsed alveoli, and redistributes fluid — it's been shown to reduce the need for intubation in COPD and pulmonary edema. It's contraindicated in a patient who can't protect their own airway, and it needs a tight-fitting mask to work. High-flow nasal cannula delivers warmed, humidified oxygen up to 60 L/min and is effective for refractory hypoxia, but it needs dedicated equipment — it isn't a standard cannula turned up high.

Circulation & Shock

Shock is a physiologic state of inadequate oxygen delivery relative to tissue metabolic demand. Its findings are often subtle before blood pressure drops:

  • Heart rate — tachycardia is a common early compensatory response, though it may be blunted by cardiac disease or medications like beta-blockers.
  • Pulse pressure — a sympathetically driven vasoconstriction narrows it in compensated shock.
  • Respiratory rate — tachypnea is a common response to tissue-level hypoxemia.
  • Blood pressure — hypotension is a late sign; shock can exist well before it appears, and a patient's usual baseline matters.
  • Mental status — alert or anxious in compensated shock; confused or obtunded once decompensated.
  • Exam — skin color (pale/cyanotic vs. pink), capillary refill and skin turgor (both should resolve in under 2 seconds), pulse strength, extremity temperature, and mucous membrane moisture.

Four Classes of Shock

Hypovolemic

  • Reduced circulating volume
  • Hemorrhage or GI/urinary losses

Cardiogenic

  • The pump itself fails
  • Cardiomyopathy, dysrhythmia

Distributive

  • Abnormal vasodilation/permeability
  • Sepsis, anaphylaxis

Obstructive

  • Extra-cardiac flow blockage
  • Tension pneumothorax, tamponade

Management Principles

  • Monitoring — track heart rate and blood pressure response after every intervention.
  • Vascular access — adults need two large-bore (18-gauge or larger) peripheral IVs, an intraosseous line, or a central line. IO access beats a central line for speed; central lines aren't well suited to large-volume resuscitation.
  • IV fluids — helpful in most shock types except cardiogenic (risk of pulmonary edema). Start with isotonic crystalloid boluses (normal saline or lactated Ringer's); in a fluid-sensitive patient, give 250–500 cc at a time and reassess.
  • Vasopressors — withheld in hypovolemic/hemorrhagic shock (volume is the treatment) and in obstructive shock (fix the obstruction instead); used early in cardiogenic shock (norepinephrine first-line, for inotropy and vascular tone); added in septic shock only if the response to a 30 cc/kg crystalloid bolus is inadequate; and never delayed in anaphylaxis — epinephrine goes first, before any fluid challenge.

Case — Circulation

Hypotensive and tachycardic with cool, mottled skin below the knee — findings consistent with shock. Two large-bore IVs go in, blood cultures are drawn, and a 30 mL/kg lactated Ringer's bolus starts while broad-spectrum antibiotics are prepared. Lactate comes back elevated, reinforcing a septic source; when blood pressure doesn't adequately respond to the fluid bolus, norepinephrine is started.

Disability

Disability was covered in depth in Undifferentiated and Differentiated Patients: a quick AVPU check plus a bedside glucose, with dextrose given immediately if the patient is hypoglycemic. It's easy for this step to get lost in a busy resuscitation, but a fingerstick takes seconds and can immediately explain — or fix — an altered mental status.

Case — Disability

A repeat neuro check after the fluid bolus shows he's more alert and appropriately answering questions. Bedside glucose is 104 mg/dL, ruling out hypoglycemia as a contributor. He's admitted to the ICU for septic shock, with surgery consulted to evaluate the leg for a possible necrotizing infection.

Pearls and Pitfalls

  • The primary survey isn't a one-time pass — repeat it often, especially after every intervention.
  • Once the patient is stable enough, do a thorough head-to-toe exam with full exposure; something hidden under clothing or a splint is easy to miss otherwise.
  • For a difficult airway, prepare a backup plan (including a surgical airway kit) before, not after, giving sedatives or paralytics.
  • During acute resuscitation, give high-flow oxygen without hesitation, then titrate down once the patient is stable — this isn't the moment to be conservative with oxygen.
  • Ensure real IV access for a critically ill adult — two large-bore peripheral lines at minimum.
