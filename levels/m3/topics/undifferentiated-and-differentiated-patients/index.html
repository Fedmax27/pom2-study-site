Undifferentiated and Differentiated Patients

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what makes the "undifferentiated" patient distinct from other clinical encounters.
  • Describe the ABCD framework and the ED "safety net" for a patient who looks unstable.
  • List vital sign thresholds that should prompt immediate concern.
  • Identify special considerations for elderly, pediatric, psychiatric, and chronically ill patients.

Approaching the Undifferentiated Patient

The ED sees anyone, at any hour, with any complaint — which means every new patient starts out undifferentiated: you don't yet know what's wrong, and you rarely know the person. The first job, often within moments of walking into the room, is deciding whether this patient is "sick or not sick" and whether something needs to happen right now. That distinction can be obvious or genuinely subtle, and when it's unclear, the right move as a student is to loop in your supervising physician immediately rather than sit with the uncertainty.

Signals That Something Is Wrong

  • Abnormal vital signs
  • Altered mental status — either depressed consciousness or unexplained agitation
  • Physician "gestalt": the sense that something doesn't add up, even before you can say why

Be a Medical Pessimist

A useful default stance: don't assume a benign cause until the dangerous ones have been actively ruled out. In practice, this often means opening an "ED safety net" early — starting cardiac monitoring, placing an IV, and giving oxygen if there's any real chance the patient could deteriorate — while the workup is still in progress.

ABCD, Fast

  • Airway — is it patent?
  • Breathing — is oxygenation and respiratory effort adequate?
  • Circulation — are blood pressure and perfusion adequate?
  • Disability — check neurologic status and a bedside glucose; give dextrose if the patient is hypoglycemic.

Vital sign ranges worth treating as red flags: heart rate over 120 or under 60 bpm, respiratory rate over 20 or under 10 breaths/min, systolic blood pressure under 90 mmHg, and temperature over 38°C or under 35°C. When any of these show up, stabilize first and investigate the cause second.

Pain control is part of stabilization too, not something to defer — a properly dosed analgesic shouldn't meaningfully interfere with your ability to examine the patient.

Once the Patient Looks Stable

Start with open-ended questions and let the patient describe things in their own words before narrowing down with targeted follow-up questions. Build a differential that leads with the most dangerous plausible diagnoses first ("worst first") rather than the most likely one — chest pain, for instance, should make you think through acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, pneumothorax, and esophageal rupture before settling on something more benign. Narrowing the differential too early is a real risk: don't fixate on the chief complaint alone. An older diabetic patient with nausea and vomiting could just as easily be having an MI or DKA as a stomach bug — keeping multiple possibilities open guards against premature closure. In practice, history-taking, exam, testing, and treatment often happen in parallel rather than as a strict sequence, and the patient's response to an intervention can itself become useful diagnostic information.

Differentiated Patients: Populations That Need a Different Lens

Some patients need their evaluation adjusted for who they are, not just what they present with.

Elderly Patients

Older patients carry a higher burden of cardiac, pulmonary, vascular, and neurologic disease, are often on multiple interacting medications, and have less physiologic reserve to buffer acute illness. History can be limited by memory issues, and disease can present atypically — a myocardial infarction with vague symptoms, or a serious infection without fever or an elevated white count. Weakness and confusion in an elderly patient are broad, genuinely nonspecific complaints that deserve a wide differential. Getting collateral history from family, prior records, or a nursing facility is often worth the effort, and the threshold for further testing should be lower when the story is vague. Before discharge, confirm the patient actually has the support and follow-up access they'll need at home.

Pediatric Patients

Preverbal children depend entirely on a caregiver's account, and even older children may struggle to describe symptoms accurately under stress. Infants remain a higher-risk group for serious bacterial infection, and possible abuse or neglect should stay on the differential for any injury that doesn't quite fit the story given. Where possible, examine young children with a caregiver present to reduce anxiety (unless abuse is suspected), while giving adolescents a chance to speak privately about sensitive topics. Children are not simply small adults: the diseases you'd worry about, the equipment you'd use, and the doses you'd give all shift substantially across infancy, early childhood, and adolescence.

Patients With a Psychiatric History

A known psychiatric history makes it tempting to attribute a new complaint to it — resist that until organic causes have been reasonably excluded. Many psychiatric medications carry meaningful toxicity and side-effect risks of their own, and medication nonadherence can itself worsen a patient's baseline condition. Separating a functional presentation from an organic one is often genuinely difficult, not a quick judgment call.

Patients With Chronic Conditions

Chronic disease — heart failure, COPD, diabetes, hypertension, end-stage renal disease, and similar conditions — is a routine part of the ED patient population. Even when these patients look stable, their history is often complex enough to warrant real skepticism. Useful habits: stabilize first if there's any acute concern, review past medical/surgical history in detail, pull records or call the primary care physician when it would change management, ask whether the current complaint is a new problem or an exacerbation of the known one, and reconcile medications carefully to avoid introducing a new error on top of an existing regimen.

Adjust the Lens: Differentiated Populations

Elderly

  • Atypical, blunted presentations
  • Less physiologic reserve
  • Lower threshold for testing
  • Confirm support before discharge

Pediatric

  • History depends on caregiver
  • Not just "small adults"
  • Consider abuse/neglect
  • Age-specific dosing & equipment

Psychiatric History

  • Exclude organic causes first
  • Meds carry real toxicity risk
  • Functional vs. organic is hard

Chronic Conditions

  • Stay skeptical even if "stable"
  • New problem vs. exacerbation?
  • Reconcile meds carefully
  • Loop in the PCP when useful
