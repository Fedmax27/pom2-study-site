Approach to the Undifferentiated Patient

Learning Objectives

  • List the steps in a systematic approach to the undifferentiated patient in the ED.
  • Explain why the life-threatening differential takes priority in the initial evaluation.
  • Describe the components of the primary survey and the secondary survey.
  • Apply pretest probability and clinical decision tools when choosing diagnostic tests.

Introduction

The emergency department differs from an outpatient clinic or an inpatient floor in three important ways: time is limited, resources are limited, and the patient is usually a stranger who believes something is acutely wrong. The table below captures the contrast.

SettingTime availablePatient familiarityPresumed acuity
Emergency DepartmentMinutesUsually a strangerAssumed urgent until proven otherwise
Outpatient clinicA scheduled visitOften an established patientUsually non-urgent
Inpatient wardHours to daysKnown from admissionAlready worked up

At triage, a nurse records the chief complaint, vital signs, past medical and surgical history, medications, allergies, and a brief history of present illness, then assigns an acuity level using the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) — a five-level scale created in 1998 by Richard Wuerz and David Eitel, running from Level 1 (resuscitation / critical, coded red) down to Level 5 (very minor, coded blue). That triage level tells you how urgently to move, but it doesn't replace your own assessment once you walk in the room.

A Step-by-Step Framework

Step 1 — Initial Actions and the Primary Survey

Within the first minute, form a gestalt of whether the patient is stable or unstable using an ABCDE scan:

  • Airway — can they speak clearly? Any facial or oropharyngeal swelling, muffled or high-pitched voice, or other signs of impending airway compromise?
  • Breathing — is the rate too fast or too slow? Any grunting, wheezing, or stridor?
  • Circulation — palpate a radial pulse (carotid if radial is absent); check rate, regularity, skin color, and blood pressure; obtain IV or IO access if it looks warranted.
  • Disability — is mental status normal enough to give a history? Grade it with AVPU (Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive); check for facial asymmetry or a focal motor deficit.
  • Exposure — undress the patient fully, including under splints, wraps, and medication patches, to look for anything hidden from view.

While support staff set up, take a rapid AMPLE history: Allergies, Medications, Past medical/surgical history, Last oral intake, and Events leading up to arrival. Then decide whether any immediate intervention is needed — IV fluids for hypotension, oxygen for hypoxia, pressure for hemorrhage, glucose or naloxone for altered mental status.

This is also the point where you classify the patient as "sick" (unstable, in imminent danger) or "not sick." Severely abnormal vitals, a concerning general appearance (diaphoretic, pale, altered), or any finding demanding immediate action all point toward "sick." For a sick patient, start the ED safety net right away — vascular access, cardiac monitor, and supplemental oxygen (shorthand: IV–O2–monitor) — because in an unstable patient, history-taking, exam, diagnostics, and treatment all happen together rather than one after another.

Case — Primary Survey

She's alert, speaking in full sentences, breathing comfortably, and her pulse is strong and regular. Uncomfortable, but not unstable — this patient is "not sick." No known allergies. A first dose of IV fentanyl is given for pain before the history continues.

Step 2 — Build an Initial "Can't-Miss" Differential

Before anything else, list the dangerous diagnoses that fit the presentation — the ones you cannot afford to miss — ahead of the more common, less dangerous ones.

Case — Initial Differential for RUQ Pain

Abdominal aortic aneurysm · mesenteric ischemia · acute coronary syndrome · bowel obstruction · perforated viscus · biliary disease (cholecystitis/cholangitis) · GI bleed · appendicitis.

Step 3 — Secondary Survey

Give the patient one or two uninterrupted minutes to describe what brought them in, then narrow down with focused questions. A useful pain-history mnemonic is COLD READ: Character, Onset, Location, Duration, Radiation, Exacerbating/alleviating factors, Associated symptoms, and what's Different today. Add pertinent positives and negatives aimed at your differential (for RUQ pain: nausea, sweating, prior clots, recent travel, sick contacts, vomiting, fever), then a focused review of systems, past medical/surgical/social/family history, current medications, and allergies. An important habit here: even when a patient arrives with someone else's working diagnosis already attached, evaluate them as undifferentiated all over again — borrowing a prior provider's conclusion risks anchoring bias. Trust, but verify.

Case — Secondary Survey

The pain is a dull ache in the right upper quadrant that started two days ago, comes and goes, radiates to the epigastrium, and was worse this morning with associated nausea — no vomiting, fever, chest pain, shortness of breath, black stools, or urinary symptoms. She's a longtime smoker, had a hysterectomy 15 years ago, and takes an antihypertensive and a cholesterol-lowering medication. On exam: vitals unchanged, mild distress, no scleral icterus, regular heart sounds with no murmurs, clear lungs, soft non-tender abdomen with a negative Murphy's sign, normal rectal exam with a negative fecal occult blood test, and a normal neurologic exam.

Step 4 — Refine the Differential

Use the secondary survey to narrow the list — drop what the history and exam argue against, and re-rank what's left by both likelihood and danger.

Case — Refined Differential

Still active: AAA, mesenteric ischemia, ACS, biliary colic, GERD, peptic ulcer, hepatitis, kidney stone. Downgraded by the benign exam: bowel obstruction, perforated viscus, active GI bleed, surgical biliary disease, appendicitis.

Step 5 — Order Tests With a Purpose

Choose tests that will rule out life threats and rule in the more likely diagnoses on your list, guided by pretest probability and any relevant clinical decision tools. Know before you order what you'll do with each result — tests confirm or exclude items already on your differential; they shouldn't be used to build the differential in the first place.

Case — Tests Ordered

CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, lipase, urinalysis, lactate, ECG, and an abdominal ultrasound, with CT held in reserve. Thirty minutes after the first dose of fentanyl, the pain has increased rather than improved.

Step 6 — Re-evaluate

EM is dynamic: patients evolve while they're in front of you. Any new symptom, any change in pain, mental status, or vital signs should trigger a fresh look — and a fresh look at the differential. Has "not sick" become "sick"?

She's now diaphoretic and tearful despite pain medication, still localizing pain to the RUQ though the abdominal exam is unchanged and benign. The new bradycardia paired with worsening pain reprioritizes the differential toward ACS, AAA, and mesenteric ischemia. A bedside ECG is obtained immediately.

The ECG shows an inferior STEMI — ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF with reciprocal depression in aVL, and additional ST elevation in V1–V2 suggesting right ventricular involvement. The cardiac catheterization lab is activated, aspirin is given, nitroglycerin is avoided (it can drop preload-dependent blood pressure in an inferior/RV MI), a crash cart is brought to bedside, and the ultrasound order is cancelled.

Step 7 — Treatment and Disposition

Every ED patient leaves through one of three doors: admitted, transferred, or discharged. This decision carries extra weight for high-risk complaints like chest pain, abdominal pain, headache, shortness of breath, and altered mental status.

Case — Disposition

She's transferred to interventional cardiology for emergent catheterization.

When a discharge is appropriate, a safe one covers four things: a plain-language summary of findings and the treatment plan; a concrete, timely follow-up plan (a call to the primary care physician can improve compliance); specific return precautions describing what should bring the patient back; and any other instructions about medications, activity, or driving.

Step 8 — Pearls and Pitfalls

  • Common diseases can present in uncommon ways — this patient's "gallbladder pain" was, on closer questioning, exertional: it started and eased with walking, a detail that only came out once someone asked directly about exertion and relief with rest.
  • The diagnosis is usually buried in the history; the physical exam mostly adds confirmatory evidence rather than revealing it outright.
  • General questions about what makes symptoms better or worse aren't always enough — ask specifically about food, exertion, and rest.
  • Information from an outside provider is useful context, not a verdict — "sent for a gallbladder workup" can quietly steer you away from ACS if you let it.
  • Every test has limits. An ECG is a snapshot, not a video; a normal one at triage doesn't rule out a process that's still evolving, which is exactly why repeating it after a clinical change matters.

Pretest Probability and Test Characteristics

Good emergency decision-making blends clinical gestalt, history, exam, vital signs, and an honest understanding of what a given test can and can't tell you. Clinical decision tools formalize part of this. Wells' Criteria for pulmonary embolism is a widely used example:

CriterionPoints
Clinical signs/symptoms of DVT3
PE is the #1 diagnosis, or equally likely3
Heart rate > 100 bpm1.5
Immobilization ≥3 days or surgery in the last 4 weeks1.5
Prior objectively diagnosed PE or DVT1.5
Hemoptysis1
Malignancy (treated within 6 months, or palliative)1

A score of 4 or below is low pretest probability — a negative D-dimer is enough to exclude PE. A score above 4 is high pretest probability — the post-test probability stays too high even after a negative D-dimer, so imaging is needed regardless.

Conclusion

Patients in the ED often present with complaints that are vague, misleading, or both, carrying a very reasonable fear that something is acutely wrong. The job is to actively hunt for the emergent diagnosis, not just the likely one — think "worst first," keep life threats on the differential until they're genuinely excluded, move through the history and exam thoroughly but efficiently, respect what your tests can't tell you, and advocate for the patient's best interest throughout.
