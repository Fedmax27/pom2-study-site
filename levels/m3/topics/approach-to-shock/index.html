Approach to Shock

Learning Objectives

  • Define shock.
  • Describe a clinically useful categorization of shock mechanisms.
  • Develop a diagnostic plan for a patient with undifferentiated shock.
  • Discuss empiric management strategies based on the suspected etiology.

What Is Shock?

Shock is a pathophysiologic state in which oxygen delivery to body tissues fails to meet metabolic demand, leading to end-organ dysfunction. It's fundamentally a perfusion problem, not a blood-pressure problem — as covered in the previous topic, hypotension is a late finding, and a patient can be in shock well before their blood pressure drops. The job at the bedside is to recognize the state early, categorize its likely mechanism, and start treating in parallel with the workup rather than waiting for a final diagnosis.

Categorizing Shock

The four categories from the previous topic cover most cases, but a fifth catch-all is worth keeping in mind for the ones that don't fit cleanly:

CategoryPhysiologyKey Examples
HypovolemicDecreased intravascular volume reduces preload and cardiac outputHemorrhagic (trauma, GI bleed); non-hemorrhagic (vomiting, diarrhea, burns)
CardiogenicImpaired pump function, rhythm, or structureArrhythmia, MI, heart failure, valvular disease
DistributivePathologic vasodilation causes relative volume depletionSepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock, toxins
ObstructiveA mechanical or pulmonary-vascular block to flowPE, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade

Working Up Undifferentiated Shock

Initial Actions

  • Rapid airway, breathing, and circulation assessment
  • Robust IV access — two large-bore lines, or an intraosseous line if peripheral access fails
  • Continuous monitoring: telemetry, blood pressure, pulse oximetry
  • ECG and chest X-ray
  • Bedside ultrasound (a FAST exam can look for free fluid, an enlarged IVC, or a pericardial effusion)
  • Start IV fluid resuscitation while the workup continues, not after it

History Clues by Suspected Cause

  • Hemorrhagic — trauma history, or a known or suspected bleeding source
  • Non-hemorrhagic hypovolemic — poor intake, diarrhea, vomiting, high ostomy output
  • Cardiogenic / obstructive — chest pain, dyspnea, leg swelling, syncope
  • Anaphylactic — sudden itching, hives, or swelling after an exposure
  • Septic — fever, cough, dysuria, abdominal or flank pain, rigors

Exam Findings

The classic picture is altered mental status, thready and fast pulses, tachypnea, and skin that's cool, pale, and slow to refill, with dry mucous membranes. Distributive shock is the exception — extremities can be inappropriately warm despite significant hypotension. Cardiogenic and obstructive shock may add dependent edema, a new murmur, or jugular venous distention.

Case — Exam

Her neck veins are flat and her extremities are warm, not cool — the opposite of what you'd expect from hypovolemic or cardiogenic shock. That combination points toward distributive shock, and is a useful contrast to the cold, mottled leg from the septic case in the previous topic: sepsis can present either way.

Diagnostic Testing

  • Core labs — CBC with differential, electrolytes, BUN/creatinine, glucose, lactate, coagulation studies, hepatic panel, calcium, urinalysis, a pregnancy test, and a blood gas.
  • Lactate is worth calling out specifically — it's a marker of anaerobic metabolism and a useful proxy for how badly perfusion is mismatched with demand.

Imaging is useful, but it shouldn't come before stabilization — deferring a CT in favor of empiric treatment is often the right call until the patient is stable enough to leave the resuscitation bay.

Case — Diagnostics

A crystalloid bolus, labs, blood cultures, and imaging all start in parallel. Lactate comes back at 4.5 mmol/L, and a urinalysis shows pyuria with gram-negative rods — a urinary source for sepsis.

Treatment Principles

Address any critical airway or breathing finding immediately, and maximize oxygen saturation. If intubation is being considered, remember that both sedatives and positive-pressure ventilation can drop preload and worsen hypotension in a shocked patient — proceed, but with that risk anticipated rather than discovered.

  • Vascular access & fluids — a short, large-bore IV (or an IO line if peripheral access fails) and a rapid crystalloid bolus (normal saline or lactated Ringer's), except in cardiogenic shock, where fluid can push the patient into pulmonary edema.
  • Blood products — indicated in hemorrhagic shock, especially trauma; minimizing crystalloid in favor of blood limits dilution and hyperchloremic acidemia.
  • Vasopressors — norepinephrine or epinephrine when volume resuscitation alone isn't correcting the hypotension (see the previous topic for which shock types call for vasopressors early versus not at all).

Case — Treatment

Empiric antibiotics start right away. When hypotension persists despite the fluid bolus, a norepinephrine infusion is added.

Is It Working?

Signs the resuscitation is actually succeeding, not just that time has passed:

  • Heart rate, blood pressure, and urine output trending back toward normal
  • Lactate clearing — falling toward or below 2 mmol/L
  • Restored intravascular volume status
  • Resolving acidosis and normalizing metabolic parameters

Disposition & Prognosis

Patients in shock need ICU-level care under intensivist supervision. Prognosis varies widely by cause — anaphylactic shock that responds to epinephrine carries minimal mortality, while septic shock carries a mortality around 50%, which is a useful reminder that the category matters as much as the fact of being in shock at all.

Case — Disposition

She's admitted to the ICU, improves steadily over the next day, and is eventually transferred to a general medical floor.

Pearls and Pitfalls

  • The clinical picture can be subtle — a normotensive patient can still be in shock.
  • Shock demands prompt, aggressive treatment in the emergency department; it isn't something to manage cautiously while waiting for more data.
  • Knowing the underlying pathophysiology is what lets you choose the right empiric therapy before the diagnosis is fully confirmed.
  • Don't delay resuscitation to wait for a diagnostic test to result — treat and diagnose at the same time.
