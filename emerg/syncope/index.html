    Syncope

    A transient loss of consciousness and postural tone from brief global cerebral hypoperfusion. Most causes are benign. The job is to find the few that are cardiac, which is what kills, and the history does most of that work.

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • First, is it syncope? Rule out seizure (and rarities like cataplexy). Brief jerking can happen in any syncope. Prolonged convulsions with a postictal period mean seizure.
    • Three mechanisms: reflex (vasovagal, situational, carotid sinus), orthostatic (volume, blood loss, drugs, autonomic failure) and cardiac (dysrhythmia, outflow obstruction, MI, PE). Only the cardiac group raises mortality.
    • The danger signs: syncope during exertion, lying down, with palpitations or no prodrome; heart failure or structural heart disease; family history of sudden death; chest pain, dyspnea or an abnormal ECG.
    • Get an ECG on everyone except young, healthy patients with a clear vasovagal story. Other labs and CT head are only for specific indications.
    • Rosen: no rule beats clinical judgment, so use the rules as a framework, not a verdict. In Canada, the Canadian Syncope Risk Score has since been prospectively validated.

    1 Differentials

    Syncope causes up to 3% of ED visits, and nearly half are admitted or observed. Patients 65 and older make up 80% of those admissions. Recurrence is as high as 50%. Presyncope carries a similar mortality and is worked up the same way.

    Critical Rosen's must-consider list
    • Life-threatening dysrhythmia; MI
    • Critical aortic stenosis; HCM; tamponade
    • Massive PE
    • Thoracic dissection; AAA
    • SAH; stroke
    • Severe hypovolemia or hemorrhage; ruptured ectopic
    • Sepsis; toxic-metabolic derangement
    Emergent Cardiac and other
    • Tachy: VT, VF, SVT, WPW, torsades
    • Brady: sinus node disease, 2nd and 3rd degree AV block, pacemaker or ICD malfunction
    • Channelopathies: long QT, Brugada
    • Outflow: mitral, aortic or pulmonic stenosis; atrial myxoma; pulmonary hypertension; congenital heart disease
    • Cardiomyopathy
    • Hypoglycemia, hypoxemia (CO), seizure, drugs
    Usually benign
    • Vasovagal: emotion, pain, instrumentation
    • Situational: micturition, defecation, cough, swallowing, vomiting, Valsalva, weightlifting, after exercise
    • Carotid sinus: necktie, shaving, turning the head
    • Orthostatic: dehydration, drugs, autonomic failure
    • Hyperventilation; psychogenic; breath-holding spells (children)
    MimicHow to tell
    SeizureMay have an aura; postictal confusion; tongue biting, incontinence. Brief myoclonic jerks happen in syncope too.
    HypoglycemiaGradual onset, incomplete recovery without treatment; diabetes or insulin; sweaty, confused.
    Drop attackNot syncope: no loss of consciousness. Older adults; sudden loss of tone, ataxia, vertigo.
    Vertebrobasilar TIA, subclavian stealBrought on by a change in posture or neck movement, or by using the affected arm. Look for vertigo, dysarthria, dysphagia and unequal arm pulses.
    Narcolepsy and cataplexy, psychogenicKnown history; a variable pattern; a diagnosis of exclusion.

    2 History

    The history and exam alone give the diagnosis in about 45% of patients. Get the story from witnesses too, plus the paramedics' first vitals and rhythm strip. Ask about before, during and after.

    Pacemaker or ICDDevice malfunction.
    Possible pregnancyEctopic; the patient often doesn't know she's pregnant.

    High-yield drugsQT-prolongers (amiodarone, sotalol, flecainide, procainamide, and many others), beta blockers, other antihypertensives and vasodilators (CCBs, nitrates, ACEi or ARB, PDE inhibitors, clonidine), diuretics, insulin and oral hypoglycemics, digoxin, antidepressants, antipsychotics (phenothiazines), anticonvulsants, benzodiazepines, opioids, cholinesterase inhibitors, antihistamines, and alcohol, cannabis, cocaine or heroin.

    3 Physical Exam

    Focus on the cardiovascular and neurologic systems, and look for anything that explains a low-flow state.

    CheckFinding → think
    Pulse rate and rhythmTachycardia, bradycardia, other dysrhythmias.
    Blood pressure; orthostatic vitals when the story fitsShock. Orthostasis from hypovolemia or drugs. Orthostatic vitals help only when standing reproduces the symptoms with a significant HR rise or BP drop; they are unreliable as a measure of volume.
    Respiratory rateTachypnea suggests hypoxia, PE or hyperventilation.
    TemperatureSepsis, causing volume depletion.
    SkinPallor, sweating: poor perfusion.
    HeadTrauma. Papilledema means raised ICP. Ketones on the breath mean ketoacidosis.
    NeckCarotid bruit (cerebrovascular disease); JVD (right heart failure, tamponade, PE).
    LungsCrackles, wheeze: infection or left heart failure.
    HeartSystolic murmur: aortic stenosis or HCM. A rub suggests pericarditis or tamponade.
    AbdomenA pulsatile mass means AAA.
    Rectal exam (if anemia or GI bleed suspected)Gross blood or melena.
    PelvisBleeding or adnexal tenderness: ectopic, anemia.
    Both arms' pulses and BPUnequal: subclavian steal or thoracic dissection.
    Neurologic examAltered mental status or focal findings: seizure, stroke, other primary neurologic disease.
    Carotid sinus massageSafe, and occasionally revealing, when the history suggests carotid sinus hypersensitivity.

    4 Investigations

    TestWhen · what it shows
    The one test for nearly everyone
    12-lead ECGYield 2–9% (lower under 40 with no heart disease), but cheap and non-invasive. Skip it only for young, healthy patients with a clear vasovagal story. Look for:

    Even after a history, exam and ECG, up to 50% have no firm diagnosis. That is why the next section is about risk, not diagnosis.

    5 Decision Rules

    The approach (Rosen's algorithm)

    1
    Young, no comorbidities, a typical story, no alternative?

    This is neurally mediated. An exam for anemia or another benign contributor is all they need.

    2
    Everyone else: ECG

    Abnormal: a dysrhythmia needs prolonged monitoring; ischemia needs a cardiac workup. Consider that workup even with a normal ECG in at-risk patients.

    3
    Normal ECG: orthostatic changes?

    Present: check the hemogram for anemia or hemorrhage, and look for volume depletion or a drug.

    Absent, so follow the clues. A reflex story: neurally mediated (carotid massage if it fits). Venous return: β-hCG and echo or US (pregnancy, tamponade). Outflow: CTA and echo (PE, dissection, stenosis, HCM). Focal neuro signs: CT head (stroke, TIA, SAH). Metabolic: chemistry (hypoglycemia, adrenal insufficiency). Seizure-like: EEG.

    Admit Rosen's hard indications
    • HxChest pain, unexplained shortness of breath, history of CHF, or significant valvular disease
    • ECGVentricular dysrhythmia, ischemia, a significantly prolonged QT, or a new bundle branch block
    Consider monitoring for age > 65, known cardiovascular or congenital heart disease, family history of sudden death, serious comorbidity (e.g. diabetes), or exertional syncope.
    Markers of short-term risk Rosen's list, drawn from the published rules
    Patient
    • Age > 65; male
    • CHF; cardiovascular or structural heart disease; serious dysrhythmia
    • Family history of sudden death before 50
    Event and findings
    • No prodrome; exertional; supine
    • Dyspnea
    • SBP < 90
    • Abnormal ECG
    • Hct < 30% (Hb < 90 g/L)
    Rosen's caution: no prediction rule has performed acceptably in rigorous validation, or shown itself better than an emergency physician's gestalt. Don't apply any of them strictly or alone. The San Francisco Syncope Rule's “CHESS” items (CHF, Hct < 30%, abnormal ECG, shortness of breath, SBP < 90) are all on this list.

    Disposition

    Usually home
    • Men < 45, women < 55 with no worrisome symptoms, signs or ECG findings: very low risk
    • A clear non-emergent cause (vasovagal, orthostatic): outpatient, usually primary care
    • Driving: recurrence at the wheel is < 1% and long-term mortality matches age- and sex-matched peers, so Rosen generally doesn't restrict driving after discharge. Local licensing and reporting rules may be stricter.
    Monitor or observe
    • Emergent diagnoses: telemetry, or an ED observation unit that can do an expedited cardiac workup (stress test)
    Admit
    • Critical diagnoses: stabilize, then ICU or an inpatient unit
    • Any of the hard indications above
