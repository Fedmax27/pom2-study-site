    Cyanosis

    Blue skin means too much deoxygenated or abnormal hemoglobin in the capillaries. Sort it into central or peripheral, give oxygen, and watch what the oxygen does: the response is the main diagnostic clue.

    If You Remember Nothing Else
    • Cyanosis depends on the absolute amount of deoxygenated Hb (about 5 g/dL), not on a percentage. Anemic patients turn blue much later. Its absence doesn't rule out hypoxia.
    • Central (lips, tongue, conjunctivae) means low arterial saturation, a shunt or an abnormal hemoglobin. Peripheral (nail beds, extremities) means slow flow of normally oxygenated blood.
    • Give high-flow O2 first and note the response. Improvement suggests a diffusion problem. No improvement suggests a shunt or V/Q mismatch, or an abnormal hemoglobin.
    • Methemoglobinemia: chocolate-brown blood, SpO2 stuck around 85%, no response to O2. Common culprits are benzocaine and other local anesthetics, dapsone and aniline dyes. Give methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IV if symptomatic or above 30%. No response suggests sulfhemoglobin.
    • Every first episode of cyanosis, or one of uncertain cause, is admitted. In any cyanotic infant, think congenital heart disease.

    1 Differentials

    By urgency

    Critical Cyanosis plus shock or respiratory failure
    • Acute heart failure; ACS
    • Hypovolemic or cardiogenic shock
    • Acute respiratory insufficiency or failure
    • Massive PE
    • Decompensated congenital heart disease, or its first presentation in a child
    Emergent
    • Methemoglobinemia: suspect it when there's no heart or lung disease to explain the cyanosis
    • Sulfhemoglobinemia (rare)
    • SVC syndrome: central cyanosis with a swollen, plethoric face, neck and arms
    • Polycythemia with hyperviscosity
    • Arterial occlusion (embolus, thrombus)
    Usually non-emergent
    • Raynaud's phenomenon (15% of people, mostly women)
    • Cold air or water exposure
    • Chronic, stable cyanotic heart or lung disease
    • Pseudocyanosis: dyes, heavy metals, topical pigments

    By mechanism

    TypeCauses
    Peripheral: slow flow, more O2 extracted
    Low outputShock, LV failure, hypovolemia
    ArterialThrombosis, embolism, vasospasm (Raynaud's), peripheral vascular disease
    OtherCold exposure, venous obstruction, blood diverted away from the limbs
    Central: low SaO2, a shunt, or abnormal Hb
    Low inspired O2High altitude (> 8000 ft)
    LungsHypoventilation; impaired diffusion; V/Q mismatch (PE, ARDS, pulmonary hypertension); upper airway obstruction, pneumonia, tension pneumothorax, diaphragmatic hernia
    ShuntsPulmonary AV fistulae; cerebral, hepatic or peripheral AV fistulae; cyanotic congenital heart disease (VSD, tetralogy of Fallot, endocardial cushion defects, coarctation, TAPVR, hypoplastic left heart, tricuspid atresia, and others)
    Abnormal hemoglobinMethemoglobin (Fe3+, can't carry O2, shifts the curve left); sulfhemoglobin
    Too many red cellsPolycythemia vera (with splenomegaly); secondary (chronic hypoxemia < 92%, cyanotic heart disease, smoking, altitude); relative (dehydration)
    Differential cyanosis
    Upper vs lower half, or left vs rightCyanotic congenital heart disease with several anomalies.

    Causes of methemoglobinemia

    Drugs
    • Local anesthetics: benzocaine, lidocaine, prilocaine
    • Dapsone; sulfonamides (incl. sulfamethoxazole)
    • Nitrates and nitrites: nitroglycerin, nitroprusside, amyl, butyl and isobutyl nitrite
    • Phenazopyridine; phenacetin
    • Chloroquine, primaquine
    • Cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, flutamide
    Chemicals and food
    • Aniline dyes (shoe dyes, marking inks)
    • Naphthalene (mothballs); chlorobenzene; nitrophenol; paraquat; silver nitrate; TNT
    • Nitrate-rich well water; food high in nitrates or adulterated with nitrites
    • Nitrous gases (arc welding); fire
    Inherited and infants
    • Hemoglobin M; NADH methemoglobin reductase deficiency; G6PD deficiency
    • Infants under 4 months have low reductase activity. Prematurity, low birth weight, dehydration, acidosis, diarrhea and hyperchloremia add to the risk.

    2 History

    Ask aboutWhy it matters
    The episode
    Onset, duration, time of day, previous episodesA first episode always gets admitted. Recurrent episodes point to vasospasm or chronic disease.
    Triggers: cold air or water, emotional stress, altitude, exertionCold or stress suggests Raynaud's. Altitude or exertion in someone with cardiopulmonary disease suggests desaturation.
    Chest pain, dyspnea, confusionTissue hypoxia and ischemia, the patient who needs treatment now.
    Background
    Congenital heart disease, heart or lung diseaseThe commonest setting for cyanosis. Ask what their usual saturation is.
    Hypercoagulable statePE or arterial thrombosis.
    Family history of cyanosis or blood diseaseHereditary methemoglobinemia, hemoglobin M, G6PD deficiency.
    Exposures: the toxic causes Don't miss
    Every drug, including OTC, supplements and herbalsAsk specifically about topical anesthetic sprays (benzocaine before endoscopy), dapsone, nitrates, sulfonamides and phenazopyridine.
    Home and work chemicalsAniline and azo dyes, phenacetin, nitrates, mothballs, well water.
    Dyes, metals, pigments on the skinPseudocyanosis, which is a colour without hypoxia.
    Infants and children
    Poor feeding, sweating with feeds, lethargy, poor weight gain, breathing troubleCongenital heart disease.
    Episodic spells of cyanosis, fast breathing and agitation“Tet spells” in tetralogy of Fallot: less blood reaches the lungs and more is shunted right to left.

    3 Physical Exam

    Spotting cyanosis is unreliable. Observers disagree, and room light, temperature and skin pigmentation all change what you see. Look where it's easiest to see.

    CheckLook for → think
    Where is the blue?
    Lips, perioral skin, tongue, oral mucosa, conjunctivaeCentral cyanosis.
    Nail beds, fingers and toesPeripheral cyanosis. Check the pulses and capillary refill.
    Sharply demarcated white-then-blue digits, sometimes the ear or tongueRaynaud's.
    Face, neck and arms swollen, plethoric, with distended veinsSVC syndrome.
    Vitals and airway
    Airway, work of breathingUpper airway obstruction, respiratory insufficiency.
    BP, HR, temperatureShock states. Temperature is usually normal.
    SpO2 with peripheral pulses and refillA low reading with poor perfusion may just be poor circulation. A reading stuck near 85% suggests methemoglobin.
    Newborn RR > 60, apneic pauses, deep breathing, sweating with feedsNonspecific: lung, heart, infection, metabolic, GI or CNS. Pauses may be CNS immaturity. Think congenital heart disease.
    Head to toe
    Mental statusHypoxic or toxic injury.
    FundiDilated, tortuous veins and papilledema in cyanotic congenital heart disease.
    NeckJVD (pulmonary edema, right heart strain).
    LungsCrackles, wheeze, poor air entry.
    HeartRate and rhythm, gallops, murmurs (especially in a newborn), central pulse strength.
    AbdomenHepatosplenomegaly (polycythemia vera, right heart failure), a pulsatile mass or bruit (an aneurysm that embolizes).
    ExtremitiesPulses, refill, signs of chronic vascular disease (hair loss, a cold limb). Clubbing means chronic hypoxemia: cyanotic heart disease, endocarditis, COPD, CF, cirrhosis, Crohn's. Nail-bed hemorrhages or end-organ damage suggest a thrombotic or embolic process.

    4 Investigations

    TestWhen · what it tells you
    Bedside
    The blood itselfChocolate brown, even in room air, suggests methemoglobin. Put a drop on white filter paper and expose it to 100% O2: normal blood turns bright red, methemoglobin doesn't change.
    Pulse oximetry TrapMethemoglobin absorbs both wavelengths (660 and 940 nm), so the probe reads about 85% whatever the real PaO2.
    Blood
    ABG (ideally on room air)The real SaO2 and PaO2. A normal PaO2 with cyanosis points to an abnormal hemoglobin.
    CO-oximetryOrder it specifically for suspected methemoglobin or CO. It reports sulfhemoglobin as methemoglobin. Non-invasive pulse CO-oximetry gets less accurate below SaO2 95% and above a methemoglobin of 14%.
    CBC and smearPolycythemia, erythrocytosis, anemia; red cell shape and fragments.
    D-dimerWhen PE is a possible cause.
    Imaging and cardiac
    Chest x-rayConsolidation, effusion, edema, pneumothorax. An abnormal heart or mediastinal shadow suggests congenital heart disease.
    ECG, on every new presentationDysrhythmia, ischemia. Right axis deviation or RVH suggests cor pulmonale or pulmonary hypertension.
    EchoSeptal defects in infants; valve disease at any age.
    CT pulmonary angiogram (or Doppler, rarely V/Q)Suspected PE.
    Ankle–brachial indexPeripheral cyanosis that doesn't improve: arterial disease or occlusion.

    5 Decision Rules

    Central cyanosis: ABCs, ABG, then oxygen

    A
    Improves on O2 (PaO2 ≥ 100 mmHg): get a CXR

    Normal heart shadow, so a lung cause. An infiltrate needs antibiotics and support. Pulmonary edema from CHF needs an ECG, an echo and diuresis. With no infiltrate, consider PE, hypoventilation, polycythemia or AV fistulae, and get a CTPA. If it's positive, anticoagulate and admit to the ICU. If it's negative, check the Hct: above 65%, phlebotomy plus IV fluids; below 65%, IV fluids and admit.

    Abnormal heart shadow, so think cardiogenic. Get an ECG, echo and cardiac enzymes, support with inotropes, chronotropes or pressors, and look for the cause of the cardiogenic shock. Consult cardiology and admit. Patients with chronic cyanotic heart disease may not need the ICU, or even admission; ask their cardiologist.

    B
    No improvement (PaO2 ≤ 100 or SaO2 ≤ 70): get a CXR plus CO-oximetry, and methemoglobin, CO and cyanide levels

    In respiratory distress, look for tension pneumothorax (needle or tube), upper airway obstruction (open it, intubate, surgical airway if needed), bronchospasm (beta-agonists, steroids) and PE.

    Not in distress, think of a shunt or an abnormal Hb: chronic methemoglobinemia, G6PD deficiency, sulfhemoglobin, chronic cyanotic heart disease.

    Peripheral cyanosis: ABCs, SpO2, then oxygen

    A
    Improves on O2: think of a low-output state

    Hypovolemia needs fluids and a search for the cause. Sepsis needs fluids, antibiotics, pressors and source control. Cardiogenic shock needs an echo plus inotropes, chronotropes or pressors.

    B
    No improvement: think of vascular occlusion, and warm the limb

    It improves with warming: vasospasm from cold exposure. Refer to rheumatology and consider nifedipine.

    It doesn't: peripheral vascular disease, or an arterial embolus or thrombosis. Measure ABIs and call vascular surgery. In critical limb ischemia, consider IV heparin with them, and look for an embolic source (endocarditis, an AAA).

    The oxygen test What the response to high-flow O2 tells you
    • ↑Improves: a diffusion problem.
    • –Doesn't improve: a shunt or V/Q mismatch (consolidation, right-to-left cardiac shunt) or an abnormal hemoglobin.
    • !Not in distress and still blue on 100% O2: think cardiac shunt, methemoglobin or sulfhemoglobin.
    Methemoglobinemia: who gets methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes, plus O2
    • Symptomatic hypoxia: dysrhythmia, angina, respiratory distress, seizures, coma, or
    • Methemoglobin > 30%

    First decontaminate skin exposures (e.g. aniline dye) with soap and water, with staff wearing protection. Stop the offending agent.

    No response to methylene blue suggests sulfhemoglobinemia (e.g. from phenazopyridine or sulfonamides). It is irreversible for the life of the red cell. Care is supportive: remove the cause. Transfusion or exchange has been described but not studied.
    Normal methemoglobin is < 1%. Cyanosis appears at about 1.5 g/dL, roughly 10–15% of total Hb. Admit if > 30%, or > 15% with symptoms. Methylene blue works by speeding up the NADPH (G6PD-dependent) reductase pathway.
    Other specific treatments
    • Symptomatic hyperviscosity or secondary polycythemia: phlebotomy plus isotonic fluid, aiming for a normal Hct (45%). Refer to hematology.
    • SVC syndrome: O2 and head of bed up. Look for a mediastinal mass. Stenting, radiation or chemo depending on the cause.
    • Raynaud's: warm the digits; vasodilators such as nifedipine or nitrates (limited data).
    • Congenital heart disease: involve cardiology early. These patients respond unpredictably to O2 and fluids.
    • Hypoxia causing ischemic chest pain: fix the hemoglobin problem while treating the ischemia.
    • CO and cyanide poisoning cause tissue hypoxia but usually not cyanosis.

    Disposition

    Home after several hours of ED monitoring
    • Vasospasm (peripheral cyanosis)
    • Asymptomatic methemoglobinemia < 15%, with the trigger removed
    • Stable primary lung disease

    Follow-up within 24 h unless their cyanosis is already known to be chronic. Return if the cyanosis worsens or they develop dyspnea, confusion or chest pain.

    Admit
    • Every first episode, or any uncertain cause
    • Methemoglobin > 30%, or > 15% with symptoms
    • Child with new CHF or suspected congenital heart disease: cardiology
    • Acute arterial occlusion: surgery
    • SVC syndrome: vascular, IR, oncology and radiation oncology
