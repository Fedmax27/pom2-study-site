Step 2 of 10 · Week 1

Diabetes Diagnosis & T1DM Care

Who has diabetes, which type, and how a child with type 1 is run day to day. Drugs, insulin pharmacology and DKA were Review 2.

1.1 · Pathophysiology T1DM & T2DM 1.2 · T1DM in children 1.4 · Approach to endocrine disease 1.5 · T2DM diagnosis
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • Any one of: fasting glucose ≥ 7.0, A1c ≥ 6.5% (adults; not for suspected T1DM), 2-h OGTT or random ≥ 11.1 mmol/L.
  • Symptoms (thirst, polyuria, weight loss) → one abnormal value is enough. No symptoms → repeat on another day. Acute illness or steroids can raise glucose: confirm once recovered.
  • T1DM = autoimmune β-cell loss (GAD, IA-2, insulin, ZnT8 antibodies). About 20% of β-cells are left at diagnosis, hence the honeymoon. T2DM = insulin resistance + relative deficiency.
  • A high reading reflects the dose before it. High lunch glucose → raise the breakfast rapid-acting dose.
  • Hypoglycemia: can they swallow? Oral glucose if yes, glucagon if no, recheck in 15 min.

The numbers that diagnose

Why these cut-offs? They're where retinopathy risk starts to climb steeply.

Type 1 or type 2 in an adult? Usually inferred from the course and the response to treatment. When it's genuinely unclear: high C-peptide + negative anti-GAD favours T2DM. Sudden weight loss leans T1DM; weight gain or stopping exercise leans T2DM.

Who's at risk of T2DM: Indigenous, South Asian, Asian, Hispanic and African-descent communities; previous GDM; first-degree relatives (monozygotic twin ~90%).

Basal-bolus (4 shots/day) or a pump lets meals be flexible. NPH-based 3 shots/day is simpler but locks in a meal and snack schedule. A CGM shows time in range, which two people with the same A1c can differ wildly on. DCCT: tighter control means fewer microvascular complications but more severe lows.

Fix the dose before the readingPre-lunch glucose of 14 every day? The breakfast rapid-acting dose (or the breakfast carb ratio) is too small. Bedtime high? The supper dose. Fasting high? The basal (bedtime) insulin. Changing the dose at the meal where you see the high treats the wrong insulin.

Any gland: structure or function

Every endocrine problem is a mass (benign or malignant, and what it presses on) or a hormone problem (too much or too little), sometimes both. Suspect a deficiency → stimulation test (ACTH stimulation for adrenal failure). Suspect an excess → suppression test (1 mg overnight dexamethasone for Cushing syndrome).

Step 3 of 10 · Week 1

Targets, Complications & Pregnancy

Choosing an A1c target for this patient, the non-glucose things that prevent complications, and diabetes before and during pregnancy.

1.9 · Glycemic goals 1.10 · Preventing complications 1.8 · Diabetes & pregnancy
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • A1c ≤ 7.0% for most. ≤ 6.5% if young, short duration, no complications and low hypo risk. 7.1–8.5% with dementia, frailty, falls, hypo unawareness, recurrent lows or limited life expectancy.
  • Longstanding disease with complications does not by itself loosen the target. Hypoglycemia risk is the gate.
  • BP ≤ 130/80 (ACEi/ARB first) · LDL < 2.0 (statin) · ACEi/ARB for albuminuria whatever the BP · annual eye exam · foot exam with monofilament · stop smoking.
  • Pre-existing diabetes → malformation risk tied to A1c at conception (organs form by ~8 weeks), so aim for A1c ≤ 7.0% before pregnancy, plus folic acid. GDM starts after organogenesis: no malformation risk.
  • GDM screen at 24–28 weeks (50 g). Treat with diet and walks, then insulin or metformin. Recheck with a 75 g OGTT 6 weeks–6 months postpartum.

Choosing the A1c target

Self-monitoring targets: fasting 4–7, 2 h after meals 5–10 mmol/L. A1c is the best-validated marker of complication risk, but finger-sticks or a CGM catch and confirm lows and guide insulin doses.

Any drop helps. The risk curve is continuous, so 9.5% → 8.5% is a real win even short of target.

Insulin needs may fall in the first trimester (hypo risk), climb through the second and third, then drop sharply after delivery (breastfeeding lowers glucose further).

Targets in pregnancy: fasting < 5.3, 1 h after meals < 7.8 mmol/L.

Glyburide crosses the placenta (neonatal lows), so it's a fallback. ACEi/ARBs are contraindicated, so switch them before conception. Screen the eyes before, during and after; pre-eclampsia risk doubles.

"GDM" that isn'tVery high insulin needs, or glucose that doesn't settle after delivery, suggest undiagnosed type 2 (or early type 1) that was there all along. That's why the postpartum OGTT matters.
Step 4 of 10 · Weeks 1–2

Thyroid Function

How the hormone is made, what each TSH/FT4 pattern means, splitting thyrotoxicosis by mechanism, and running levothyroxine.

1.12 · Thyroid physiology 2.1 · Thyroid dysfunction
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • TSH first. A normal TSH rules out primary thyroid disease, except when you suspect pituitary/hypothalamic disease: then order TSH + FT4.
  • Thyrotoxicosis = too much hormone from anywhere. Hyperthyroidism = the gland is making it. Radioiodine uptake splits them: high → reduce production; low (thyroiditis, exogenous) → support only.
  • Graves: methimazole first (remission possible). Baseline CBC + liver enzymes + pregnancy test; fever or sore throat = come in now (agranulocytosis). PTU in the first trimester only.
  • Levothyroxine ~1.5 mcg/kg/day, half-life 1 week: recheck TSH at 4–6 weeks. Empty stomach, 4 h from calcium/iron. Start low if elderly or cardiac.
  • Subclinical hypothyroidism: treat only if pregnant, TSH > 10, anti-TPO positive, or as a symptom trial, and stop the trial if symptoms don't change.

Making the hormone

T4 is 90% of what the gland secretes and lasts a week. T3 is 10× more potent and mostly made in the tissues by D2 (T4 → T3). D3 inactivates (reverse T3).

TBG carries most of it. Thyroid hormone raises metabolic rate, heat, heart rate and β-receptor numbers, which is why thyrotoxicosis looks adrenergic.

Same gland, opposite antibodies: Hashimoto anti-TPO blocks synthesis → hypothyroid. Graves anti-TSH-receptor stimulates → hyperthyroid.

Radioiodine uptake scan: the best single test for the cause, but 2 days and contraindicated in pregnancy. A classic Graves picture (symmetric goitre, eye disease, family history) often needs neither the scan nor TRAb.

Hypothyroidism and levothyroxine

Who to test: compatible symptoms, high cholesterol, anemia, low sodium, autoimmune disease in the patient or family, Down or Turner syndrome, past thyroid treatment, lithium or amiodarone, pregnancy.

Myxedema coma: obtundation and hypothermia, with low BP, slow HR, low sodium and glucose, hypoventilation.

Antibodies and ultrasound rarely change management in overt Hashimoto; ultrasound is for a palpable nodule.

Two levothyroxine trapsDon't recheck at 2 weeks: steady state takes about 5 weeks, so an early TSH tempts a second change before the first has landed. And a patient who loses a lot of weight on an unchanged dose can become thyrotoxic: lower the dose, recheck in 4–6 weeks.
Step 5 of 10 · Week 2

Thyroid Nodules & Cancer

What a nodule can be, why a needle can't call a follicular lesion, and the four cancers by cell of origin.

2.3 · Pathology of thyroid nodules
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • A nodule is hyperplasia (multinodular goitre, the commonest), inflammation (Hashimoto) or neoplasm. Most are benign and most patients are euthyroid.
  • Follicular adenoma vs carcinoma = invasion of the capsule or vessels. The cells look identical, so FNA can't tell them apart; the excised nodule can (hemithyroidectomy).
  • Papillary (85–90%): diagnosed on nuclear features, which FNA sees well. Lymphatic spread (a neck node can be the first sign), multifocal, excellent prognosis.
  • Follicular spreads by blood (bone, lung). Both differentiated cancers take up radioiodine and are followed with thyroglobulin.
  • Medullary = C cells: calcitonin, amyloid, RET point mutation, 25% MEN 2 → exclude pheochromocytoma before surgery. No iodine uptake. Anaplastic: older patient, fast-growing mass, usually unresectable.

"My sister has papillary cancer. Will she be OK?"The honest answer: it's malignant but usually has a very good prognosis. Not "benign" (that's the adenoma), not "aggressive" (that's anaplastic), and you can't promise it depends on more surgery or radioiodine, because that depends on the report (size, focality, invasion, extension, nodes, margins) which you haven't seen.

Treatment: surgery for FNA-positive or suspected cancer > 1 cm (hemithyroidectomy for low risk, total for high risk), node dissection if nodes are involved, radioiodine for high-risk differentiated cancer. Staging pivots on age 55: under 55, a differentiated cancer is stage I unless there are distant metastases (stage II).

Step 6 of 10 · Week 3

Growth & Puberty

The short child, the late bloomer and the early one. Each comes down to one or two measurements: growth velocity, bone age, the weight line and LH.

3.5 · Disorders of growth 3.4 · Abnormalities of puberty 3.3 · Physiology of puberty
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • Plot it and calculate the mid-parental height first: (mother + father ± 13 cm) ÷ 2, add for boys, subtract for girls. Many "short" kids are on their parents' line.
  • Growth velocity is the worry number: < 4.5 cm/yr from age 4 to puberty, or crossing percentiles. Normal velocity = a normal variant: familial (bone age = real age) or constitutional delay (bone age delayed, late-blooming parent).
  • Weight fell first → nutrition or chronic illness (celiac, Crohn's, CKD). Short and chubby → endocrine (hypothyroid, GH deficiency, steroids). Karyotype every short girl (Turner).
  • Puberty starts with thelarche in girls (8–13) and testes > 3 mL in boys (9–14). Pubic hair is adrenal, so it can't date puberty. Delayed: no signs by 13/14; high LH/FSH = gonad failed (Turner, Klinefelter), low = brain (Kallmann, CNS, functional).
  • Precocious: < 8 girls, < 9 boys. LH high = central (girls mostly idiopathic, boys need an MRI); LH low with high sex steroids = peripheral (CAH, McCune–Albright, tumour, exogenous). Central is treated with a continuous GnRH agonist.

GH deficiency: the commonest endocrine cause. Normal size at birth (fetal growth doesn't need GH); neonatal hypoglycemia, prolonged jaundice, micropenis; midline defects. Acquired: craniopharyngioma, cranial radiation, head injury.

Testing: a random GH is useless (pulses). Screen with IGF-1 and IGFBP-3, confirm by failing two stimulation tests. No insulin tolerance test in children.

GH is approved for: GH deficiency, SGA without catch-up by age 2, Turner, Prader–Willi.

Low LH/FSH: "think about brain problems." CNS tumour, trauma or radiation; Kallmann (GnRH neurons and olfactory nerves migrate together, so no sense of smell); functional causes (eating disorder, heavy exercise, chronic illness); high prolactin. Constitutional delay looks the same early on; family history and a normal growth rate point to it, and sometimes only time tells.

Precocious puberty

The testis tells you the sourceLH drives Leydig cells, which live in the testes. Both testes enlarged (≥ 4 mL) = central. Virilized with small testes = testosterone from elsewhere (CAH, adrenal tumour, exogenous). One big testis = Leydig cell tumour.

Benign variants have one sign, no growth spurt and a normal bone age: premature thelarche (6 months–2 years) and premature pubarche (6–8 years, early adrenarche).

McCune–Albright: peripheral puberty + polyostotic fibrous dysplasia + "coast of Maine" café-au-lait. Somatic Gsα activating mutation.

Why a GnRH agonist stops puberty: the pituitary only answers pulses; a constant signal down-regulates its receptors. Treat to protect final height and wellbeing.

Step 7 of 10 · Week 3

The Cycle & PMOS

How the axis switches on, the one feedback flip that makes ovulation, what's normal about a period, and PMOS (formerly PCOS).

3.3 · Puberty & the menstrual cycle 3.10 · PMOS
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • Puberty releases a brake. GABA holds GnRH down in childhood; kisspeptin on GPR54 is the essential trigger; leptin is permissive. First sign: LH pulses at night.
  • Estradiol flips from negative to positive feedback at midcycle → LH surge → ovulation ~36 h later. The luteal phase is fixed at ~14 days, so ovulation is 14 days before the next period.
  • Normal: cycles 21–35 days, up to 80 mL loss. Irregular, often anovulatory cycles are normal for ~2 years after menarche. Dysmenorrhea = prostaglandins → NSAIDs. Heavy bleeding → tranexamic acid. PMS/PMDD are defined by timing.
  • PMOS triad: hyperandrogenism + ovulatory dysfunction + mimics excluded. Ultrasound not required. Exclude: β-hCG, prolactin, TSH, 17-OHP, testosterone (> 5 nmol/L = think tumour), a partner's testosterone gel.
  • Treat what bothers her most after lifestyle: COCP or cyclic progestin (≥ 4 bleeds a year protects the endometrium), letrozole for fertility, spironolactone/cyproterone with contraception for hair. Screen glucose, lipids, BP.

Menarche is late, not early: about 2–2.5 years after thelarche. Adrenarche (pubic hair, odour, acne) runs on its own adrenal clock.

Too little fat, too little leptin: anorexia and intense training delay puberty or stop periods.

Menopause: 12 months without a period; follicles run out, so FSH climbs (primary hypogonadism). Timing tends to follow her mother's.

Insulin resistance isn't a criterion but is nearly always there: central fat, acanthosis nigricans, skin tags, sleep apnea, fatty liver. Check A1c and lipids. A normal BMI doesn't rule PMOS out.

Step 8 of 10 · Week 3

Gender-Affirming Care

The words, the diagnosis, mental health first, adult hormones and monitoring, and the three separate steps for youth.

3.8 · Transgender medicine
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • Sex, gender, gender identity and sexual orientation are four separate things. Gender dysphoria is the distress a mismatch can cause. Diagnosis is by listening: insistence, persistence, consistence.
  • Mental health first. Suicidality is 2–3× higher; a supportive family is the biggest protective factor.
  • Trans women: estradiol + anti-androgen (spironolactone → check potassium); VTE is the big risk, higher with oral than transdermal. Trans men: testosterone → check hematocrit.
  • Monitor every 3 months in year 1, then 1–2×/yr. Screen the organs that are there: Pap if there's a cervix, mammogram if breast tissue, prostate if a prostate.
  • Youth: nothing medical before puberty → blockers from Tanner 2 (fully reversible) → hormones ~16 (partly) → top surgery from 16 after a year on hormones. Discuss fertility before any of it.

Adult criteria (WPATH): persistent documented dysphoria, capacity to consent, age of majority, other health issues reasonably controlled (not excluded). Document dysphoria early in primary care; it strengthens the referral.

Step 9 of 10 · Weeks 2–3

Pelvis, Histology & Development

The clinically useful anatomy (vessels, nerves, pouches, where fluid goes), the tract down the microscope, early embryology, and how the tract forms and malforms.

3.6 · Pelvis & perineum 2.7 · Gyn histology & embryogenesis 3.7 · Female tract development
If You Remember Nothing Else
  • Uterine artery (internal iliac, in the cardinal ligament) runs over the ureter: "water under the bridge", so find the ureter before clamping. Ovarian artery comes off the aorta, in the suspensory ligament; ovarian lymph drains to para-aortic nodes.
  • Pelvic pain line: fundus, tubes, ovaries → sympathetics, T11–L1, felt in the lower abdomen and back. Cervix, upper vagina → parasympathetics, S2–S4, felt in the buttock and back of the leg. Perineum = pudendal (S2–S4).
  • Pouch of Douglas is the lowest point of the peritoneal cavity; reach it through the posterior fornix. Straddle injury → urine in the superficial pouch, up the abdominal wall, never the thighs.
  • Transformation zone = where cervical cancer starts and what a Pap must sample. Proliferative endometrium (straight glands, mitoses) = estrogen; secretory (coiled, sawtooth) = progesterone, so she ovulated.
  • No testosterone → Wolffian ducts fade (Gartner's cyst). No AMH → Müllerian ducts make tubes, uterus, cervix, upper ⅓ vagina. Imperforate hymen → hematometrocolpos. Found a Müllerian anomaly? Image the kidneys.

Folic acid before conception: the tube closes around the time a period is missed. Valproate and carbamazepine → spina bifida; rubella early → deafness, cataracts, PDA; no safe alcohol.

Why check the kidneysThe mesonephric duct sprouts the ureteric bud and guides the Müllerian duct. A faulty one can cost the kidney and the uterus on the same side, especially in didelphys and unicornuate uterus. And a teen with no periods whose menstrual blood has nowhere to go has hematometrocolpos, most often behind an imperforate hymen.

Homologues: genital tubercle → clitoris; urethral folds → labia minora; labioscrotal swellings → labia majora (scrotum). The gubernaculum becomes the ligament of the ovary and the round ligament, which runs through the inguinal canal into the labia majora.
