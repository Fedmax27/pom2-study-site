The Diabetic Whose Sugar Looked Fine

A real ED presentation, worked through one step at a time. Make the calls as you go — then read exactly what happened, and where the real team nearly got it wrong.

The Patient

28F · Emergency Department

One week of polyuria, polydipsia, poor appetite, and vomiting. Background: type 2 diabetes that started as gestational diabetes eight years ago, obesity, and a prior admission for severe hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis — a detail that's going to matter later, since it puts a second, unrelated diagnosis on the table before she's even triaged.

The key player in this case. AtorvastatinStatin — lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking HMG-CoA reductase GemfibrozilFibrate — lowers triglycerides via PPAR-alpha activation

Five drugs, three different diseases. Before a single vital sign is taken, that medication list alone tells you this history is doing a lot of the diagnostic work — hover each one if a class doesn't ring a bell.

How She Got Here

The Lead-Up

She took her dapagliflozinSGLT2 inhibitor — blocks glucose reabsorption in the kidney, independent of insulin as usual again this morning — "didn't want my sugars to spike." None of these four events looks dramatic in isolation. Stacked together — a chronic SGLT2 inhibitor, a recent infection, and days of poor intake — they're the textbook setup for exactly the diagnosis that's easiest to miss.

How an EM Physician Reads This

Tachycardia plus tachypnea with a normal oxygen saturation in a diabetic patient should trigger one specific mental branch: is this simple volume depletion, or is it respiratory compensation for a metabolic acidosis? The two can look similar on a monitor. The tell is usually the character of the breathing — deep and regular (KussmaulDeep, rapid breathing that compensates for metabolic acidosis by blowing off CO₂ respirations) points toward acidosis; shallow and rapid points more toward pain, fever, or anxiety.

Either way, this vital sign pattern by itself is reason enough to send a gas before settling on a diagnosis — not after.

Approach & Review

Reading Any Vitals Panel

This is the systematic scan worth running on every vitals panel, not just this one — keep it in your back pocket.

1
Pair them physiologically, not in isolation.

HR + BP together for circulation; RR + SpO₂ together for ventilation and oxygenation.

2
Ask what's compensating for what.

A fast HR or RR is rarely the primary problem — it's usually the body correcting for volume loss, acidosis, pain, or fever.

3
A normal number doesn't mean a normal system.

SpO₂ 98% only confirms oxygenation — it says nothing about ventilation or acid-base status.

4
Turn the pattern into a short differential

before reaching for a single diagnosis or a single test.

5
Re-check vitals after every intervention.

A repeat set is itself a diagnostic test — it tells you whether your working diagnosis is actually correct.

Checkpoint

Before Any Labs Come Back

Take thirty seconds and actually write something down before you look at the labs — the exercise only works if you commit to an answer first.

What's on your differential for a diabetic with a week of polyuria, polydipsia, poor appetite, and vomiting?
Worth having on the list: diabetic (euglycemic) ketoacidosis, starvation ketosis, gastroenteritis/dehydration, an occult infection, and — given her history — hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis. Nobody's checked a glucose yet, so DKA has to stay on the list on history alone. Notice that "her glucose is probably fine, she's on a good regimen" isn't a reason to drop it — that assumption is exactly what this case is going to test.

Definitions Matter

"Euglycemic DKA" is classically defined as ketoacidosis with a blood glucose below roughly:

The "classic" DKA threshold most people memorize (13.9 mmol/L / 250 mg/dL) describes hyperglycemic DKA. Euglycemic DKA is defined well below that — which is exactly what makes it easy to miss if the number is the first thing you check rather than the last.

What Actually Jumps Out

Scanning a panel like this, an EM physician's eye goes to the gap before the glucose. A gap of 20 with a pH of 7.27 is a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, full stop — that's true no matter what the glucose says. The glucose only tells you which flavor of ketoacidosis you might be dealing with, not whether one exists at all.

Getting that order backwards — anchoring on the glucose first, gap second — is exactly how euglycemic DKA gets missed on a busy shift.

Approach & Review

Reading Any Blood Gas

The same five-step read applies whether it's a VBG, an ABG, or any panel with a pH and an anion gap on it.

1
Look at the pH first.

Acidemic (<7.35) or alkalemic (>7.45)? That tells you which direction the primary process is pushing.

2
Find the primary disturbance.

Whichever value — pCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ — moves in the same direction as the pH abnormality is the primary problem.

3
Check compensation.

Is the other system responding by roughly the expected amount? If not, a second primary disorder is layered on top.

4
If it's a metabolic acidosis, calculate the anion gap.

Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻). Above roughly 12 means unmeasured acids are present.

5
Run the gap differential.

High gap: ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, toxic alcohols, uremia, salicylates. Normal gap: GI bicarbonate losses or renal tubular acidosis.

6
Never let a reassuring glucose skip step 4.

The gap and the ketones are what actually confirm or exclude DKA — not the glucose.

Checkpoint

Does This Rule Out DKA?

Glucose is 111 mg/dL. Bicarbonate 18, anion gap 20, pH 7.27. Does the normal-looking glucose rule out DKA?

Hyperglycemia is part of the textbook triad, not a hard requirement written into anyone's physiology. The acidosis and ketosis are the dangerous parts; the glucose is just the number most people check first out of habit.

The Twist

The Working Diagnosis

The admitting team's call: starvation ketosis. She'd barely eaten for three days — same anion gap, more comfortable label. It's not an unreasonable first guess. It's just not a complete one.

Building In a Check

One habit that catches cases like this: before signing off on any diagnosis, ask whether it explains every abnormal number in front of you, not just the convenient ones. "Starvation ketosis" explains mild ketones. It does not comfortably explain an anion gap of 20 or a pH of 7.27 — simple starvation is typically a mild, well-compensated acidosis, nowhere near this degree of derangement.

When the numbers don't fully fit the label, the usual answer is that the label is wrong, not the numbers.

Whenever a patient isn't trending the way your working diagnosis predicts, that's the system telling you to revisit the diagnosis before reaching for more of the same plan.

Approach & Review

Reading a Repeat Set of Labs

A single value is a snapshot. This is how to read the second one.

1
Never interpret one value in isolation once a repeat exists.

Direction matters as much as magnitude.

2
Ask: does this trend fit my working diagnosis and treatment?

If the patient isn't responding the way the diagnosis predicts, one of the two is wrong.

3
Separate "not better yet" from "actively worse."

A flat gap 30 minutes into fluids is different from a widening gap six hours into treatment.

4
Re-anchor if the trajectory doesn't fit.

This is usually the moment a missed diagnosis gets caught — if it's going to be caught at all.

5
Don't let one reassuring number carry the whole picture.

A slowly rising glucose means nothing if the gap and pH are moving the wrong way.

Checkpoint

Time to Confirm It

The anion gap hasn't budged in six hours of "starvation" management. What single test would directly confirm ketoacidosis?

β-hydroxybutyrateThe dominant ketone body produced during DKA — a direct, quantitative marker of ongoing ketogenesis is the ketone body that actually predominates in DKA, more so than acetoacetate — which is the only one a standard urine dipstick detects. That mismatch matters twice: a dipstick can underestimate true ketosis, and can even look worse as a patient improves, since treatment converts β-hydroxybutyrate back into acetoacetate. A direct serum level sidesteps both problems, which is why it — not a urine dip — is the test to send and to trend.

Hers came back at 5.29 mmol/L — markedly elevated (normal is under roughly 0.6 mmol/L). But getting there wasn't simple: her triglycerides had peaked at 2050 mg/dL, and the sample kept hemolyzing from the lipemia. The lab had to spin it down and remove the fatty layer before the ketone level could even be measured. Real labs don't always cooperate with the algorithm — knowing that a "pending" or "unable to process" result can itself be a clinical clue is part of the job.

Mechanism

Why the Glucose Lied

SGLT2 inhibitors work by dumping glucose into the urine — independent of insulin. That lowers blood sugar without fixing the actual problem: insulin is still relatively deficient, glucagon still dominates, and fat keeps getting broken down into ketone bodies. The two arms of this diagram run in parallel and don't know about each other — that's the whole reason the glucose can look reassuring while the acid-base picture quietly deteriorates. (See the glucose-lowering drugs lecture for how the rest of this drug class behaves.)

Knowledge Check

Beyond This Case

Besides infection and reduced intake, which is also a well-documented trigger for SGLT2-inhibitor euglycemic DKA?

This is exactly why surgical teams are now told to hold SGLT2 inhibitors for several days before elective procedures — fasting plus surgical stress reproduces the same low-insulin, low-intake setup that put this patient in the ED.

Knowledge Check

Before Any Insulin, Anywhere

In any DKA patient, a potassium below 3.3 mmol/L before starting insulin means you should:

Insulin drives potassium intracellularly. Starting it on a patient who's already hypokalemic risks a fatal arrhythmia before the acidosis itself does any more harm.

The Mental Checklist Before Insulin

Before hanging any insulin infusion in DKA — this case or any other — the same three-item check runs every time: potassium (hold below 3.3), fluids already running, and a working diagnosis that actually accounts for the acidosis in front of you. It's a small, boring habit. It's also exactly the kind of habit that keeps a fixable metabolic problem from becoming an iatrogenic cardiac one.

What Was Actually Done

Management

Why This Order

The sequence below isn't arbitrary. Insulin treats the ketoacidosis — the actual emergency — regardless of what the glucose reads, so it starts as soon as potassium is safe. The SGLT2 inhibitor gets stopped because it's the precipitant here, not an innocent bystander sitting on the med list. Everything after that is about making sure the insulin deficiency that started this doesn't get another chance.

  • Insulin infusion started — treating the ketoacidosis, not the glucose number.

Take-Home

Teaching Points

Strip away the specific numbers and this case reduces to four transferable habits — the kind worth carrying into the next shift, not just this diagnosis.

  • A "normal" glucose doesn't clear a vomiting, tachypneic diabetic on an SGLT2 inhibitor — check a gap and ketones anyway.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors should be held during acute illness, before surgery, and with reduced intake — see the glucose-lowering drugs lecture.
  • Trend the anion gap and ketones, not the glucose, to follow resolution — see acute & chronic complications.
  • Real labs have real snags — severe lipemia can block the exact test you need most, and that itself is information.
