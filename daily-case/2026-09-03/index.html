The Hypoglycemia That Kept Coming Back

A real ED presentation, worked through one step at a time. The first correction isn't the twist here — figuring out why it doesn't hold is.

Dotted, colored terms — like this — show a quick definition on hover or tap.

The Patient

78M · Found at Home

His wife finds him unusually drowsy at lunchtime — slurred, slow to respond, not himself. She calls an ambulance. Background: early-stage Alzheimer's dementiaA progressive neurodegenerative disease affecting memory and executive function — relevant here because he manages his own medications and type 2 diabetes with, by his family's account, good long-term control.

Metformin 1g twice dailyAn insulin sensitizer — on its own, essentially never causes hypoglycemia Gliclazide 40mg dailyA sulfonylurea — stable dose for 7 years per his chart

Two medications, stable for years. Nothing here looks dangerous on paper — which is exactly why what happens next is worth paying attention to.

Knowledge Check

Before You See a Single Number

In a patient who manages his own diabetes medications but has early dementia, which drug carries the highest risk of a dangerous, prolonged low blood sugar if a dose gets accidentally doubled?

Metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors essentially never cause hypoglycemia on their own — they don't force insulin release independent of glucose. Sulfonylureas do exactly that, which makes them one of the few oral diabetes drugs genuinely dangerous in overdose. (More on the mechanism and the rest of this drug family in the glucose-lowering drugs lecture.)

How an EM Physician Reads This

"Check a glucose" on any altered patient isn't a formality — it's often the single fastest reversible cause you can catch or exclude in under a minute, before imaging, before most labs. Here it's not subtle: 2.1 mmol/L (38 mg/dL) is well into the range that causes exactly the drowsiness his wife described.

First Response

The Obvious Fix

The ambulance crew give 250mL of IV 10% dextrose en route. By the time he reaches the ED, he's more alert and talking normally — the kind of fast, satisfying response that makes a case feel finished.

It isn't.

The Problem

It Doesn't Stay Fixed

Despite a continuous dextrose infusion (uptitrated from 5% at 125mL/hr to 10% at 100mL/hr) and glucagon on top of it, he keeps dipping back below 3.5 mmol/L, hour after hour, in the resuscitation room.

Checkpoint

Why Isn't This Working?

How an EM Physician Reads This

A single hypoglycemic episode that corrects with dextrose and stays corrected is one story. Recurring hypoglycemia despite an active dextrose infusion is a different story entirely — it means something is still actively pushing insulin out of his pancreas, faster than you can pour sugar in. Turning the dextrose rate up further is treating the symptom, not the driver.

The question stops being "how do we raise his glucose" and becomes "what do we do to make his own insulin stop being the problem."

Checkpoint

Naming the Move

His urine screen confirms gliclazide on board. Recurring hypoglycemia despite an active dextrose infusion means his pancreas is still being forced to secrete insulin. What drug directly suppresses that ongoing insulin secretion — rather than just adding more glucose to outrun it?

Octreotide, a somatostatinA hormone that broadly inhibits secretion across the body — growth hormone, insulin, glucagon, and several GI hormones analog, is now considered first-line adjunct therapy for refractory sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia — it acts directly on the beta cell to shut off insulin release, which dextrose alone cannot do.

Approach & Review

Working Up Recurrent or Refractory Hypoglycemia

A generalizable framework — not specific to sulfonylureas, useful for any patient whose sugar won't stay up.

1
Confirm Whipple's triad.

Symptoms consistent with hypoglycemia, a low measured glucose at the time, and resolution of symptoms once glucose is corrected — all three, not just a low number on a machine that might be wrong.

2
Rule out exogenous causes first.

Insulin, sulfonylureas, alcohol, and certain fluoroquinolone antibiotics are the most common and most directly treatable causes — check the medication list before chasing anything rarer.

3
If exogenous causes are excluded, think endogenous.

Insulinoma, adrenal insufficiency, hypopituitarism, severe liver or kidney failure, sepsis, and non-islet-cell tumors can all drop glucose without any drug on board.

4
Match your monitoring window to the drug, not the symptoms.

Feeling better after one correction doesn't mean the drug's effect is over — some agents keep stimulating insulin release for well past the point a patient looks and feels fine.

5
Have a plan beyond "more dextrose."

If glucose keeps dropping despite adequate replacement, that's your cue to add a drug that suppresses insulin secretion rather than just outrunning it.

Mechanism

Why More Sugar Can Feed the Problem

Two inputs converge on the same channel: the drug forces it shut, and every dose of dextrose hands the beta cell more raw material to keep responding. Octreotide works because it acts downstream of the sulfonylurea entirely — it doesn't matter how much gliclazide is still bound.

Knowledge Check

Beyond This Case

Why is octreotide, rather than just more dextrose, considered definitive treatment for refractory sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia?

Octreotide doesn't displace the drug or clear it faster — it acts on a completely separate receptor pathway to shut off insulin exocytosis from the beta cell. That's exactly why it works regardless of how much sulfonylurea is on board or how long it's expected to keep acting.

What Was Actually Done

Octreotide, and It Holds

50 micrograms of octreotide, given subcutaneously. His glucose stabilizes. With hourly monitoring, there are no further hypoglycemic episodes.

Renal function is normal, his venous pH is 7.41, and his lactate is only mildly elevated at 2.3 mmol/L — nothing here suggests a second process on top of the sulfonylurea effect. This was never a diagnostic mystery. It was a management problem, and it just took the right tool to actually solve it.

Checkpoint

One More Thing to Justify

Take a moment before revealing the answer.

His glucose has been stable for hours after octreotide. Why keep him in hospital for 48 hours rather than discharging him once he looks and feels normal?
Feeling fine is not the same as the drug being gone. Gliclazide can keep stimulating insulin release well past the point symptoms resolve, and a single dose of octreotide wears off over several hours too — so the real risk window is defined by the sulfonylurea's duration of action, not by how he feels right now. Discharging early on the strength of "he seems okay" is exactly how a patient bounces back with another hypoglycemic episode at home, this time with no one watching.

Take-Home

What Almost Happened

The Version of This Case That Goes Badly

If his wife hadn't been home at lunchtime, or if the team had called this "fixed" after the first dextrose bolus brought him back to baseline, the next drop could easily have happened somewhere with nobody watching. There's no dramatic diagnostic twist in this case — the danger was never in figuring out what was wrong. It was in stopping too early, satisfied by a response that was never going to last on its own.

  • Recurrent hypoglycemia despite adequate dextrose means something is still actively driving insulin release — treat the driver, not just the number.
  • Octreotide suppresses insulin secretion directly and is now first-line adjunct therapy for refractory sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia.
  • Match the observation period to the drug's expected duration of action, not to how quickly symptoms resolve.
  • Cognitive impairment plus self-administered medication is its own risk factor, independent of the specific drug — medication reconciliation and family involvement are part of the actual treatment plan.
Knowledge Check

One More Drug Class to Know

Besides sulfonylureas and exogenous insulin, which of the following can also cause drug-induced hypoglycemia through a similar beta-cell K-ATP channel-blocking mechanism?

Certain fluoroquinolones — gatifloxacin most notoriously, levofloxacin less commonly — can block the same pancreatic K-ATP channel sulfonylureas target, triggering unexpected hypoglycemia, occasionally severe enough to need octreotide too. Worth remembering the next time an antibiotic is on the medication list of a patient with unexplained low sugar.
