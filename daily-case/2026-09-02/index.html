The Confusion Nobody Blamed on Sugar

A real ED presentation, worked through one step at a time. Make the calls as you go — a shunt in her head makes this differential a lot bigger than it first looks.

The Patient

22F · Emergency Department

Headache, polydipsia, and progressively worsening confusion. Background: morbid obesity (BMI 56.6), idiopathic intracranial hypertension managed with a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shuntA tube draining excess cerebrospinal fluid from the brain's ventricles to the abdomen, to relieve raised intracranial pressure and an occipital nerve stimulator, and months of supratherapeutic glucocorticoid dosing for a presumed inflammatory condition.

How She Got Here

The Lead-Up

Four threads running in parallel — a shunt, a steroid taper problem waiting to happen, an opioid load, and now a metabolic derangement. Any one of them could explain "confusion" on its own. That's the actual difficulty of this case.

How an EM Physician Reads This

180/120 is a hypertensive emergency-range reading, not a rounding error. In isolation it might just be pain and anxiety. In a patient with a VP shunt and a history of transient confusion, it also has to make you think about intracranial pressure — and a fingerstick glucose of 583 doesn't automatically explain that BP away.

The move here isn't "pick the metabolic story or the neurosurgical story." It's carry both until one is ruled out.

Checkpoint

Before Any Labs Come Back

Take thirty seconds and actually write something down — the exercise only works if you commit to an answer first.

A VP-shunted patient on chronic steroids and high-dose opioids presents with headache and transient confusion. What's on your differential?
Worth having on the list: shunt malfunction / raised intracranial pressure, intracranial hemorrhage, hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) or DKA, opioid toxicity or withdrawal, steroid-induced psychosis, and — easy to forget precisely because she's on steroids — adrenal crisis if a dose was missed. A high glucose on the monitor doesn't let you skip the neurosurgical half of this list.
Knowledge Check

HHS vs. DKA

Which combination is most classically associated with HHS rather than DKA?

HHS trades the dramatic acidosis of DKA for a dramatically higher glucose and osmolality — there's usually just enough residual insulin to suppress ketogenesis, but nowhere near enough to control glucose. That's exactly why HHS patients often look "less sick" on a blood gas while being just as dangerous — arguably more, since HHS actually carries a higher mortality rate than DKA.

Lab Interpretation

First Bloodwork

Glucose 583 mg/dL (32.4 mmol/L) · measured Na⁺ 135 mmol/L · K⁺ 4.6 mmol/L · HbA1cGlycated hemoglobin — reflects average blood glucose over the prior ~3 months 9.9%.

What Actually Jumps Out

An HbA1c of 9.9% with no prior diabetes diagnosis is itself a clue: this glucose didn't spike in the last few hours, it's been climbing for months — tracking almost exactly with how long she's been on high-dose steroids. The measured sodium of 135 looks unremarkable at a glance; it's the corrected value that tells the real story, because severe hyperglycemia pulls water out of cells and dilutes serum sodium, masking real free-water loss.

Approach & Review

Reading Any Suspected Hyperosmolar Panel

The systematic read for any severely hyperglycemic patient, DKA or HHS.

1
Never trust the raw sodium at extreme glucose levels.

Hyperglycemia osmotically pulls water into the vascular space, diluting sodium — the raw number understates true free-water deficit.

2
Correct the sodium before you interpret it.

Classic correction: add 1.6 mmol/L to sodium for every 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 (some now use 2.4 as a more physiologically accurate factor at very high glucose).

3
Calculate (or estimate) osmolality.

2×Na⁺ + glucose/18 + urea/2.8 (SI units). Above ~320 mOsm/kg is the classic HHS threshold, and correlates with mental status change.

4
Check for ketones anyway.

Even in a picture that looks like "pure" HHS, mixed DKA/HHS pictures exist — don't assume they're mutually exclusive.

5
Match the correction rate to the risk.

Whatever the number, don't fix it too fast — rapid osmolality shifts risk cerebral edema, especially in a patient whose brain is already unhappy.

Checkpoint

Do the Correction Yourself

Measured Na⁺ is 135 mmol/L. Glucose is 583 mg/dL. Using the classic factor (+1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100), what's the corrected sodium?

(583 − 100) / 100 × 1.6 = 7.73. 135 + 7.73 = 142.7 mmol/L — solidly within the normal range, despite the raw value sitting at the low end. This is the whole point of correcting it: the "normal-looking" raw sodium was actually hiding a real free-water deficit that the correction reveals.

Lab Interpretation

The Detail That Names the Disease

C-peptideA byproduct made in equal amounts to a person's own (endogenous) insulin — a marker of how much insulin the pancreas itself is still producing 5.77 ng/mL (elevated) · urine ketones ++/+++ · serum ketones absent · mild transaminitis · neutrophilic leukocytosis.

Elevated C-peptide plus severe hyperglycemia, with urine ketones positive but serum ketones absent, points most toward:

A high C-peptide means her pancreas is still pumping out insulin — the problem isn't supply, it's that steroid-driven insulin resistance has overwhelmed it. The urine ketones are a mild, nonspecific finding (can reflect fasting/dehydration); the absent serum ketones are the more reliable negative — together they argue against true ketoacidosis and for HHS.

Knowledge Check

The One People Underestimate

Compared to DKA, mortality in HHS is:

HHS patients tend to be older with more comorbidities, and the profound dehydration and hyperosmolality do real damage on their own, acidosis or not. The absence of dramatic blood gas findings can make HHS feel like the "less scary" cousin of DKA — the mortality data says otherwise.

Working It Up

Ruling Out the Shunt

Non-contrast CT brain: no hemorrhage, no abnormal parenchymal densities, no midline shift, normal ventricles. CT angiography/venography: no acute intracranial pathology, no venous thrombosis. No clinical or radiologic evidence of shunt malfunction — imaging quality was partly limited by artifact from the neuromodulation device.

Why Image At All, Given the Glucose

It's tempting to skip imaging once a glucose of 583 is sitting in front of you — it's such a satisfying explanation. But she has a VP shunt and a headache, and shunt malfunction can be rapidly fatal if missed. The metabolic explanation being likely is not the same as it being confirmed, and the cost of being wrong here is enormous. Image first, then let the metabolic workup do the rest of the explaining.

Approach & Review

Working Up Any Undifferentiated Confusion

The framework this whole case is really testing — useful on every altered patient, not just this one.

1
ABCs and a fingerstick glucose, immediately.

Before anything else — glucose is the fastest reversible cause to catch or exclude.

2
Take a focused history for red flags.

Hardware (shunts, pacemakers), medications (opioids, steroids, anticoagulants), and the tempo of onset (sudden vs. gradual) all narrow things fast.

3
Do a real focused neuro exam.

Focal deficits push you toward structural causes; a symmetric, non-focal exam pushes toward metabolic/toxic/infectious ones — but doesn't rule structural out.

4
Sort causes into buckets, and work more than one.

Structural, metabolic, toxic, infectious, and endocrine causes aren't mutually exclusive — a patient can have hardware and a metabolic derangement at the same time.

5
Don't let the most convenient explanation close the workup.

The most dangerous bucket (structural) is exactly the one it's easiest to talk yourself out of checking once a metabolic number looks bad enough.

Mechanism

Why You Can't Just Stop the Steroids

Two consequences of the same chronic exposure now pull in opposite directions: the steroid is driving her glucose up, but her body has also become dependent on it to avoid adrenal crisisLife-threatening cortisol deficiency — hypotension, shock, and collapse when the body can't mount its own stress response. (See the approach to endocrine disease lecture for the HPA axis this hinges on, and diabetes pathophysiology for insulin resistance.)

Knowledge Check

Beyond This Case

Glucocorticoids raise blood glucose primarily by:

Unlike SGLT2 inhibitors (which lower glucose by blocking renal reabsorption) or sulfonylureas (which force insulin release), steroids push glucose up through the liver and muscle/fat tissue — the pancreas isn't the problem, which is exactly why her C-peptide is high, not low.

What Was Actually Done

Management

Why Slow and Careful

She already had one episode of transient encephalopathy from this metabolic state — correcting it too fast risks triggering cerebral edema, the same danger taught with rapid correction in pediatric DKA. Everything here moves deliberately: fluids and insulin bring the glucose and osmolality down gradually, while the steroid dose comes down on a schedule measured in days, not a single stop.

  • Aggressive isotonic fluid resuscitation, plus IV insulin, titrated to avoid too-rapid correction.
  • Electrolytes monitored and repleted through treatment.
  • Glucocorticoids continued, not stopped — high-dose IV hydrocortisone tapered gradually over a 13-day course.
  • Transient bacteremia identified and treated per culture sensitivities — infection was likely a contributing precipitant.
Checkpoint

Naming the Risk

Her ACTH and morning cortisol both came back suppressed. If her steroids were simply stopped today instead of tapered, what specific endocrine emergency is she now at risk of?
Months of exogenous steroid have told her hypothalamus and pituitary to stop signaling for cortisol, and her adrenal glands have atrophied from disuse. Pull the exogenous source away suddenly and there's no cortisol reserve to fall back on — the result is hypotension, shock, and collapse. It's the same HPA-axis suppression driving her hyperglycemia that now makes the "obvious" fix (stop the drug) dangerous.

Take-Home

Teaching Points

Strip away the specific numbers and this case reduces to habits worth carrying into the next shift.

  • A high glucose is a reason to test the metabolic hypothesis, not a reason to stop testing the others — especially with neurosurgical hardware in the picture.
  • HHS trades DKA's dramatic acidosis for a dramatically higher glucose and osmolality, and is arguably the more dangerous of the two.
  • Correct sodium before you interpret it at extreme glucose levels — the raw number can hide a real free-water deficit.
  • Chronic steroids can cause the very hyperglycemia in front of you and simultaneously make abruptly stopping them dangerous — see the approach to endocrine disease lecture.
