Sim Lab

Get It Into Your Units

Your lectures work in mmol/L; this chart is in mg/dL. Convert first, then correct for albumin — because a raw total calcium means nothing until you know the albumin.

Live converter — calcium mg/dL → mmol/L (× 0.2495):

mg/dL  =  mmol/L

His albumin is 4.1 g/dL, which is 41 g/L. Using the formula from lecture 2.2 — corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 − albumin) — what is his corrected calcium in mmol/L?

15.1 mg/dL × 0.2495 = 3.77 mmol/L. Then 3.77 + 0.02 × (40 − 41) = 3.77 − 0.02 = 3.75 mmol/L.

Note how little the correction moved it — his albumin is normal, so there was nothing to correct for. The correction matters when albumin is abnormal; here it simply confirms the number is real. Either way he is far above the > 3 mmol/L threshold at which lecture 2.2 says admit.

Knowledge Check

What Does a Number This High Tell You?

A corrected calcium of 3.75 mmol/L, by itself, tells you which of the following?

Severity and etiology are separate questions. The height of the calcium drives urgency — above 3 mmol/L he is admitted regardless of cause — but it does not name the diagnosis. Very high values do shift the odds toward malignancy, and primary hyperparathyroidism is the commonest cause of hypercalcemia overall, but neither is established by a number alone. One test settles it, and it isn't the calcium.

Interpretation

PTH 4.2 — Suppressed

Why This Single Value Ends Half the Differential

His parathyroids are behaving correctly. Faced with a calcium of 3.75 mmol/L, a normal gland shuts off — and his has, to 4.2 against a floor of 9.0. That single result eliminates every PTH-mediated cause at once: primary hyperparathyroidism, tertiary hyperparathyroidism, and familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia all require a PTH that is high or inappropriately normal.

Which means the calcium is coming from somewhere the parathyroids don't control — and the branch he has landed in is where malignancy lives.

Follow the fork from lecture 2.2: suppressed PTH → PTH-independent → exclude malignancy first, because it is the most time-critical cause on that list.

Checkpoint

Name the Mechanism

His PTHrP comes back at 83 pg/mL against a reference of 11–20. Name the protein — and, if you can, the syndrome it produces.

PTHrP — parathyroid hormone-related peptide — producing humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy (HHM). A tumour secretes it systemically; it binds the same PTH receptor that PTH does, so it drives bone resorption and renal calcium retention exactly as PTH would. But the assay for PTH doesn't detect it, so measured PTH stays appropriately suppressed by the hypercalcemia it is causing.

PTHrP accounts for the majority of malignancy-associated hypercalcemia. It is the mechanism behind the PTHR being "shared with PTHrP" in Thyroid & Parathyroid Physiology.

Knowledge Check

Beyond This Case

Why is PTH suppressed rather than elevated in humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy, even though PTHrP is acting on the PTH receptor?

Two separate facts have to hold at once. Physiologically the glands are healthy and doing exactly the right thing — sensing a very high calcium through the calcium-sensing receptor and switching off. Analytically the immunoassay is specific for PTH and does not pick up PTHrP. So the hormone driving the hypercalcemia is invisible on the PTH result, and what you see instead is appropriate suppression. That is precisely what makes a suppressed PTH such a useful pointer rather than a confusing one.

Mechanism

The Bone Scan Detail Is the Point

Humoral, Not Structural

It would be intuitive to assume a widely metastatic cancer raises calcium by eating through bone. His scan argues otherwise — no widespread osteoblastic metastases, yet a calcium of 3.75 mmol/L.

That is the distinction between the two malignant mechanisms in lecture 2.2. Local osteolytic hypercalcemia needs tumour physically in bone dissolving it, classically breast cancer and myeloma. Humoral hypercalcemia needs no bone involvement at all — a tumour anywhere secretes PTHrP into the circulation and the skeleton releases calcium on command from a distance. His tumour is in his kidney; his calcium is coming from his whole skeleton at once.

Paraneoplastic hypercalcemia occurs in roughly 17% of renal cell carcinoma and marks aggressive disease.

Sim Lab

Your Move: Sequence the Treatment

Corrected calcium 3.75 mmol/L, creatinine 1.81, clinically dehydrated. Build the acute plan in the order you would actually do it — click to place, click a placed step to remove it.

    Volume first, always. Hypercalcemia causes a nephrogenic-DI-like polyuria, so these patients arrive volume-depleted — and a depleted kidney cannot excrete calcium. Saline both dilutes the calcium and restores the filtration that clears it. His creatinine of 1.81 is largely that dehydration.

    Then the bisphosphonate. Zoledronic acid shuts down osteoclast-mediated resorption, which is the tap PTHrP has turned on — but it takes 2–4 days to work, so it is never the thing that rescues the first few hours.

    Then the actual disease. Everything above is symptomatic. In humoral hypercalcemia the calcium only stays down if the tumour driving it is treated.

    Note what is not in this plan: a loop diuretic. Furosemide appears in the classic teaching sequence but only after full rehydration — given to a dry patient it worsens the depletion and the hypercalcemia. This patient was rehydrated and given zoledronate without it.

    What Was Actually Done

    The Real Sequence

    • Volume: 3 litres of 0.9% saline as boluses, then a continuous infusion at 150 mL/hr for two days.
    • Antiresorptive: zoledronic acid 4 mg IV.
    • Oncological: pembrolizumab 200 mg IV every 3 weeks plus lenvatinib 20 mg orally daily — checkpoint inhibition with a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, the current standard for advanced RCC.
    • Palliative: dexamethasone and radiation to the cerebral lesions.

    Knowledge Check

    Read the Discharge Number

    He leaves hospital with a calcium of 13.5 mg/dL — still well above normal. What does that best reflect?

    Saline and a bisphosphonate act on the consequences — volume and bone resorption. Neither touches the tumour that is still releasing PTHrP into his circulation every hour. A partial fall is exactly what you should predict, and it is why the oncological treatment is the definitive calcium treatment rather than an afterthought. Expecting a normal calcium at discharge here would mean misunderstanding what is driving it.

    Approach & Review

    Working Up Any Hypercalcemia

    The generalisable sequence — reusable on every hypercalcemic patient, not just this one.

    1
    Confirm it is real.

    Repeat the calcium and correct for albumin. Roughly 40% of total calcium is protein-bound, so an abnormal albumin makes the raw number lie in either direction.

    2
    Grade the urgency separately from the cause.

    Corrected calcium above 3 mmol/L, or any neurological or cardiac features, means treat now — before you know why.

    3
    Measure PTH. This is the branch point.

    High or inappropriately normal → PTH-mediated. Suppressed → PTH-independent. Every subsequent test depends on which side you land, which is why ordering the downstream tests first wastes them.

    4
    If PTH-independent, look for malignancy first.

    It is the most time-critical cause. PTHrP for humoral hypercalcemia, imaging for a primary, SPEP and light chains for myeloma, and consider vitamin D excess and granulomatous disease.

    5
    Treat the cause, not only the number.

    Fluids and bisphosphonates buy time. In a humoral picture the calcium is only fixed when the tumour is.

    Take-Home

    What This Case Is Really About

    Clinical Teaching Point

    He had none of the classic renal cell carcinoma triad — no haematuria, no flank pain, no palpable mass. What he had was malaise, a few pounds of weight loss, and one abnormal number on a basic panel. The metabolic derangement was the presentation.

    This is the argument for checking a calcium in vague constitutional illness. It is a cheap, additive test on a panel you were already sending, and it is occasionally the only visible edge of something large.

    • The height of the calcium sets the urgency; PTH sets the diagnosis.
    • A suppressed PTH in hypercalcemia is not confusing — it is the parathyroids behaving correctly, and it points you straight at the PTH-independent causes.
    • Humoral hypercalcemia needs no bone metastases at all. A normal bone scan does not exclude malignant hypercalcemia.
    • Fluids first, then antiresorptive, then the tumour — and expect only a partial fall until the last of those happens.
