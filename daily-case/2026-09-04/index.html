Checkpoint

Before Any Results

Commit to an answer before revealing — the exercise only works if you actually decide.

A thin 45-year-old with 12 hours of severe abdominal pain and vomiting, absent bowel sounds, HR 130 and BP 90/60. What are you working up, and what are you worried about?
Reasonable list: bowel obstruction, perforation, mesenteric ischaemia, gastroenteritis, pancreatitis, and — given the hypotension and tachycardia — sepsis from an intra-abdominal source. Absent bowel sounds plus shock physiology makes a surgical abdomen the front-runner for almost everyone. That is the correct initial read. Hold onto how reasonable it feels; the rest of this case is about what that reasonable read costs.

Approach & Review

When the Labs Don't Match the Story

A generalizable habit, not specific to this case: what to do when results arrive that your working diagnosis cannot explain.

1
Sort each abnormal result into "explained" or "unexplained" by your current diagnosis.

Do this explicitly. Obstruction explains the urea and the leucocytosis. It does not explain a bilirubin of 6, an INR of 6.49, or a glucose of 60.

2
Count the unexplained findings.

One outlier is noise. Three unexplained abnormalities spanning different organ systems is a second diagnosis announcing itself.

3
Ask what single process could produce all of them at once.

Jaundice + coagulopathy + hypoglycaemia together point at the liver failing, or at a systemic state driving the liver to fail. Neither is a bowel problem.

4
Treat the unexplained findings as a reason to re-examine, not just to re-test.

The most valuable next move is usually going back to the patient, not ordering another panel.

Knowledge Check

Reading the Combination

Unexplained jaundice, a markedly raised INR, and persistent hypoglycaemia occurring together most specifically indicate:

Clotting factors are made in the liver, so a soaring INR that isn't from warfarin is a synthetic-function failure. Hepatic glycogen stores and gluconeogenesis defend the fasting glucose, so persistent hypoglycaemia points the same way. Obstruction would raise bilirubin but not the INR to 6.49 or drop the glucose. Each finding alone has a long differential — together they name the organ.

T + 0:30 Working diagnosis

Treated as a Gut Problem

The documented working diagnosis: intestinal pathology — gut infection, obstruction, or perforation. Management was conservative and symptomatic:

RanitidineH2-receptor antagonist — reduces gastric acid secretion Hyoscine butylbromideAntispasmodic — relaxes smooth muscle for colicky abdominal pain Ondansetron5-HT3 antagonist antiemetic 50% dextrose 30 mLGiven for the glucose of 60 mg/dL, then followed by a dextrose-saline infusion
How an EM Physician Reads This

Every one of these orders is defensible. The pain is treated, the vomiting is treated, the low glucose is corrected. Notice what the dextrose does though: it makes the most alarming number on the panel go away without anyone having to explain why a 45-year-old was hypoglycaemic in the first place.

That is how anchoring actually operates in practice. Not as a wrong decision, but as a series of individually correct decisions that quietly close the question.

What Jumps Out

A pH of 6.95 is barely compatible with life. The bicarbonate of 12 and lactate of 4.8 say this is a severe metabolic acidosis, and the PaCO₂ of 46 says something worse: she is not compensating. A patient with this degree of acidosis should be blowing off CO₂ hard, driving the PaCO₂ into the twenties. A "normal-ish" CO₂ here means respiratory failure on top of the acidosis.

And the glucose is 60 again — despite dextrose four hours ago. The finding that was made to go away has come back.

Knowledge Check

Beyond This Case

A patient has a pH of 6.95 with a bicarbonate of 12 mmol/L. Their PaCO₂ is 46 mm Hg. What does that PaCO₂ tell you?

In a metabolic acidosis this severe, expected compensation (Winter's formula: PaCO₂ ≈ 1.5 × HCO₃ + 8 ± 2) predicts a PaCO₂ around 26 mm Hg. Measuring 46 means she is retaining nearly twice the CO₂ she should be — the respiratory muscles are losing. This is one of the most dangerous patterns in emergency medicine precisely because the number sits inside the normal reference range and does not get flagged.

T + 4:15 Airway · the hinge of the case

Found During Intubation

Diltiazem 12.5 mg IV brought the rate from 170 to 130. But her GCSGlasgow Coma Scale — 15 is fully alert; 8 or below generally mandates a definitive airway fell to 9/15 (E2 V2 M4), and she was intubated.

The Detail That Changes Everything

While positioning her neck for intubation, the team noticed a 2 × 3 cm swelling in the front of her neck.

Not found on the triage exam. Not found in four hours of observation. Found because someone finally had their hands on her neck, extending it, at hour four — during a procedure being done for a different reason entirely.

Neck ultrasound: a thyroid nodule, heterogeneous, with increased vascularity on colour Doppler. An urgent thyroid profile was sent.

Checkpoint

Re-read the Whole Case

Every finding so far, with one new fact: there is a thyroid abnormality.

Tachycardia 130 → AF with RVR at 170. Unexplained jaundice. Agitation then falling consciousness. Pedal oedema. Vomiting and abdominal pain. Persistent hypoglycaemia. A vascular thyroid nodule. Name the unifying diagnosis.

Thyroid storm — decompensated thyrotoxicosis. It is a genuine multi-system mimic: it can present as an acute abdomen, as sepsis, as heart failure, as delirium, or as liver failure. In this patient it presented as all of them at once, which is exactly why it took four hours and an unrelated procedure to find.

Note that she scored 0 for temperature: she was afebrile throughout. Waiting for fever would have meant never scoring her at all.

Worth Noticing

A completely suppressed TSH with raised T4 and T3 — primary hyperthyroidism, exactly the pattern in the thyroid function algorithm. But look at how modest the hormone elevations are. T4 of 150.69 against an upper limit of 148 is barely abnormal.

This is the single most important lab lesson in thyroid storm: the severity of the storm does not track the height of the hormone levels. Storm is a clinical diagnosis about decompensation, not a threshold on an assay. A patient can die with a T4 that a lab would flag as only marginally high.

Approach & Review

Treating Thyroid Storm: Why the Order Matters

A generalizable framework. Each step blocks a different point in the pathway, and one ordering error can make the patient worse.

1
Block the adrenergic effects — beta-blocker (propranolol).

Most of what is killing the patient acutely is catecholamine-driven. Propranolol also blunts peripheral T4→T3 conversion. Withhold if hypotensive or in cardiogenic shock.

2
Block new hormone synthesis — a thionamide (PTU or methimazole).

PTU is generally preferred in the acute storm because it also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion: T3 falls roughly 45% in 24 hours with PTU versus 10–15% with methimazole.

3
Block hormone release — iodine, but only ≥ 1 hour AFTER the thionamide.

This is the ordering trap. Iodine is substrate for hormone synthesis. Give it before the synthesis pathway is blocked and you fuel the fire you are trying to put out.

4
Block peripheral conversion — glucocorticoids.

Hydrocortisone or dexamethasone. Also covers the relative adrenal insufficiency that accompanies severe thyrotoxicosis.

5
Treat the precipitant, and support everything else.

Infection, fluids, cooling, electrolytes. The storm rarely arrives without a trigger, and it will not settle while the trigger is untreated.

Knowledge Check

One More Trap

Which of the following is specifically avoided in thyroid storm?

Aspirin displaces thyroid hormone from thyroid-binding globulin, raising the free hormone fraction — the opposite of what you want. It is a genuinely easy error, because the reflex in a tachycardic, agitated, febrile-looking patient is to reach for an antipyretic. Use paracetamol/acetaminophen instead. Cholestyramine is a legitimate adjunct: it interrupts enterohepatic recycling of thyroid hormone.

T + 12:00 Outcome

Torsades, and Then

She developed torsades de pointesA polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with a twisting axis, characteristically precipitated by a long QT and by low magnesium, potassium or calcium. Magnesium sent at that moment returned at 0.7 mmol/L. She was given MgSO₄ 2 g over 5 minutes, then 5 g over 6 hours.

She died approximately 8 hours after ICU admission — about 12 hours after she first walked into the emergency department with abdominal pain.

On Reporting a Death

The authors published this case precisely because it went badly. Thyroid storm carries a 20–30% mortality even when recognised promptly, and this patient arrived already in multi-organ failure — jaundiced, coagulopathic, hypoglycaemic. It is genuinely unclear that an earlier diagnosis would have saved her.

What is clear is narrower and more useful: for four hours, nobody had a reason to touch her neck. That is the part worth carrying forward.
